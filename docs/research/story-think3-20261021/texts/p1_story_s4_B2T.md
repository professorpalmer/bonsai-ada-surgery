## final text

# The Light at the End of Nothing

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, folded into a square the size of a playing card, slipped beneath the door of the keeper's cottage just before dawn. The ink was still damp. The paper smelled of salt and something green, like the crushed leaves of the gorse that grew in tangles along the cliff path.

The envelope had no stamp, no address, no return name. Only two words in a small, slanted hand: *For Elias.*

Elias had been keeper of the Virek Point Lighthouse for thirty-one years. He knew the shape of every gull that nested in the gallery, the way the wick smoked blue in the early morning, the particular groan the tower made when the wind hit the eastern face at forty knots. He did not know the shape of this letter. He held it up to the weak November light and read.

*Dear Mr. Elias. My name is Maren. I am not born. I will be born in the summer of 2039, in a town called Dael, three hours north of here, on a road that runs through the beech trees. I am writing to you because I will always have been writing to you, and the distance between my not-being and your being is simply the length of a sentence, and I have decided to cross it.

Tell me about the light.

Yours in the future,
Maren*

Elias set the letter on the worktable beside the logbook and the cold coffee and the photograph of his wife, Celia, who had died the previous March in a hospital room where the curtains were the color of dishwater. He read it a second time. A third. He checked the date on the letter—today's date, though the postmark was absent. He checked his calendar. 2039. Twenty-four years. He was sixty-three now.

He folded the letter again and put it in the drawer with the other things he kept: the small brass key to the fog signal house, the list of every ship he'd watched pass, the last page of the book Celia had been reading.

The second letter came on the sixteenth. It described the light from the sea, from the perspective of someone who had never been on the sea. *I imagine you stand in the dark and the beam swings, and you think of it as a hand. I imagine you do not. I imagine you think of it as a sentence repeated until the meaning wears thin. I imagine the glass fogs and you wipe it with a sleeve that is not clean. I am right about this part.*

Elias, who had cleaned the lens with a chamois and a tin of compound every forty minutes, felt a strange tightening in his chest. She had been right. He had never told anyone that. He wrote back. He did not know how, because there was no return address. He wrote it anyway, on the back of a logbook page, and slipped it beneath the door. It was there the next morning.

The third letter was longer. She told him about Dael, a town she had not seen. She described the beech trees in the way a child describes a country from a book—vividly, with no certainty. She told him about a mother who sang off-key while making bread. She told him about a summer she had not had, in which a boy named Oren had pushed a red bicycle across a square and fallen. *I don't know if the boy's name is Oren. I don't know if the bicycle was red. I am assembling him from the feeling of what a summer should contain, and the assembly is not finished.*

Elias began to look forward to the letters. He set his watch. He wiped the lens. He stood at the gallery rail and let the beam sweep out into the black, and for the first time in thirty-one years, the routine felt less like a sentence and more like a conversation.

By the twelfth month, the letters had changed. The handwriting grew more confident, though the slant remained. The green smell faded and was replaced by something he could not name—perhaps the particular dryness of a library, or the ozone tang before a storm that never came. She wrote to him now not from the future but from *now*, from some vantage point beside him, as though she stood in the gallery a few paces behind and watched the light turn and said, in her careful hand, *See how the glass fogs. See how you wipe it. I am here. I have always been here. You simply cannot see me because I am the space between the words, and you are too close to read the spaces.*

Elias started to sleep badly. He would stand at the gallery rail at two in the morning and feel, with a certainty that frightened him, that the air behind him was thicker. Warmer. He would turn. There would be nothing. The wind off the Atlantic would hit his face with its old, indifferent salt.

In the spring, the letter arrived on a Thursday and it was different. The paper was softer. The ink was a deeper blue.

*Mr. Elias. I am getting older, or I am getting closer, or these are the same thing. I want you to know that I will not be born. I need you to understand that this is not a warning and not a joke. I am a letter that will be written and then erased, a light that was lit and then the oil ran out. I am the space between the words and it is running out. I have been trying to tell you. Every letter is a draft and the drafts are thinning.*

Elias held the letter until the paper bent. He walked out into the courtyard and stood beneath the gorse. The gorse was white with flowers. He did not know what he was looking for. He was looking for a girl who had not been born. He was looking for a reason the light should keep turning.

The next letter was very short.

*The gorse is blooming. I can smell it. I am almost there. I want you to know that you will not forget the light. That is my only request. Not that you stay. Not that you keep the beam. Just that when you are old and the tower is a ruin and the lens is in a museum, you will remember that for a time, someone wrote to you from the space between, and you read the spaces.*

Then: *I am here. I am in the gallery. I am in the glass. I am in the smoke of the wick. I am the forty-minute interval. I am the fog and the clean chamois. I am the word you say when you think no one is listening. I am the word you say when you say Celia's name to the empty room. I am that word. I have been that word all along. I am the word you say when you love someone who is not there and the love does not go anywhere because there is no place for it to go and so it stays in the room with you and the room becomes larger and the light in it becomes less necessary but you keep it lit because someone told you to, and that someone is me, and that someone is you, and the difference is smaller than you think and the difference is the whole world and the difference is the letter and the letter is the space and the space is—*

The sentence ended mid-word. The ink was smudged at the end, as though the pen had been dropped, or the hand had let go.

Elias stood in the gallery for a long time. The beam swung. The glass fogged. He reached for the chamois. He wiped the lens. He wiped it again.

He did not write back. There was nothing to write. He put the last letter in the drawer with the brass key and the list of ships and the photograph, and he sat at the worktable with the cold coffee and the November light, and he let the tower groan its eastern groan, and he kept the light turning.

Years after that, people would walk the headland and pass the Virek Point Lighthouse, which had been automated by then, its tower dark and its gallery empty. A woman in a museum in Dael, in the summer of 2039, would stand before a brass lens and read a plaque that said: *Virek Point Lighthouse, automated 2047. Keeper Elias Vann, 1989–2047.*

She would touch the glass with her finger. She would not know why the glass was warm.

She would walk out into the beech trees and feel, for one particular moment, that the air behind her was thicker than it should be.
