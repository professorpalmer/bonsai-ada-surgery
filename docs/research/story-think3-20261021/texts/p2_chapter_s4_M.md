# Chapter One: The Track Side

The fog came in off the ridge at six in the morning the way it always did, thick as wool, swallowing the pines one by one until the whole valley of Kettle Hollow looked like it was sitting at the bottom of a glass jar.

Miles Dorsett walked the last three miles of the abandoned spur line the way he had for forty-one years, except now he did it on foot, in his own time, with no timetable to answer to. He'd been a conductor on the Blue Mountain & Northern, the little narrow-gauge line that had run the switch from Kettle Hollow to the interstate at Harrow's Pass. They'd killed the service in 2014. Paperwork. Cost-benefit. A memo in a city three states south that had never once been by the tracks.

But the tracks were still there. Forty years of steel set in ballast in a mountain that froze hard every January and thawed sloppy every March. The ties were gone long ago — someone had hauled them out for firewood, back when the timber was cheap and nobody cared about heritage — so now the rails ran on gravel and the occasional rotting stump of a sleeper. Enough to keep the grass from taking. Enough to keep the line *a line*, the way Miles liked to think.

He walked them now for his legs. For the rhythm. His knees had gone soft, the left hip a constant dull complaint in cold weather, and the walk was the one thing that still made the machinery of his body feel like it belonged to him. His boots on the gravel made a soft, grinding sound. *Klack, klack, klack* of the loose stones underfoot, and sometimes the rails sang a high thin note as the morning temperature dropped and the steel contracted against nothing.

He'd been up since five. Coffee in the thermos he'd carried every shift since the sixties. The little tin whistle hung from his neck, worn smooth on the lip by four decades of blowing. He didn't blow it anymore. Nobody was out on the track to answer. But the habit sat in the muscles of his cheeks the way a prayer sits in the mouth.

The town itself sat a half mile east, hugging the bend in the Kettle River like a dog at the foot of a bed. Thirty-eight houses. Two churches. The hardware store. The diner that was really a kitchen with a counter and a woman named Ruth who knew every debt in the valley and would write it on the back of a check if you forgot. The old depot stood at the east end of town, its red paint peeling in long strips, the platform still there but the tracks beyond it overgrown. The depot had been turned into a "historic rail museum," which meant a few models, a plaque, and a window display of a conductor's uniform that fit no one because the uniform had been the size of a man who was still alive.

Miles passed the creek crossing at the four-mile mark. The water was brown and quick, churning over rocks that had been smooth for a hundred years. He watched the current for a moment the way he'd always watched it on the job — not for pleasure, but because water level and flow changed things. A high water meant the bridge was stressed. A low water meant the cut banks had undercut. He could read the river the way other men read newspapers.

That was the thing about being a conductor on a mountain line. You weren't driving a train. You were *listening* to it. The slope of the grade, the way the track curved, the particular groan of the couplers on the switch that always came on the second pass, the way a particular set of rails rang a half-tone flat when they were cold — all of it went through the soles of your boots and settled somewhere behind the ribs. You learned to hear the mountain the way a doctor hears a lung. And when something was wrong, you knew before you could say why.

It was that knowledge that made the morning strange.

Not the body, exactly. Miles saw the body at the three-mile marker, where the spur dipped beneath a low overhang of shale. He'd seen the legs first, one white boot jutting out from under a fallen branch, then the other. Then the rest of the shape, folded sideways in the gravel. A man, dark hair, a coat that was the color of the river in fall. The face was turned toward the track bed, and Miles didn't get a full look. The head was down.

But the legs.

The legs were wrong.

Miles stopped. He stood on the rails, one foot on each, and he looked at the two legs that were sticking out. The boot on the left leg was a size bigger than the one on the right. And the way the legs had come to rest — not splayed, not crumpled — was *set*. Deliberately placed. Like someone had laid them out.

He knelt. His knee cracked, a dry pop, and he ignored it. He reached out and touched the boot. The leather was cold. Not frost-cold. *Dead* cold. He pulled his hand back.

The coat was soaked at the shoulder, one side, in a way that suggested the rain had come from one direction only, or the body had been turned. He looked up at the shale overhang. The rock face was streaked with a dark, wet line running from the top down to about head-height. Not rain. The streaks on the rock below were uniform, old, from weather. This one was fresh.

He pulled out his phone. No signal out here, of course. He'd known that when he walked. He'd been walking to the three-mile marker because that's how far he walked before turning around. He'd never gone past it. The overhang was new to him, or at least the shape under it.

He stood, slow, and looked the full length of the track both directions. East, the line ran toward town, and the gravel was clear. No boot prints. No drag marks. No scuff of a heel on the ballast. West, the line went up the grade toward the pass, and the gravel there was raked by the wind into little ridges. Also clear.

Whoever this man had been on the track, he hadn't walked onto it.

Miles looked at the body again. The hands were folded across the chest, the fingers interlaced, the thumbs tucked. A position of rest. A position of *being put down*. And the coat — he could see the shape of the shoulders through the wet fabric, and it was *too broad*, too square. A man's coat cut for a man who'd spent his life in a cab, shoulders set from the lever and the wheel.

He took out the tin whistle from his neck. Held it to his mouth. Blowed, long and low, the note a conductor used to call the engine into the station when the platform was empty and the platform was *his*. The sound carried, flat and bright, over the gravel and the water, and went on into the fog.

He listened for the echo. On a line that was still in service, the echo would come back in a way that told you the grade, the curvature, the distance to the next junction. The echo here came back thin and wrong, broken by the shale overhang, and that was fine. That was expected.

But underneath the echo, he heard something else. A faint, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* coming from the west. Not a train. Too light. Too regular. Like the sound of a rail hammering a loose spike, or a man testing a section of track with a wrench, walking it by feel.

It stopped.

Miles stood on the rails for a long minute, the fog thickening, the river churning brown and fast. He looked at the body. He looked up the line. He looked at his hands, which were shaking, not from cold.

He'd been a conductor for forty-one years. He'd seen a body on the track once, in 1987, a young man who'd jumped from the southbound between Kettle Hollow and Harrow's Pass. The train had been moving at fifty. He'd walked the track that day until his knees gave out, and he'd carried the news to the town for three hours, because there was no cell phone then and the nearest house was a mile up the ridge. He remembered the way the town had taken it. The way it had *filed it away*.

This wasn't that.

Miles Dorsett took off his boots and left them in the gravel. He picked up the body's white boot — the bigger one, the one on the left foot — and turned it over. On the insole, in faded blue ink, someone had written a single word, small and neat, like a passenger's name on a ticket stub:

*CONDUCTOR.*

He put the boot back on the foot. He picked up the tin whistle and put it back on his neck. He started walking east, toward the hardware store and the diner and the little red depot with its peeling paint and its empty museum of a life, and he walked with the rhythm of a man who was running out of time, because the tick-tick-tick had started again, very faintly, somewhere up the grade, and he could hear it the way he'd always heard it, behind the ribs, in the bones, where the mountain lived.
