**The Unwritten Light**

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, folded into the mail slot of the lighthouse cottage at 4:37 in the morning, when the fog was still thick enough to swallow the headland whole. Eamon found it between the grocer's bill and the quarterly report to the Coast Authority. A small square of cream paper, the corners softened by handling. A name in the lower right corner, written in a hand so small and uneven it might have been a child's first attempt at letters:

Maren

He opened it.

*Dear Mr. Light,* it began, and then the sentence broke, restarted, broke again.

*Dear Mr. Light. I don't know how to say this. I've been practicing for a while. I'm not really here. I don't know why I'm writing but I think you'll get it and I think that means something. My mother says the sea is big and I think it's the biggest thing and I think you live in the biggest thing and I want to tell you about the thing that's going to happen soon but I don't know the word for it yet. So I'm going to say: the light is going to be different. I think it's going to be a different kind of light. I hope that makes sense. I hope it's true. I hope I'm not making it up. — Maren*

Eamon read it twice. The lamp in the cottage was burning low. Outside, the fog pressed its wet face against the glass and would not let go.

He made tea. He did not make a phone call, because there was no number, no village, no mother with whom to speak. There was only the letter, and the small, careful handwriting, and the impossible morning.

He pinned the letter to the board above the stove, where he pinned all the things that mattered: the tide tables, the service rota, a photograph of his daughter from thirty years ago when she was still small enough to fit in his arms. The new name stood next to hers like a twin.

The second letter came four days later. The same cream paper. The same small hand, though slightly more confident in its loops.

*Dear Mr. Light,*

*I was at the window again. I don't know why I keep doing this. The window faces north and the sea is grey and I think the grey is the color of before. Before something. I don't know what. I know there's a room I'll have one day with a window that faces east and the light will come in at a particular angle and it will be the exact color of a peach skin, and I know this even though I haven't seen that room. I know it the way you know the lamp will turn. I know it because it will. Is that how you know the lamp will turn, Mr. Light? You don't check. You just know. That's how I know about the east window. That's how I know about the dog. A dog with one white ear and the way he'll tilt his head when he hears your boots. I don't know the dog's name yet. Maybe you'll know. — Maren*

Eamon read this one three times. He set down his pencil. He looked at the photograph of his daughter, Clara, who had been a nurse in Dublin and who had died of a fever in a ward during the winter of '94, twenty-two years before this fog, twenty-two years before these letters. He had not been a father for a long time. He had not been a father in any sense that the world would recognize. He had been a man on a rock, winding a clock, keeping a flame alive for ships that might never come.

He opened his own drawer. He found a pen. He wrote on a scrap of paper, though he would never send it.

*Dear Maren,*

*I don't know how to know things the way you do. I know the lamp will turn because I turn it. I know the tide because I read the table. I don't know the east window. But I think I'm glad you do. I think the peach-skin light must be very good. I hope the dog's name is easy to say. — Mr. Light*

He folded it. He put it back in the drawer. The letter from Maren went back on the board.

He stopped drinking the whiskey he kept for the storms. The woman arrived on a Thursday in October, three weeks before the due date that Maren's letters had, with increasing specificity, named.

She came up the path in the rain, wearing a coat that was too large for her shoulders and carrying a bag with no name on it. Eamon opened the door. He stood in the hall, damp from the walk, and looked at her.

"I think I've been writing to you," she said.

It was not a question.

Eamon looked at the board above the stove. Thirty small letters from Maren, pinned in the order they arrived, forming a strange pale constellation. The handwriting of a child who did not yet exist, written by a woman who stood in his doorway.

"I've been writing for months," she continued. "In a kind of... I don't know. I've been in a fog, but not the weather kind. I'd sit at my desk and I'd write, and I wouldn't know why. I'd write to a man on a rock who keeps a light on. I'd write about a garden I haven't seen. I'd write about a dog I haven't met. And I'd seal the letters and I'd put them in the mail slot of your cottage every morning for three months."

Eamon said nothing. The rain came through the gap in the door.

"Why?" he asked.

She looked at him. Her eyes were the color of the grey sea.

"Because I needed to know someone would read them. Because I was going to be alone in that light, you understand. The east-window light. I didn't know if it was real. I didn't know if any of it was real. And if someone on a rock, in a lighthouse, in the middle of nowhere, could sit down and read a letter from a girl who didn't exist yet and pin it to the wall, then it was real. Then it had happened. Then it would."

Eamon looked at the board. Thirty letters. A small, careful constellation of a child not yet drawn.

The tin box was on the desk behind him. A dozen folded, unwritten-scrap letters, all addressed to Maren, all unsent.

He opened the tin. He held out a letter.

"For her," he said. "The day she reads them. I don't know how she'll get them. I don't know if she'll ever know."

The woman took the letter. She held it up to the grey window.

"Maren," she said.

And Eamon understood, then, that the letters had not been sent at all. That they had been written in a single, long, breathless moment, in the space between a woman's body and the world, and that the fog on the rock had been the only place they could land. That Maren was not a person in a body but a person in a light, turning and turning, and that the lamp he had kept for thirty years had been, all along, her lamp.

The woman put her hand on the doorframe.

"I'm going to leave," she said. "In a few weeks she'll come. And I'm going to give her the east window. And the dog, when he's old enough. And I'm going to tell her about you. I don't know how. I don't know if she'll believe you."

Eamon looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded.

The woman went down the path. The fog closed behind her like a hand.

Eamon stood in the doorway for a long time after. Then he went back into the cottage. He took the tin box from the desk. He opened it. He read the letters. He put them back in the box. He sealed it. He wrote a note on the lid in his small, steady hand:

*For Maren. When she is ready.*

He put the tin on the highest shelf, next to the photograph of Clara, who was still small, who was still here, who would not be gone until the very end.

Then he went up to the lamp. The fog was still thick. The light turned. And for the first time in thirty years, Eamon did not wind the clock. He stood at the window and watched the dark water move, and he thought: the light is going to be different. He thought: I hope that makes sense. He thought: I hope it's true.

The light turned. And turned. And did not stop.

"I think I've been writing to you," she said.

It was not a question.

Eamon looked at the board above the stove. Thirty small letters from Maren, pinned in order, forming a pale constellation. The handwriting of a child who did not yet exist, written by a woman who stood in his doorway.

"I've been in a kind of fog, but not the weather kind. I'd sit at my desk and I'd write, and I wouldn't know why. I'd write to a man on a rock who keeps a light on. About a garden I hadn't seen. About a dog I hadn't met. And I'd put the letters in your mail slot every morning for three months."

Eamon said nothing. The rain came through the gap in the door.

"Why?" he asked.

She looked at him. Her eyes were the color of the grey sea.

"Because I needed to know someone would read them. Because I was going to be alone in that light, the east-window light, and I didn't know if any of it was real. And if someone on a rock, in a lighthouse, could sit down and read a letter from a girl who didn't exist yet and pin it to the wall, then it was real. Then it had happened. Then it would."

Eamon looked at the board. Thirty letters. A small careful constellation of a child not yet drawn.

The tin box was on the desk behind him. A dozen folded scraps, all addressed to Maren, all unsent. He opened the tin. He held out a letter.

"For her," he said. "The day she reads them. I don't know how she'll get them. I don't know if she'll ever know."

The woman took the letter. She held it up to the grey window.

"Maren," she said.

And Eamon understood, then, that the letters had not been sent at all. That they had been written in a single long breathless moment, in the space between a woman's body and the world, and that the fog on the rock had been the only place they could land. That Maren was not a person in a body but a person in a light, turning and turning, and that the lamp he had kept for thirty years had been, all along, her lamp.

The woman put her hand on the doorframe.

"I'm going to leave. In a few weeks she'll come. And I'm going to give her the east window. And the dog, when he's old enough. And I'm going to tell her about you. I don't know how. I don't know if she'll believe you."

Eamon looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded.

The woman went down the path. The fog closed behind her like a hand.

Eamon stood in the doorway. Then he went back into the cottage. He took the tin box from the desk. He read the letters. He put them back. He sealed it. He wrote a note on the lid:

*For Maren. When she is ready.*

He put the tin on the highest shelf, next to the photograph of Clara, who was still small, who was still here.

Then he went up to the lamp. The fog was still thick. The light turned. And for the first time in thirty years, Eamon did not wind the clock. He stood at the window and watched the dark water move, and he thought: the light is going to be different. I hope that makes sense. I hope it's true.

The light turned. And turned. And did not stop.

## final text

# The Unwritten Light

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, in the mail slot of the lighthouse cottage at 4:37 in the morning, when the fog was thick enough to swallow the headland whole. Eamon found it between the grocer's bill and the quarterly report to the Coast Authority. A small square of cream paper, corners softened by handling. A name in the lower right corner, in a hand so small and uneven it might have been a child's first attempt at letters:

**Maren**

He opened it.

> *Dear Mr. Light. I don't know how to say this. I've been practicing for a while. I'm not really here. I don't know why I'm writing but I think you'll get it and I think that means something. My mother says the sea is big and I think it's the biggest thing and I think you live in the biggest thing and I want to tell you about the thing that's going to happen soon but I don't know the word for it yet. So I'm going to say: the light is going to be different. I think it's going to be a different kind of light. I hope that makes sense. I hope it's true. I hope I'm not making it up. — Maren*

Eamon read it twice. Outside, the fog pressed its wet face against the glass and would not let go. He made tea. He did not make a phone call, because there was no number, no village, no mother with whom to speak. There was only the letter, the small careful handwriting, the impossible morning.

He pinned it to the board above the stove, where he pinned the things that mattered: the tide tables, the service rota, a photograph of his daughter from thirty years ago when she was still small enough to fit in his arms. The new name stood next to hers like a twin.

The second letter came four days later. The same cream paper. The same hand, slightly more confident in its loops.

> *I was at the window again. The window faces north and the sea is grey and I think the grey is the color of before. Before something. I don't know what. I know there's a room I'll have one day with a window that faces east and the light will come in at a particular angle and it will be the exact color of a peach skin, and I know this even though I haven't seen that room. I know it the way you know the lamp will turn. That's how I know about the dog. A dog with one white ear and the way he'll tilt his head when he hears your boots. I don't know the dog's name yet. Maybe you'll know. — Maren*

Eamon read this one three times. He looked at the photograph of his daughter, Clara, who had been a nurse in Dublin and who had died of a fever in a ward twenty-two years before this fog, twenty-two years before these letters. He had not been a father in any sense the world would recognize. He had been a man on a rock, winding a clock, keeping a flame alive for ships that might never come.

He opened his drawer. He found a pen. He wrote on a scrap of paper, though he would never send it.

> *I don't know how to know things the way you do. I know the lamp will turn because I turn it. I know the tide because I read the table. I don't know the east window. But I think I'm glad you do. I think the peach-skin light must be very good. I hope the dog's name is easy to say. — Mr. Light*

He folded it. He put it back in the drawer.

Over the months, the letters grew. The handwriting straightened, sentences lengthened. Maren described a garden with blackcurrush bushes and a stone bench shaped like a sleeping fox. A grandmother who hummed in a key Eamon could almost hear. The day she would be born, not as something that had happened but as a thing approaching, a tide she was riding.

> *I'm not scared. I think. Or I am and I don't know how to say the size of it. The doctors say I'm doing well. I think of your lamp. I think of how it turns, and turns, and turns, and never once does it stop, even in the storm. I think that's what I'll be. Turning. Never quite stopping. — Maren*

Eamon stopped drinking the whiskey he kept for the storms. He kept a tin box on the desk and each evening wrote a few lines to Maren and folded them and put them in the tin. He never sent a single one. He wasn't sure sending was the right word.

He began to speak to the lamp. Not in words, exactly, but in the way you speak to something you care for and have no one else to talk to. He told it about the dog with the white ear. About the blackcurrush. About the east window, which he now understood to be in a house that did not yet stand, in a year that had not yet begun.

And still the letters came. Every few days, in the fog, on the small square of cream paper, in the hand that was slowly, imperceptibly, becoming the hand of a girl who would grow.

The woman arrived on a Thursday in October, three weeks before the due date that Maren's letters had, with increasing specificity, named. She came up the path in the rain, wearing a coat too large for her shoulders. Eamon opened the door.

"I think I've been writing to you," she said.

It was not a question.

Eamon looked at the board above the stove. Thirty small letters from Maren, pinned in order, forming a pale constellation. The handwriting of a child who did not yet exist, written by a woman who stood in his doorway.

"I've been in a kind of fog, but not the weather kind. I'd sit at my desk and I'd write, and I wouldn't know why. I'd write to a man on a rock who keeps a light on. About a garden I hadn't seen. About a dog I hadn't met. And I'd put the letters in your mail slot every morning for three months."

Eamon said nothing. The rain came through the gap in the door.

"Why?" he asked.

She looked at him. Her eyes were the color of the grey sea.

"Because I needed to know someone would read them. Because I was going to be alone in that light, the east-window light, and I didn't know if any of it was real. And if someone on a rock, in a lighthouse, could sit down and read a letter from a girl who didn't exist yet and pin it to the wall, then it was real. Then it had happened. Then it would."

Eamon looked at the board. Thirty letters. A small careful constellation of a child not yet drawn.

The tin box was on the desk behind him. A dozen folded scraps, all addressed to Maren, all unsent. He opened the tin. He held out a letter.

"For her," he said. "The day she reads them. I don't know how she'll get them. I don't know if she'll ever know."

The woman took the letter. She held it up to the grey window.

"Maren," she said.

And Eamon understood, then, that the letters had not been sent at all. That they had been written in a single long breathless moment, in the space between a woman's body and the world, and that the fog on the rock had been the only place they could land. That Maren was not a person in a body but a person in a light, turning and turning, and that the lamp he had kept for thirty years had been, all along, her lamp.

The woman put her hand on the doorframe.

"I'm going to leave. In a few weeks she'll come. And I'm going to give her the east window. And the dog, when he's old enough. And I'm going to tell her about you. I don't know how. I don't know if she'll believe you."

Eamon looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded.

The woman went down the path. The fog closed behind her like a hand.

Eamon stood in the doorway. Then he went back into the cottage. He took the tin box from the desk. He read the letters. He put them back. He sealed it. He wrote a note on the lid:

> *For Maren. When she is ready.*

He put the tin on the highest shelf, next to the photograph of Clara, who was still small, who was still here.

Then he went up to the lamp. The fog was still thick. The light turned. And for the first time in thirty years, Eamon did not wind the clock. He stood at the window and watched the dark water move, and he thought: *the light is going to be different. I hope that makes sense. I hope it's true.*

The light turned. And turned. And did not stop.
