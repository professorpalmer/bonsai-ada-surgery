## final text

Elias Wren had kept the light at Gull’s Head for twenty years, and he knew its moods better than his own hands knew a cup of tea. Each morning he climbed one hundred and twelve steps, polished the brass, checked the oil, and listened for the small cough of the lamp. Each evening he descended again and let the fog bell tell him where the sea ended and the cliff began. The village below had forgotten that a lighthouse still needed a person; the new station at the harbor mouth was said to be enough. Elias did not care. He had sworn to his late father that the beam would not die while his lungs still drew salt air. On storm nights he sat in the watch room with a tin of biscuits and logbook, and if no ship was in distress, he wrote the weather down as a prayer.

The first letter came in March, folded in a plain envelope with no stamp, no return address, and no signature but the initials E. M. The date inside the handwriting was eleven months in the future. The paper smelled faintly of milk and kerosene, and the words were small, careful, and childlike. Dear Mr. Wren, I am writing from a room I do not yet own. My mother says the sea is louder today. I can see a red hat hanging by the window. Please do not worry. I am not lost. I only want you to know that your light is the first thing I ever called a promise. I have asked you to leave one oil lamp on the porch every Thursday, because it is the only thing in this village that feels older than winter and because your hands will remember it long after I am born.

He read it three times. Outside, the gulls were screaming their thin black arguments over the harbor. He turned the page and found no further words, only a sketch of the lighthouse drawn from the east shore, with a tiny figure standing in the door. The figure wore a blue coat, and beside it was a line: I will be blue when I am older. Elias laughed, but the sound startled him into silence. He had not laughed since his wife was buried in the snow. He looked at the date again and at the initials. E. M. He thought of his daughter, Elise, who had left for the mainland years ago, and of his son, who had died at sea before a child could learn his name. He closed the logbook and sat down, wondering if a lighthouse could be a place where the future writes itself very slowly.

More came each month. They arrived in the letterbox at dawn, or tucked beneath the door by the postman with a look that suggested he did not know what he was carrying. The handwriting never changed, though the paper did. One sheet was blue; another had a small stain like a thumbprint. The letters described ordinary things: a kettle that would not stop whistling, a cat that appeared on the windowsill, a winter coat that was two sizes too large. Then they described the lighthouse. My father's lamp still turns, it said once. I watched it from the cliff and felt my heart make a small knock. Elias wrote nothing in the logbook about the letters, but he began leaving the porch lamp lit every Thursday. The villagers thought it strange, but no one asked, because the sea had taught them all to trust very old habits in winter now.

One frosty night in October, he found a new envelope with a different mark: a small blue bird drawn beside the initials. Inside, the letter began: Dear Mr. Wren, I am four years old now. I have learned to climb the steps. I count to twelve and forget the rest. My mother says you are a keeper, but I do not understand what is kept. The lamp is bright, but it does not look like it is looking. Is it lonely? Please tell me what it sees when it turns. Elias opened his notebook and, for the first time in years, wrote a reply in ink that smelled of salt. He wrote: Dear child, I am old, but the lamp is not lonely. It sees the boats that need it, the fog that hides them, and once, in a long night, a red hat on the cliff in the dark.

The letters stopped for three weeks. Elias nearly convinced himself that madness had climbed the spiral stair and taken his old mind by the hand. Then a letter arrived that said: I am sorry. My mother says you must be tired. I am six now, and I know I am not supposed to write before I am born. We are only pretending, but the pretending is real, because I can see your light. That line stayed with him. He began to ask the postman where the envelopes came from, and the postman only shook his head and said, “I don't know, sir. I carry what the sea sends.” Elias stopped asking. He began instead to leave a small glass of water on the windowsill each evening, and the water was gone by morning, though the lighthouse was empty. He no longer felt foolish; he felt watched by the future, softly.

Elias did not tell Elise. He had learned long ago that some doors open only when the person on the other side is ready to step through. He wrote back in a small hand, telling her about the fog, the oil, the gulls, and the strange blue bird. Once he wrote: I am not sure I understand you, but I will keep the porch lamp lit until you come. The next letter came without an envelope, just a sheet tied with a red thread. It said: I am eight now. I saw a woman in the harbor who looks like you, but younger. She had your chin and your tired eyes. She said you are waiting for me. Elias recognized the woman's coat and the way she stood at the water's edge, as if listening for a voice that had not yet learned its name in the salt air now.

He went to the harbor and looked for her. There was only a woman with a child's red hat, standing where the boats tied up at dusk. She was maybe thirty, and her eyes had the same deep worry as his own. “My daughter is due in April,” she said when he approached. “I don't know her face yet. I only feel that she will know yours.” Elias asked for her name. She told him it would be Mira. Mira, not E. M., but close enough for a child to misspell. He stood there while the tide pulled at the ropes, and for the first time he understood that the letters had not come from the dead. They had come from a girl not yet born. The sea, which had always been a question, suddenly sounded like an answer waiting to be heard by someone who was not there yet.

April came with rain and the smell of cut grass. Elias kept his lamp lit every Thursday, though the village no longer asked him to. He polished the brass until it shone like a small, patient moon. On the morning the baby was born, he was on the cliff, watching the harbor lights blink in and out as if the sea were testing a new language. He had no right to know the child's name, yet he whispered it into the wind: Mira, Mira, Mira. When he returned to the lighthouse, the porch lamp was still burning, and in the sand at the door was a blue bird drawn in chalk, so faint the tide might have erased it. He knelt and tried to keep it dry, but the wind had already softened it into nothing. That was all right. He had seen enough in the long years before then.

That winter, Mira came to the lighthouse with her mother. She was small, serious, and afraid of the height, but when the lamp turned, she did not look away. She held the blue bird from the chalk drawing in her pocket, though Elias never saw it. She touched the brass railing and said, “You kept the porch lamp.” He said, “I did not know I should.” She answered, “I knew you would.” After she left, Elias found a final letter in the letterbox, dated the next December, but the page inside was blank. He read it once, then closed the book. Outside, the light turned, and the sea carried the sound of it far into places no one had yet learned to name. He walked down the steps and did not feel alone; he felt kept, as if the future had learned its first word in his house that winter.
