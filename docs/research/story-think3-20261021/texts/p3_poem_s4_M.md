## final text

The last row of peaches calls my name at dawn,
the same way it did on the first morning,
the same way it did every morning after,
the same way it will not call again.
My forearms still remember the particular ache
of a sack half-full of gold, the weight of it
settling into the small muscles I didn't know
I had, until the week I started here.
I pull the cap off my bucket by the gate,
where the paint has cracked in long, curled flakes
that look like the skins of the plums I'm sorting
in the cold blue hour before the heat arrives.
My supervisor nods from the scale-house window,
a small green flag that means: last of the day.
We've walked these rows until the dirt is soft
beneath my boots, until the branches know
the particular dip of my left wrist as I turn
a fruit to check for rot, for worm, for sun.
The crew gathers where the tractor waits,
we talk in short, sun-bleached sentences—
next year, next year, next year—and a kid
laughs at nothing, or everything, at once.
I wash my hands in the barrel by the shed.
The water runs pink, then red, then brown.
My palms are mapped with the day's fine bruises:
the thumb of the thorn, the line of the stem.
I drive the truck slowly down the long lane
where the orchard thins into the open fields
where the sky has no more purpose than to be
enormous, empty, and the exact right blue.
I'll see the town by four. I'll see the town
where the ice-cream shop still smells like mint
and the parking lot holds the last of the cars
from a season that is almost, fully gone.
And the road stretches out, and I drive it
with clean hands in my lap and the radio low,
a song I almost recognize, a song I'll forget.
The summer will not stop because I leave.
The peaches will not stop ripening. But I will.
I carry nothing home but the smell of stone fruit.
