# The Long Menu

On the *Meridian*, bread had the status of faith. Not the kind they preached on Chapel Deck, but the kind you could hold, break, and pass across a table without being asked. Four hundred and twelve years after leaving the Pacific coast, no one aboard had ever tasted wheat grown under an open sky, and the smell of flour in the galley was the nearest thing to weather any passenger had ever known.

Mara Chen held the cook's station for eleven years, the whole span of her adult life on the ship. She was the fifth of her line to inherit the title; her mother, her grandmother, and her great-grandmother had each spent their last decades at the same pass, each learning from the one before. The *Meridian* had no chefs, no restaurants, no menus with more than one line. There was the galley, and there was Mara.

The ship's records called it a cultural continuity protocol. The crew called it something else. Every month the *Meridian* broadcast a Memory Day: one dish from the pre-launch world, announced from the captain's office with a short history read alongside it. Sourdough on January third. Miso on April ninth. Today, on the four-hundred-and-twelfth year of the voyage, the announcement had arrived at Mara's terminal at 0400, three hours before her first shift. *The final approach to the target system begins. Tonight's Memory Day dish: bread with salt and honey, as it was made on Earth.*

Bread with salt and honey. She had made salt bread every year for eleven years. She had made honey cakes for the harvest festivals. But never together, and never in this way, as though the dish itself were a message. In the recipe vault, the entry for that night's dish was three lines long, the kind of thing the original colonists' cooks had jotted down in a hurry: flour, water, salt, honey, a little fat, heat until golden. No ratios. No temperatures. The vault preserved what Earth had kept. It did not preserve what Earth had only known.

Mara spent the morning in the hydroponics bay, talking to the growers. They were quiet people, good at it. They could tell the difference between wheat stressed for cold and wheat stressed for light, and they could feed her enough for a feast, but they could not tell her what a grandmother's kitchen in 2061 had sounded like.

"The honey," she said. "Do you have anything besides the standard blend? The old records list wild honey in the Pacific Northwest batches."

One grower, a young man named Okafor who had never been outside the bay's ring lights, thought for a moment. "The seed archive has a note," he said. "On one of the Pacific honey jars. My grandmother transcribed it when the digital label failed."

Mara had to ask for the note. It was typed in the old way, on real paper, the letters uneven. *Take the second spoon. The first is for the kids. The second is for the bread. Do not be greedy. It will be gone in a week, but the recipe lives longer than the jar.*

She read it three times in the corridor, standing under the humming lights while a shuttle drifted past the observation windows. *Do not be greedy.* She thought of her own mother, who had once given up her portion of rationed honey to her nephew for a birthday cake, and who never spoke of it afterward, as though the loss had been an error in the books.

By the time she returned to the galley, she had made a decision. She would not make the bread the vault described. She would make the bread the note described.

The dough went in at dusk. She worked by hand for an hour before the ship's standard kneading protocol, just to feel it, the way her grandmother used to work it before the machines came. The recipe vault had a machine. The note did not.

She measured flour to the nearest gram, because precision was the only thing she had ever known to be reliable, but when she reached for the salt, she stopped. The vault called for "a little." Her grandmother had called for "a breath's worth." Mara added what a breath's worth seemed right, and then she added a pinch more, because the vault had no way of knowing that the *Meridian*'s water came from a cycle that made every thing it touched taste a little of metal.

The honey she took from the second jar Okafor had brought up. The first jar, she set aside for the crew's children, because the note said so and because it felt correct in a way she could not fully name.

While the loaves rose, she set the tables on the common deck, the one that faced the starfield. Four hundred and twelve years in motion, and tonight the stars would be different. The *Meridian* had been traveling toward a world it would never land on, and every generation had been told it was a gift, a chance to begin again. The children had been taught the word *Earth* the way a child might learn the word for a country that had never been.

Mara cut the loaves at the appointed hour. The smell rose through the common deck like a tide. She had not seen the first wave of the voyage in all her life, but she could have sworn that when the crust split and the steam came up, something in the deck changed. A door opened. A window cracked.

She carried the first plate to the captain, who stood at the observation window, staring at the starfield. He ate slowly. When he finished, he said, "It's not in the vault."

"I know," Mara said. "That's the point."

By the end of the meal, four hundred people had eaten bread with salt and honey, and the deck was quieter than it had been in years. A few children asked for more, and Mara gave them more. The old woman at the end of the table, whose name she did not know, touched Mara's hand for a moment and said, "You made it the way my mother would have."

Mara had no way to verify that. She did not need to.

The next morning, she sat at her terminal and opened the recipe vault. She added a new entry, in the old style, on real paper, the letters uneven. *Bread with salt and honey. Flour, water, salt, honey, a little fat, heat until golden. Take the second spoon. The first is for the kids. Do not be greedy. It will be gone in a week, but the recipe lives longer than the jar.*

She signed it with her name, then thought better of it and crossed it out. The recipe did not need her. The recipe belonged to everyone.

That night, as the *Meridian* moved forward through the stars, Mara stood at her pass and began to knead the dough for the next day. Outside, the stars turned. Inside, the bread rose, slow and certain, carrying the weight of everything it had been and everything it would become.
