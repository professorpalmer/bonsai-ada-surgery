The city of Kesshul smelled of salt and old smoke, and, beneath both, the faint green sweetness of the algae that climbed the rim of the world's edge.

From the rooftops of the Shanties, where the roofs of the poor were made of shed turtle-scales bound with rope, the city sprawled across the great carapace of the beast. Three hundred acres of stone, timber, and rope spread over the shell, and at the very center of it all rose the Spire of the First Keeper, a needle of black basalt that pierced the clouds above the sky. Below the city, the belly of the giant drifted through an ocean no one had ever seen the bottom of.

Every morning, before the bells began their slow chime, the Tidecallers stood in the Long Plaza with salt in their hands. They were thin old men and women with white hair and salt-crust on their lips. They would pour the salt onto the shell in a long arc, from the front rim to the rear, and speak the old words: *Sleep, sleep, old friend. Keep the deep things at bay.* Then they would step back, and the shell would ripple — not the whole of it, only a small tremor at the edges, like a finger tapping a table — and the sea would heave away from the city's edge.

The old stories said the turtle had been sleeping for ten thousand years. The old stories also said the turtle was dreaming the ocean. Both things were true, or close enough to it. The city had built its houses in the ridges of the shell, following the natural lines where the plates met, and the water that ran down the slopes of the great carapace collected in the cisterns that the engineers had cut into the stone. Kesshul drank from its own body. That was the first thing they taught you as a child: the city was not on the turtle. The city was the turtle, or part of it, the way a nail is part of a body.

Maren Voss learned that lesson the hard way.

She was twelve years old, with ink-stained fingers and a gap between her front teeth, and she worked in the Archives of the Deep Shelf, which was to say that she cataloged the tide charts and repaired the water clocks and told the elders their stories when they asked to have them told. She was not supposed to be in the Shanties that morning. She was supposed to be counting the hours on the third clock and pretending she did not know what the water had been saying.

But the water had been saying something.

The tide charts showed a rise. Not the usual swell, not the heave of the beast's sleep-turn. The water was coming up against the rim in long slow waves, and each wave that touched the edge of the city left a mark in the salt line that the Tidecallers had drawn that morning. The mark was a line of green. Algae, growing in the water itself, rising with the tide, climbing the shell like a vine.

Maren knew what that meant because her mother had been a Tidecaller before her lungs failed. Her mother had told her, on the last night, with the salt line still on the floor of their small room: *When the sea begins to grow, the turtle is waking. Count the days. Tell no one.*

Maren had counted the days. She had counted them on the backs of tide charts, in the margins of the water clock logs, in the blank space at the end of each page where no one looked. She had counted ninety-three days since the first green line. She had not told anyone. She had not told the Head Archivist, who was a dry and thin man named Corvane who smelled of pipe smoke and who had looked at her, that morning, and said, *You have a look about you, Maren. A look of someone who has seen something and has not yet decided to say it.*

Now, standing on the roof of the Shanties, she looked at the edge of the city. The rim was a wall of shell three hundred feet high, and below it, in the dark water that the city's lamps had never lit, the sea was coming up. Not with waves. With a wall. A slow, green, shining wall, and on it, shapes that were not algae. Shapes that were not fish. Shapes that were too large to be fish, and moving, and turning, and rising.

Below her, in the Long Plaza, the Tidecallers were pouring their salt. The salt hit the shell and hissed, and the great carapace trembled — and this time it was not a small tremor. The whole city swayed. The water in the cisterns sloshed. The bells of the Sunward Tower began to chime, and the sound was wrong, low and long and the sound of a bell that has been underwater a long time.

Maren looked at the sky. Above the clouds, she thought, the turtle was waking.

And whatever was in the sea below was waking with it.
