# The Lighthouse of the Unfinished

In the village of Marrow's Point, at the end of a peninsula that most cartographers had stopped drawing a century ago, there stood a lighthouse that had never once been finished.

The tower rose in white stone from the black cliff, its first three floors perfect: smooth walls, a wide spiral stair, a brass bell that still rang in the gale. But above the third floor, the structure simply stopped. There was no lantern room, no glass gallery, no beam. The unfinished top was a flat disc of grey mortar and exposed rebar, like a bite taken from the world, and from that open disc the sea wind always moaned, a low, tuneless note that the villagers called *the argument*.

For a hundred and twelve years, a keeper had stood on that lighthouse.

It is said the first keeper, a man named Aldric Voss, climbed to the cliff in the winter of 1811 with nothing but a rope, a chisel, and a letter from the admiralty that ended with the word *regardless*. He had been told to build a light to save the ships that went down on the rocks past the point — the same rocks, the letter added, that had swallowed the *Queen Anne* and forty-two souls three winters earlier. Voss had been a stonemason of the old school, the kind who believed a wall was a promise made in stone. He built for forty years. When he died, at eighty-nine, with his hands still stained of lime, the tower reached only the third floor. He had left no apprentice and no explanation, only a single leather journal wedged in a crack of the masonry, and a note in his cramped, slanting hand: *The light is not a thing. The light is a direction.*

Since then, keepers had come and gone, each of them building a little and dying a little, and each leaving the journal. The village had grown used to the pattern the way one grows used to weather. Every generation, some young soul from Marrow's Point took the lamp, the broom, and the journal, and climbed the spiral stair in the storm, and the tower gained perhaps a yard before their time ran out.

The village itself was a quiet, salt-bleached place of forty houses and one chandler's shop and a school with two children. Its people were fishermen, boatwrights, and menders of nets. They had a good word for a person who had given up something that was not quite over: *unlit*. *You're unlit*, they'd say to a fisherman who'd sold his boat in a bad year. *You're unlit*, they'd say to a woman who'd stopped singing after her husband drowned. Not broken. Not finished. Just unlit.

And the lighthouse, that great unlit thing on the cliff, was the village's most honest monument.

---

It was in the autumn of 1994, when the last ferry to the mainland had been retired to a boatyard and the village was officially declared a "conservation area" — a phrase that meant the postbox would be painted a cheerful blue and a plaque erected — that a girl named Edda Harrow turned sixteen and went to see the journal.

Edda was the kind of person who listened to the sound a door made when it closed on a draft. She was tall for her age, with red hair she kept braided over one shoulder, and hands that were never quite still, as though some small repair were perpetually in progress. Her mother, Nune, mended nets and hummed off-key. Her father had gone down with the trawler *Marian* the previous February, and no body had ever been brought in. The village had called Nune unlit, gently, in the way they called everyone gently. Edda, who had not yet learned to be gentle with words, had asked her mother why she hadn't said the word back.

Nune had looked at her for a long time. Then she had said: "Because you don't get to unlit a person you love. You can only light them, and you can only do that slowly, and you can only do it by being here, and I am here."

Now Nune was here, and the trawler was sold, and the school had only one child, and the blue plaque was going up next Thursday, and Edda was holding the journal, or rather — the journal had been held for her, wrapped in oilskin, by Mrs. Pell, who ran the chandler's shop and also ran, in the unofficial way, the keeping of Marrow's Point, its records and its moods.

"Your father," Mrs. Pell said, tapping the journal's cover with a calloused thumb, "was going to be the keeper, you know. He'd saved up for a year. Then the *Marian* went, and the saving stopped."

"I didn't know."

"Most people don't know. It's the sort of thing that finishes before it starts."

Edda opened the journal. The pages were soft as cloth, the ink faded to the brown of tea. The first entry, in Aldric Voss's hand, read:

*Day 1. I have set out the foundation stones. I have looked at the sea and I have looked at the stones, and I have begun to understand that the two of them are trying to argue with each other, and that my task is not to end the argument but to make it a good argument. A lighthouse is not a wall between the light and the dark. It is a place where they shake hands.*

Edda read through the whole journal that night, sitting at her mother's kitchen table with the lamp turned low, the salt wind knocking at the windows. The keepers after Voss had written in their own hands — a sailor's scratch, a schoolteacher's careful script, a fisherman's heavy loops — and their entries were short. *Stair done. Wall begun. The storm will take it. It will not take it. My hands will not. The light is not a thing.* Over and over, that line, until the last keeper of the 1940s had written, *I have given it my years and I will give it my rest. If the light is a direction, then I have faced the right way. God keep the next one.*

The next one, Edda thought, is me.

She did not decide to become a keeper. That would have been a decision, and Edda had learned, in the quiet way of the unlit village, that the most important things in a life are not decided. They are noticed, the way you notice that a bird is there in a tree before it has decided to leave.

But she decided to climb the stair.

---

The climb took an hour and a half, because the spiral stair was steep and narrow and worn in the middle where a hundred and twelve keepers' feet had worn it, and because Edda climbed the way one climbs a thing one has a feeling about: slowly, and with attention.

At the third floor, she could see the gap. The unfinished top sat against the sky like a plate of bone, and above it, the open disc, and from it, the moan. She stood on the edge of the flat floor and looked out, and what she saw stopped her.

The rocks.

She had known of them since she was a child — the teeth of the sea, the Black Teeth, the places where the *Queen Anne* and a dozen other ships had gone down. She had seen them from the beach, distant and grey. But from the cliff, from the third floor of the lighthouse, they were right there. And they were not grey.

They were lit.

Not by the sun. The afternoon was going to be an early, cold one, and the sky was the flat grey of a dishpan. But the rocks themselves glowed, a deep amber light rising out of the stone, as if the sea had been boiling them for a century and had finally set them on fire from the inside. The light moved, slowly, in long slow waves, and where it moved, the air above the water shivered like a curtain being drawn.

Edda stood there for a long time, her fingers on the railing, her breath in little white stars.

She understood, without being told, what the argument was.

The lighthouse was not unlit. It was unlit *for a reason*. The light that lived on the cliff was not the light that was built. It was older, and it did not need a tower. The tower was a promise made by a mason who could not yet see, and the moan in the unfinished top was the sound of the light inside the rock, saying, *I do not need your help. I have been saying it for two hundred years.*

And yet.

And yet there was still the argument. Because the light was old, and the old light did not know all the rocks. The new rocks had grown over the centuries, new teeth in the black jaw of the sea, and the old light, in its slow amber way, did not see them. Ships still went down. The *Marian* had gone down. Forty-two souls had gone down in 1788. And the village, which had never lost a lighthouse to its light — because it never had a light — had lost, over two hundred years, every one of its people to the sea in small, quiet, unlit ways: the trawler, the storm, the cold water, the argument between the rocks and the light that had no one to translate them.

So Voss had built. Not a light. A *translator*.

And now the argument, two hundred years old, was waiting.

---

Edda did not go home that evening. She went to Mrs. Pell's shop, and she asked, very calmly, for the chisel, the lime, and the rope, and a pot of coffee.

Mrs. Pell looked at her for a long time.

"You're not going to finish it," she said. It was not a question.

"No," Edda said. "I'm going to finish the argument."

She did not build. That would have been a mistake, and she could feel it, the way you can feel a bad note before you sing it. What she did was smaller, and harder, and it took three months of nights and early mornings, and it required, when she finally told her mother, the whole of Nune's quiet, careful strength.

Edda took a length of glass, a long narrow pane from an old window in the village school, and she set it in the wall of the third floor, facing the rocks, facing the sea. She set it with a care that would have made a mason weep, and she set it at a precise angle, the angle of the rocks as seen from the cliff at every hour of every month, the angle of the light as it moved in its slow amber tide. She worked by night, by the light of her mother's lamp, and her mother, who had not yet learned to speak about the water the way Nune had learned, who had not yet learned to say the word *unlit* or the word *light* without a catch in the throat, brought her coffee, and brought her the glass, and brought her the rope, and brought her, when the cold came in off the sea, a woolen shawl and a long, long silence that was not an argument but something older and kinder.

When the glass was set, Edda climbed to the third floor and stood where the open disc of the unfinished top was, and she looked out at the rocks, and the rocks were still lit, still amber, still moving. But now, on the cliff, there was a new thing. A thin line of light, a sliver of it, moving along the edge of the glass, following the rocks, following the old light, following the new teeth of the sea that the old light did not know.

And the argument changed.

It did not stop. It could not stop. But it became a duet, instead of a moan. Two voices, the old light in the rock and the new line on the glass, moving together in a long, slow, tuneless harmony that was, when you stood on the cliff and listened for a long time, a kind of music. A kind of light.

And in the morning, when the ferry had gone and the plaque had gone up and the village was painted its cheerful blue, the fishermen saw the line of light on the cliff at the edge of the sea, and they saw the new rocks, and they said, *There's a light there now, isn't there? There's a light there now, and it's not built, and it's not unlit.*

And Nune, standing on the beach with her hands in her pockets, looked at the cliff and looked at the sea and looked at the line of light, and for the first time in a year and a half, she hummed.

Not off-key. Not in a way that matched anything. Just a note, low and steady, the note of a thing that had been waiting a long time to be heard.

And Edda, standing on the third floor with the salt wind in her hair and the argument singing behind her, thought: *The light is not a thing. The light is a direction.* And then, for the first time, she added a word of her own, small and quiet, in a journal that would outlive her, and the next keeper, and the next, and the next:

*And I am the direction.*

The tower was never finished. The top remained a flat disc of grey mortar and exposed rebar, and the argument still went on, a low, tuneless, dueting moan that the villagers called, from that winter on, *the lullaby*. And the rocks still glowed amber in the dark, and the line of light still moved along the glass, and the ships — most of them, the new ships, the ones with their steel hulls and their radar and their careful charts — still went down, occasionally, in the old, quiet way, because the sea is not a problem to be solved, and the argument is not a thing that ends, and the light, when it is a direction, goes on.

But the *Marian*'s name was written on a plaque on the cliff, in the blue paint of the conservation area, and Nune hummed, and the village, which had been unlit for a hundred and twenty years, was lit.

Not by a tower. Not by a beam. Not by anything that could be built or fixed or finished.

By the slow, steady, unlit light of a people who had learned, at last, that the argument was not the thing to end.

The argument was the thing to sing.

And on the cliff, the lighthouse of the unfinished stood, unlit, and lit, and singing, and the sea kept moving, and the rocks kept glowing, and the line of light kept its slow, patient round, and the argument went on, and went on, and went on — a duet of two hundred years and one girl with red hair and a chisel in her hand and a mother's lamp on the table below, and a voice in the wind, and a voice on the glass, and a voice, at last, in the throat of a woman who had not hummed for a year and a half, and a voice in a journal, in a hand that would be faded to tea by the next century, that read:

*The light is not a thing. The light is a direction. And I am the direction.*

And the sea, which had been arguing with the cliff for two hundred years, went on.
