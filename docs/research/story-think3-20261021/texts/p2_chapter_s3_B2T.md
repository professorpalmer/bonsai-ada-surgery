## final text

The first thing Arthur Vane noticed about the dead man was that he was wearing black leather train shoes, the kind with rubber soles and a brass cap over the toe, the kind that had once been issued to every conductor on the Grey River Line. The body lay on the gravel shoulder beside the abandoned rail cut, one hand still curled around the strap of a canvas bag, the other open toward the sky like a man expecting the mountain to answer. Above the town, the pines were black against a gray morning, and the air smelled of wet pine, cold iron, and something sweeter underneath, something that would later make Arthur's throat tighten.

Arthur was sixty-eight, retired, and had not walked beside a running train in four years since a bad knee and an even worse heart had convinced him that the mountains could not be outrun by memory. He had worked the Grey River Line for thirty-two years, from the long tunnels at Blackrock Pass to the slow grade into Hollow Creek, and he knew the mountain the way a man knows the weather in his joints. When he saw the shoes, he understood before the constable did that this was no accidental fall.

Constable Dale Mott came up the trail with the local sheriff's deputy, a broad man in his forties who smelled of coffee and tobacco and who had the habit of talking as though every sentence might be overheard by the trees. “Morning, Mr. Vane,” he said, though his eyes were already on the body. “Guess you'll have to come along with us.”

Arthur did not answer. He crouched near the dead man's hand, not touching the body, but reading the ground the way he once read the tracks: for the small signs that a thing had been forced, not allowed. The gravel was disturbed in a shallow fan behind the body, as if the man had fallen forward rather than backward. There was no blood spatter. There was no broken branch, no torn sleeve. Only the bag, and the strange neatness of the shoes, and the fact that the dead man's watch had been stopped at exactly four-fifteen.

Arthur knew what four-fifteen had once meant.

For decades, the Grey River Line had been the pulse of Hollow Creek, a thin steel ribbon that wound through the mountains and out again. The morning express left the depot at six, the afternoon passenger at two, and the evening freight at four-fifteen. The freight train was not meant for passengers, but it was known around town as the last departure because after it came there was no more movement until dawn. Conductors called it the quiet train, and riders hated it, because if a man missed it, he missed the mountain.

The dead man's face was not in the local papers. Arthur would learn later that he had been traveling from the east, a man from another country's habits, and he had come to Hollow Creek only once before. The town had remembered him not as a resident, but as the stranger who asked questions.

Constable Mott straightened and looked at the sheriff, Ruth Calder, who had climbed from the truck with a notebook already in her hand. She was a small woman, but her voice carried the kind of patience that made people talk. “Mr. Vane,” she said. “You seem to be looking for something.”

“I'm looking for what he did first,” Arthur replied.

Ruth nodded once, as if he had answered a question she had not asked. “Mott says you're a retired conductor. Is that true?”

Arthur did not smile, though his mouth wanted to. “Retired is a polite word,” he said. “They don't retire a conductor. They simply stop letting him touch the controls.”

A thin smile touched Ruth's lips, but it did not reach her eyes. “The controls have changed. There are cameras now, radio checks, and men in offices who can tell you where the train is to the second.”

“That's not what I meant.” Arthur kept his gaze on the ground. “I meant a man can be told what to do, but the track still tells the truth. A person can lie with his mouth, with his face, with his whole life. The track doesn't. It only shows you what happened when the weight of things passed over it.”

Mott shifted uncomfortably. “Sir, maybe you should tell the medical examiner what you're seeing.”

“I'm seeing what I see,” Arthur said. “I'm not here to explain it to a man who can't tell a switch stand from a sign.”

Ruth's eyes hardened, but only for a moment. “You may continue walking the line, Mr. Vane. But stay on the public trail.”

Arthur nodded. He had no choice anyway. The mountain had drawn a line in the dirt, and a dead man stood at its edge.

They moved along the gravel path, past the old water tank and the rusted signal tower that had been decommissioned after the last locomotive ran. Arthur walked slowly, his boots clicking on the stones, and he let the town open around him in pieces. Hollow Creek was the kind of place where every house seemed to know the name of every person who passed. It sat in a shallow valley between the two ridges of the Gray Mountains, and the town had grown around the depot as if the depot were its heart. Now the depot had closed for the year, but the old building still stood, its paint peeling in long curls, the lettering above the doors faded to the color of ash.

Arthur had once worked every mile of that line. He had once walked the length of the freight train at four-fifteen, tapping the brake shoes with a hammer and listening for the faint tick that told him a wheel was beginning to go. He had once carried the lantern through the tunnels at Blackrock Pass and watched the black mountains pass by like a dark sea with no shore. He had once met women who left their husbands behind and men who came home after wars, and he had seen the faces they tried not to wear. He had carried secrets the way the rails carried weight: quietly, and with a terrible patience.

Now the town had become smaller, not only in number of people but in the size of its stories. People spoke in lower voices, and the past was treated like something that might wake if looked at too long. Arthur had always liked that. A mountain town knows that everything is connected by distance. If a man disappears, the mountain tells you where he has been by the footprints it has left.

Constable Mott stopped at the edge of the old rail cut and pointed to a patch of earth near the gravel shoulder. “There’s something there,” he said.

Arthur knelt.

A single white flower had grown through the gravel, a small alpine primrose with a petal of green at its center. It was the only living thing Arthur had seen for a long time, and it made him feel as if the mountain had offered a clue rather than a body.

“Not a clue,” he said. “A sign. Someone planted that.”

Mott blinked. “Planted?”

Arthur pointed to the roots. The soil around them had been disturbed, fresh, but not freshly tilled. It had been packed down, perhaps with a heel, perhaps with a boot. “Not a flower,” Arthur said. “A mark. A place where someone stood and wanted to be seen by the next person.”

Ruth crouched beside him. Her notebook was open, but her pen was still. “Seen by whom?”

“By the train,” Arthur said.

Mott laughed. “There hasn’t been a train here since ’21.”

“Not a train,” Arthur said. “A man.”

The sheriff looked at the flower, then at the body, then at Arthur. “You’ve been walking those tracks too long.”

“Long enough to know what the mountain is trying to say,” Arthur replied.

They followed the old rail cut for another half mile, past the abandoned freight yard where the old handcars still rusted under the pines. The mountain grew steeper, and the trail narrowed. Arthur’s knee ached with the cold, but he did not slow down. He had learned long ago that the body’s complaints could be ignored for a short distance if the mind kept working. He listened for the wind in the branches, for the distant sound of the river, for the faintest creak of metal where the old rails still ran under the grass.

At the bend in the cut, he found it.

A single spike, driven into the ballast at a strange angle, its head bent upward. Not part of the track. Not part of the old signal work. It was a marker, crude and deliberate, set in the gravel where no one would look unless they knew exactly where to look.

Arthur touched the spike. The metal was still cold.

Mott frowned. “You think it was placed this morning?”

Arthur shook his head. “No. I think it was placed long ago, and this morning someone came back and touched it. The head is bent, but the dirt around it is fresh. Someone walked here, bent the spike, and left.”

Ruth stared at the spike. “Why?”

Arthur looked up the mountain, where the trail disappeared into the trees. “Because a conductor’s job is not to move people,” he said. “A conductor’s job is to tell them when to stop.”

A long silence followed, broken only by the wind in the pines. Arthur felt the weight of the morning settle over him, not with fear, but with the particular gravity that comes when a man realizes the story has found him. He had thought the mountain was finished with him. He had thought the last departure had been behind him.

But the track had spoken, and it had called his name.

“Who is the man?” Ruth asked.

Arthur did not answer immediately. His eyes were still on the spike, but his mind had already begun to move along the line, back through the years, back to the faces he had once known and then forgotten. He had carried enough secrets for a lifetime, but this one had a different smell. It did not smell of pine or cold iron. It smelled of old paper, of a train that had never been built, and of a death that had been waiting.

“He’s not from here,” Arthur said at last.

Mott nodded. “We checked. He was coming from the east. No name in the local papers. No hotel booking. He just walked in.”

Arthur looked at the body again. “Then he’s not going back.”

Ruth closed her notebook. “Mr. Vane, I’m asking you to be careful. This town has closed its books on the old line. We don’t need a retired conductor opening them again.”

Arthur finally stood, his knees stiff, his breath shallow in the cold air. “Sheriff,” he said, “there’s one thing about a mountain town. It always thinks it’s safe because it’s alone. But a mountain isn’t alone. It’s connected. Every rock, every tree, every dead man is part of the line. And when one part breaks, the whole track feels it.”

Ruth watched him for a long moment, then gave a nod that was not quite agreement, but something close enough. “You’re free to walk the public trail. If you find anything else, you tell me.”

Arthur turned to go, but stopped. “One more thing,” he said. “The flower. If someone planted it, they wanted it to be seen from the old rail cut. That means they wanted the train to see it.”

Ruth’s brow lifted. “There’s no train.”

“No,” Arthur said. “But there are men who still dream of it. And in this town, a dream can be as dangerous as a weapon.”

He walked back toward the depot, his boots clicking on the gravel, and behind him the mountain closed like a door. The morning light was still gray, but the clouds above the pines had begun to break, and the sun touched the tops of the trees in thin lines of gold. Arthur did not look up. He had learned long ago that if you looked at the sun, you forgot where you were going.

He needed to remember.

He needed to find out who had set the spike, who had planted the flower, and who had left the dead man at the last departure. The town had thought the line was finished, but the mountain had a way of keeping its own schedule. And Arthur Vane, retired conductor of the Grey River Line, had not been through a single true stop in forty years.

The next stop had just arrived.
