## final text

# The Last Kitchen of the Aurora

By the time the dough was proofed, the *Aurora* had already turned the Earth more than a thousand times. Every morning in the galley began the same way: the cold metal of the proofing boxes, the long hiss of the reconditioner, and the smell of yeast, which no one alive on board had ever seen rise in a real oven, on a real hearth, in a real kitchen. There was only one galley on the ship, three decks below the deck, and the people who cooked in it were, by their own reckoning, the last descendants of people who had ever cooked anywhere at all.

Hera Marsh had been the second cook of the *Aurora*, and Elowen was the third, though the title had become a kind of joke, because the ship carried four hundred and seventeen cooks and a pantry that could feed a small village on the surface. But only one person, by tradition and by the quiet agreement of the crew, was the *cook*. The others made meals. The cook made home. And home, on the *Aurora*, was a thing that had to be invented again from scratch every single day.

Elo was forty years old, born in the ship, raised in the ship, with no memory of a sky that was not a ceiling and no recollection of rain that did not smell of recycled air. Her grandmother, the first cook, had kept a leather-bound journal of recipes in the galley safe, a small relic from a world no one had ever visited. In it were entries in three languages, with a pencil note on the first page that read: *Make it like your mother would have. The ship will never be that. Make it anyway.*

That was the job. That was the whole job. The ration council, made up of engineers, botanists, and the ship's physician, decided what the *Aurora* could eat. They were careful people. They thought in grams, in caloric density, in protein conversion rates. They had once voted to cut the chocolate reserve to save three hundred kilograms of cocoa beans. They had once voted, during the long quiet years, to reduce the wine harvest to a ceremonial measure, enough for two glasses at the anniversary dinner and no more. They were not cruel. Cruelty was not on their list of considerations. They simply believed that the *Aurora* was a machine that carried people, and that people, given the minimum, would adapt and call it enough.

The garden was the one place the council had not touched. The *Aurora* carried a hydroponic bay the size of a small city, and the *Aurora*'s children, who had never seen a field, had never seen a tree that grew outside a wall, tended it with the seriousness of people who had never seen a garden that was not a garden. There were tomatoes. There were peppers. There were potatoes, which the botanists grew as a backup crop but the crew loved with an almost religious devotion. There was a small plot of wheat, grown because the cook's grandmother had asked for it in her journal, in the last entry she ever wrote: *I need bread. Not protein. Bread.*

Elo found the journal again on a Tuesday in the four hundred and seventeenth year of the voyage. She was looking for a recipe for a sauce, a small thing, a red thing, when she opened the cover and found a second set of pages, yellowed and brittle, tucked in between. They were not part of the journal. They were a letter, written in a cramped hand, dated four hundred and forty-one years ago, in the early years of the voyage, before the children were born and before the ship had settled into its long and slow drift.

The letter was from the first captain. It described a night when the star maps had gone wrong, when the navigation computer had frozen, and when the ship had been, for a single evening, lost. It described the feeling of the engine hum, the smell of the air, the sound of the crew in the corridors, and it described the meal that had been served that night: bread, salt, butter, and a small dish of something green and bitter that the captain had not been able to name. He wrote that it had tasted, for one hour, like a place he would never see again. He wrote that he intended to make sure that no one aboard the *Aurora* would ever be served only sustenance. He wrote that the galley was not a department. It was a promise.

Elo read the letter twice. Then she sat at the table in the galley and cried, not from sadness, but from the particular and terrible recognition of being exactly what someone, very far away, had hoped you would be.

The ration council met on the first day of the month. Elo was not supposed to be there. She was supposed to be in the kitchen, or in the garden, or in the pantry. But she brought the letter, and she brought with it a pot of stew that had been simmering all day, made with the last of the winter potatoes and a handful of tomatoes that were not supposed to be used until the spring rotation. The pot smelled of the garden and of the bread and of the long, slow work of the hands that made it, and the council members, who had been in the room for an hour already, stopped talking and reached for bowls.

They ate. They ate without speaking, which was a thing that rarely happened in the galley. They ate the stew, and then they ate the bread, and then the woman who had been the most difficult to convince, the physician, set down her spoon and looked at Elo and said, very quietly, "What is this?"

"Home," Elo said. "It's not really. But it's what we keep making. That's what we keep making."

The vote was not in favor of the chocolate reduction. It was not in favor of the wine reduction. It was a small vote, and it was a quiet one, and it did not change the ship's course by a single degree. But it changed the garden. The botanists, who had been planning to convert the wheat plot to a third algae farm, paused, and then, after a long and careful review, decided to keep the wheat. They kept it because it was cheap to maintain, and because it was useful, and because, in the final paragraph of their report, which was written in language so formal it might as well have been in Latin, they noted that the crop's "psychological yield" was difficult to quantify and therefore should not be discounted.

It was, in the end, a small victory. The *Aurora* still carried four hundred and seventeen cooks, and the ration council still met on the first of the month, and the chocolate reserve was still three hundred kilograms short of what it had been. But the wheat grew. The bread rose. And in the galley, three decks below the deck, the journal stayed open on the table, its pages soft from handling, and the pencil note on the first page was still there, still saying what it had said four hundred and seventy years before: *Make it like your mother would have. The ship will never be that. Make it anyway.*

Elo made the bread that evening. She made it the way her grandmother had made it, and the way her mother had made it, and the way she had made it before she was born, in a world that had not been a ship, and in a kitchen that had not been a galley, and in a home that was not a ship's hull, and in a home that was, by every measure, a kitchen with a door, and a table, and a light, and a person at the other end of the table, waiting.

She put the loaf in the oven. The reconditioner hissed. The *Aurora* turned, as it had always turned, and the stars outside the observation ports were not a map of somewhere else. They were just stars, and they were just there, and the bread was in the oven, and the smell of yeast filled the galley, and for one hour, the ship was not a machine that carried people, and the people were not a cargo, and the bread was not a ration. It was home. It was, as close to home as the ship had ever been, and that was enough. It had always been enough.

Elo took the loaf from the oven. She wrapped it in cloth. She carried it to the table where the journal lay open, and she cut a slice, and she ate it, and it tasted of the garden and of the bread and of the long, slow work of the hands that made it, and it tasted, for one hour, of a place she would never see again.

She made it anyway.
