## final text

The last row of peaches calls my name again,
though I know I'll not be picking them by light.
My hands are still a map of where I've been—
the nectar pressed beneath each calloused joint.

The truck's engine coughs twice and gives up,
and I stand there with a crate still half-full,
a bruise on my thumb shaped like a moon,
the radio humming something old and dull.

Forty-three days of sun behind my eyes.
Forty-three mornings of the same cold gate,
the same spray of dew that stings the skin,
the same long walk where the rows go straight.

I think of all I left in the dirt—
a glove, a cigarette, a song I hummed
while the crows watched from the topmost branch,
indifferent, as they are with everything.

Maria waves from the truck by the scale.
"Same time next year?" she says, not asking.
I nod. My throat does something strange,
and the weight of the crate feels like a blessing.

I turn the crate over in the dirt
so every last peach rolls out and stays,
a small red army scattered in the grass
to rot, to feed the worms, to find their way
back to the roots that fed me all this summer.

The sky is going violet at the edge,
and I can smell the irrigation pipes
breathing out their cool, mineral breath.

I will not come back. I know I will.
But tonight, I drive with both windows down
and let the warm dark fill my whole car,
and let the orchard hold my name
in its quiet, rotting, generous dark.
