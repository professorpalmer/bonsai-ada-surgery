# Chapter One: The City on the Shell

The first thing Mara knew each morning was the sound of the sea breathing against her window.

Not the crash of waves, not the hiss of foam, but a low, slow exhale that rose from the hull of the world and filled the stone corridors of the underbelly district where she lived. The Tide City breathed in at dawn and out at dusk, and her bedrock was the turtle's spine.

She pressed her palm against the cold stone wall and counted the groan. One. Two. Three. The shell expanded, a sound like a cathedral settling after a long wind, and the cobblestones beneath her feet shifted half an inch to the left. She had grown up learning to walk that half-inch. Every child in the underbelly learned it before they learned their own names.

Mara was twenty-two and the youngest of the Spine-Walkers, the three women whose job it was to climb to the highest point of the shell every night and check the Great Scar. No one spoke of what the Scar had been, what had carved that long, jagged gash across the turtle's dorsal plate a hundred and forty years ago. The elders said it was a curse. The scholars at the Lantern Spire said it was a wound from a war fought in a sky no one had seen since. Mara only knew that the Scar wept at high tide, and the weeping salt had eaten half the northern bridges.

Tonight, she would not climb. Tonight, the Spine-Walkers were grounded by a gale that rolled across the shell with a sound like ten thousand doors slamming shut at once.

Instead, Mara stood in the narrow alley between the chandlers' row and the bone-carvers' shop, listening.

The gale was new. It came from the east, where the shell dipped toward the open ocean and the sky met the sea in a smear of white and grey. She could smell it now — ozone and something older, something green and rot-sweet that reminded her of the deep trenches below the underbelly, where the thermal vents fed the city's furnaces and the black water lapped at the foundations.

"You're going to walk into the maw again, aren't you?"

Mara turned. Old Petra sat on her step with a bowl of broth balanced on her knee, her face a topography of wrinkles that she had refused to smooth for forty years. "I'm going to watch the sky, Petra. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Is there?" Mara repeated, and then she was walking toward the eastern edge of the underbelly, where the alley opened into the Shell Court.

The Shell Court was a broad, shallow depression in the middle of the turtle's back, paved in pale stone that had been polished smooth by centuries of bare feet. In the center stood the Loom, the great bronze mechanism that regulated the city's tides by drawing and releasing the saltwater that pooled in the grooves of the shell. The Loom was silent now, its gears locked by the gale warning. But even locked, it hummed with a faint vibration that Mara felt in her teeth.

She stood at the rim of the court and looked out.

The sea was not calm. It had never been calm, not since the first sailors had lashed their ropes to the turtle's shell and declared themselves a city. But it was wrong. The waves were too slow, too deliberate, as if the water itself was thinking. And at the far edge of the horizon, where the turtle's head was submerged in the deep, something was moving. Something vast. The sea parting and closing, parting and closing, with a rhythm that was not the tide.

"Mara."

She spun. A boy stood in the alley behind her, barely twelve, with a chandler's oil-stained tunic and eyes the color of the thermal vents below — a pale, molten orange. He was holding a length of rope and a coil of lantern wire.

"I saw it too," he said. "From the rope walk. It's not the current. It's *something* out there."

Mara looked back at the sea. The parting had stopped. The water was still now, a perfect mirror of the storm clouds above. And in that mirror, she saw it: the shape, vast and indistinct, but *there*. A head. A jaw the size of the Shell Court. Something rising from the deep, something that had been under the turtle for longer than the city had been on it.

The gale died.

It didn't fade or soften. It simply stopped, as if the air had been cut from its roots. The silence that followed was so complete that Mara could hear the blood moving in her ears.

And then the turtle turned its head.

It was a motion so vast and slow that the whole city groaned. The cobblestones beneath the underbelly shifted three full inches, not half. The Loom shuddered and its gears, long locked, began to turn of their own accord with a sound like the world cracking open.

Mara grabbed the rim of the Shell Court as the stone tilted beneath her feet, the city tilting, the whole great back rising and dipping and *turning*, and she felt — for the first time in her life — not the breath of the sea, but the breath of something else. Something beneath.

Something awake.
