**Salt of the Old Sea**

On the ship Meridian, nothing grew in the open sky. Every leaf, every sprout, every worm that writhed through the dark of a compost tray had been born inside the hull of a vessel that had been falling through the dark for two hundred and six years. The first cook had come aboard with a recipe box bound in leather. It held four pages: chicken soup, bread, salted cod, and a birthday cake she never finished writing.

By the time Mara inherited the galley, the box had been copied and recopied until the leather cracked like dry riverbed mud.

Mara was forty-one, broad-shouldered, and the only person aboard the Meridian who could taste something and say what it was. The ship's biologists could read the spectral signature of a culture. The chemists could tell you the amino acid ratio of a vat-broth to the third decimal place. But the cook tasted it the way her grandmother's grandmother had, by tongue and memory, and called it right.

"Too thin," she would tell the vats. "You're not making stock. You're making gray water with ambition."

This was considered an insult, and she had long ago learned to smile when people said "you sound like my grandmother," which was a compliment that had lost all meaning two generations ago.

There were four thousand seven hundred and sixty-three people on the Meridian. The number was always the same, because births matched deaths with a patience that made the statisticians weep in their offices. The galley fed everyone three times a day, plus snacks, plus the weekly feast day, which everyone called "Friday" and no one could tell you why.

The food was not bad. That was the first thing people who left Earth and had never tasted it got wrong. They were so desperate for the old Earth that they'd call a meal of nutrient paste and algae a "degradation," when what it was was adaptation. The Meridian's kitchens grew the greens in hydroponic towers that smelled of wet stone and copper. The protein came from insect farms in the lower decks, where the larvae were fed on the compost of old trays and grew fat and obedient. The flour was ground from a wheat that had been bred in the dark for nine generations to be palatable in the mouth even without an outside sun.

It was food. It fed people. It even, on some days, was good.

But good is not the same as home, and home was a country Mara had never seen.

Every evening, after the third meal, Mara would walk the observation ring alone. The Meridian's windows were not windows. They were thick panels of ceramic and glass, and beyond them there was nothing to see but stars. The stars had not changed in two centuries. She knew this the way a doctor knows the shape of a heartbeat from the monitor's beeping. Same stars. Same black. Same slow crawl of the ship through a silence so complete it had a texture.

On the wall of the observation ring hung a plate. Not a dish, a plate. A real one, glazed blue on one side and white on the other, with a chip in the rim where someone had dropped it a long time ago. It had come off the old Earth in a cargo container, one of the first things they'd carried into the hull, and it had been hung here ever since, facing the stars.

Mara would stand in front of it and say, "Good evening, plate," and that was the end of her day.

The next morning, she would open the galley, start the vats, and begin to make the day's meal. She had a routine as old as the ship itself. First, she checked the algae. She would open the tank, lift a handful of the green, and hold it up to the light. A good algae was dark green with a faint blue sheen. A bad algae was the color of dishwater, and a bad algae meant a leak in the nutrient line and three days of tasting something sour on the back of your tongue.

Second, she checked the insect trays. The larvae were always there, always moving, always fat. She would run her hand over the surface and feel for the rhythm of them, the way a doctor feels for a pulse. If the rhythm was wrong, she would write a note to the maintenance crew and leave it on their door, in the same handwriting her mother had used to write grocery lists in the old Earth days.

Third, she made the broth.

The broth was the meal, not the meal's accompaniment. The broth was the thing that made a person look up from their tray and feel, for just a second, that they were not in a metal room, that the steam rising from their bowl had once been a river, had once been the morning mist in a garden they had never seen.

The recipe for the broth was four pages long, which was more than the original recipe box held. Over the generations, the cooks had added things. A pinch of what the chemists called "fermented yeast extract," which tasted, to Mara, like something alive. A spoonful of the mineral water from the deep tanks, which had a saltiness that reminded her of her grandmother's tears, though she did not say that aloud. A leaf of the garden mint, which the botanists had bred to be sharper than the old Earth mint, because a sharper mint held up better in the vats.

The original recipe box had four pages. The broth recipe had four pages. And yet the broth was not the soup that the first cook had made in the first year aboard the Meridian, when she had been young and the ship had just left the black of the Earth, and the whole crew had gathered in the galley and wept, because they had made chicken soup and it had tasted, for just a second, like home.

Mara did not make chicken soup anymore. There was no chicken. There had never been chicken aboard the Meridian. But she made broth, and the broth was close enough, and close enough was the only thing any of them had.

On the last day of the month, Mara made a meal that was not in the recipe book. She made it for the whole galley crew, and only them. She used the good flour, the wheat that had been bred in the dark. She used the insect protein, but she ground it fine and made a paste, and mixed it with the algae and a little of the mineral water, and she baked it into a loaf. She did not salt it. She did not flavor it. She just made it into the shape of a loaf, and put it in the oven, and waited.

The bread came out golden, though the light in the galley was not sunlight. It was the pale, even glow of the grow-lights, and the bread was golden in the same way a river stone is golden in the light of a streetlamp. The smell filled the galley and the crew stopped what they were doing and just stood there, breathing in.

Mara cut the loaf into thick slices and set them out on the plate from the old Earth. Not the whole plate. Just the center of the plate, where the chip was, and the bread sat there like a small, golden thing that had no business existing.

"Whoa," said Tomas, the youngest cook. He had been born aboard the Meridian. He had never been anywhere but the hull. He picked up a slice, held it up to the light, and said, "It smells like the garden. The one that's closed. The one that's not open anymore."

"It smells like bread," Mara said.

"It smells like the garden."

Mara looked at the plate. She looked at the chip in the rim. She looked at the stars through the observation panel, the same stars they had been for two hundred and six years.

"It smells like home," she said. "And home is just the next meal."

Tomas ate the slice. He chewed it slowly, and when he finished, he set it down and said, "Next month, I want to make a cake. The one from the recipe box. The one that's never finished."

Mara smiled. She went to the recipe box, opened it, and read the last page, which said, in handwriting that had been copied and recopied until the letters were barely legible: "Birthday cake, for the ones who stay. For the ones who don't leave. For the ones who make the next meal."

She closed the box. She set the plate back on the wall, facing the stars. And in the galley, the vats hummed, and the algae grew, and the bread cooled on the table, and the Meridian fell through the dark, carrying four thousand seven hundred and sixty-three people, and a plate, and a recipe for a cake no one had ever finished, toward the next meal.

# Salt of the Old Sea

On the Meridian, nothing grew under an open sky. Every leaf, every sprout, every worm writhing in a compost tray was born inside the hull of a vessel that had been falling through the dark for two hundred and six years. The first cook came aboard with a recipe box bound in leather. It held four pages: chicken soup, bread, salted cod, and a birthday cake she never finished writing.

By the time Mara took over the galley, the box had been copied and recopied until the leather cracked like dry river mud.

Mara was forty-one, broad-shouldered, and the only person on the Meridian who could taste something and say what it was. The ship's biologists could read the spectral signature of a culture. The chemists could tell you the amino acid ratio of a vat-broth to the third decimal. But the cook tasted it the way her grandmother's grandmother had, by tongue and memory, and called it right.

"Too thin," she would say to the vats. "You're not making stock. You're making gray water with ambition."

This was considered an insult, and she had long ago learned to smile when people said, "You sound like my grandmother," which was a compliment that had lost all meaning two generations ago.

There were four thousand seven hundred and sixty-three people on the Meridian. The number was always the same, because births matched deaths with a patience that made the statisticians weep in their offices. The galley fed everyone three times a day, plus snacks, plus the weekly feast day, which everyone called "Friday" and no one could tell you why.

The food was not bad. That was the first mistake people who left Earth and never tasted it made. They were so desperate for the old Earth that they'd call a meal of nutrient paste and algae a "degradation," when what it was was adaptation. The Meridian's kitchens grew the greens in hydroponic towers that smelled of wet stone and copper. The protein came from insect farms in the lower decks, where the larvae were fed on the compost of old trays and grew fat and obedient. The flour was ground from wheat bred in the dark for nine generations to be palatable on the tongue even without an outside sun.

It was food. It fed people. And some days it was even good.

But good is not the same as home, and home was a country Mara had never seen.

Every evening, after the third meal, Mara would walk the observation ring alone. The Meridian's windows were not windows. They were thick panels of ceramic and glass, and beyond them there was nothing to see but stars. The stars had not changed in two centuries. She knew this the way a doctor knows the shape of a heartbeat from the beeping of a monitor. Same stars. Same black. Same slow crawl of the ship through a silence so complete it had a texture.

On the wall of the observation ring hung a plate. Not a dish, a plate. A real one, glazed blue on one side and white on the other, with a chip in the rim where someone had dropped it a long time ago. It had come to the Meridian in a cargo container, one of the first things carried into the hull, and it had been hung there ever since, facing the stars.

Mara would stand in front of it and say, "Good evening, plate," and that was the end of her day.

The next morning, she would open the galley, start the vats, and begin making the day's meal. She had a routine as old as the ship itself. First, she would check the algae. She would open the tank, lift a handful of the green, and hold it up to the light. A good algae was dark green with a faint blue sheen. A bad algae was the color of dishwater, and a bad algae meant a leak in the nutrient line and three days of tasting something sour on the back of your tongue.

Second, she would check the insect trays. The larvae were always there, always moving, always fat. She would run her hand over the surface and feel for their rhythm, the way a doctor feels for a pulse. If the rhythm was wrong, she would write a note to the maintenance crew and leave it on their door in the same handwriting her mother had used to write grocery lists on the old Earth.

Third, she made the broth.

The broth was the meal, not a side of it. The broth was the thing that made a person look up from their tray and feel, for just a moment, that they were not in a metal room, that the steam rising from their bowl had once been a river, had once been the morning mist in a garden they had never seen.

The recipe for the broth was four pages long, which was more than the original recipe box held. Over the generations, the cooks had added things. A pinch of what the chemists called "fermented yeast extract," which tasted, to Mara, like something alive. A spoonful of the mineral water from the deep tanks, which had a saltiness that reminded her of her grandmother's tears, though she did not say that aloud. A leaf of the garden mint, which the botanists had bred to be sharper than the old Earth mint, because a sharper mint held up better in the vats.

The original recipe box had four pages. The broth recipe had four pages. And yet the broth was not the soup the first cook had made in the first year aboard the Meridian, when she had been young and the ship had just left the black of the Earth, and the whole crew had gathered in the galley and wept, because they had made chicken soup and it had tasted, for just a moment, like home.

Mara did not make chicken soup anymore. There was no chicken. There had never been chicken aboard the Meridian. But she made broth, and the broth was close enough, and close enough was the only thing any of them had.

On the last day of the month, Mara made a meal that was not in the recipe book. She made it for the whole galley crew, and only them. She used the good flour, the wheat that had been bred in the dark. She used the insect protein, but she ground it fine and made a paste, and mixed it with the algae and a little of the mineral water, and baked it into a loaf. She did not salt it. She did not flavor it. She just made it into the shape of a loaf, and put it in the oven, and waited.

The bread came out golden, though the light in the galley was not sunlight. It was the pale, even glow of the grow-lights, and the bread was golden in the same way a river stone is golden in the light of a streetlamp. The smell filled the galley and the crew stopped what they were doing and just stood there, breathing in.

Mara cut the loaf into thick slices and set them out on the plate from the old Earth. Not the whole plate. Just the center of the plate, where the chip was, and the bread sat there like a small, golden thing with no business existing.

"Whoa," said Tomas, the youngest cook. He had been born aboard the Meridian. He had never been anywhere but the hull. He picked up a slice, held it up to the light, and said, "It smells like the garden. The one that's closed. The one that's not open anymore."

"It smells like bread," Mara said.

"It smells like the garden."

Mara looked at the plate. She looked at the chip in the rim. She looked at the stars through the observation panel, the same stars they had been for two hundred and six years.

"It smells like home," she said. "And home is just the next meal."

Tomas ate the slice. He chewed it slowly, and when he was done, he set it down and said, "Next month, I want to make a cake. The one from the recipe box. The one that's never finished."

Mara smiled. She went to the recipe box, opened it, and read the last page, which said, in handwriting that had been copied and recopied until the letters were barely legible: *Birthday cake, for the ones who stay. For the ones who don't leave. For the ones who make the next meal.*

She closed the box. She set the plate back on the wall, facing the stars. And in the galley, the vats hummed, and the algae grew, and the bread cooled on the table, and the Meridian fell through the dark, carrying four thousand seven hundred and sixty-three people, and a plate, and a recipe for a cake no one had ever finished, toward the next meal.
