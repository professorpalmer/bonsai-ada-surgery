Chapter One
The Last Whistle

Arthur Calloway woke at 4:30 because the mountain had begun to cough. It was not a word for wind, or fog, or the groan of frost in the granite, but a sound he had known since he was a boy with his ear pressed to the rail outside the old Pinehaven depot. The town lay below him in a valley stitched by the river and the abandoned railway line that curled up the mountain like a scar.

He stood at the window, a cup of coffee cooling in his hands, and watched the first grey light lift from the pines. For forty-two years he had read the rails the way other men read the weather. He knew which ties heaved in spring, which curves swallowed a whistle, which section of the cut had a loose stone that would sing under a slow train. He had retired two winters ago, when the mountain line was closed for the last time and the company sold its cars to a logging outfit in the valley. Now he lived above the old depot, where the smell of diesel and wet oak still clung to the beams.

The coffee was good. The mountain was quiet. And that was the problem.

He dressed in his usual clothes, the same navy trousers and cardigan he had worn for three decades, and put on his boots. On the way down to the kitchen he paused at the small shelf where he kept his silver watch, his old conductor’s whistle, and a brass station token from the day he had retired. The token was tarnished at one edge. He used to click it between his thumb and forefinger when he was trying not to smile.

Outside, the air tasted of ice. He took a steel pole from the porch, not for work, but because the old spur to the trestle ran across the back of his property, and the leaves had come down thick over the rails again. If he could see the track, he could tell whether the river had swollen or a tree had fallen. It was a habit, not a need.

The spur had not seen a passenger train in ten years, but the company still mowed the grass at the junction in August. The weeds had grown taller this year, perhaps because the new landowner in town had stopped paying for the crew. The rails gleamed faintly in the early light, like bones pulled from snow.

Arthur walked the line slowly, tapping the ties where he passed. One, two, three. His rhythm was automatic. He had once walked this exact stretch a thousand times, always with his cap in his hand and his eyes on the point of the track ahead. Now his knees complained when the ground tilted, but his memory did not.

At the trestle, the old wooden structure rose over a narrow gorge, its planks black with age and birds. Below, the river moved dark and quick. The body was there before he saw it, or rather before he recognized it as a body.

It sat against the abutment stone, legs stretched out across the lower rails, as if the woman had been waiting for a train that would never come. Her coat was the color of dried blood, and her hair, pale as birch bark, hung over her face. One white glove hung open from her hand. The other lay a foot away, as if she had dropped it in surprise.

Arthur stopped.

The silence that followed was not silence at all. It was the sound of the river, the faint tick of cooling iron, and the blood moving in his own ears. He had been a conductor, not a detective, but he had seen enough. A body found on an abandoned track in a small town was not an accident waiting to happen. It was a statement.

He crouched, keeping his weight low, and looked at the rails. The woman’s shoes were on her feet, but the toes were pointing away from the direction the train would have come. If someone had placed her there, they had made a small mistake. They had forgotten that a train from the valley would have run from left to right, and the driver’s foot would have pressed the brake on the near side. The body had been laid with the wrong shoulder to the track.

She had not fallen off a train.

Arthur straightened, and his pulse gave a hard knock against the inside of his ribs. He looked toward the valley. A dog was barking somewhere down the road. Somewhere else, a screen door slammed.

The town was waking up.

The first sound of wheels was not a train. It was the Sheriff’s truck, its red and blue lights blinking against the grey light at the entrance to the gorge. The truck skidded onto the dirt road below the spur, and a moment later Sheriff Dana Whitfield was climbing the embankment with the brisk, careful steps of a woman who had spent twenty years in a town that still tried to remember who was who.

She saw him before he saw her.

"Arthur?" she called. "You’re early."

He did not answer at once. "You’ve seen it."

She stopped a few feet away, her hand resting on her shoulder holster without taking her hand out. Her face had the flat, polite expression she used when she did not want to alarm anyone, but there was a tightness under it that he recognized from thirty years of sharing a cup of coffee at the depot after the last train.

"Who found it?" she asked.

"The mountain did, I think," he said. "It woke me up."

Dana looked past him, then at the body. Her eyes moved quickly over the scene, the way a good officer’s do, but they lingered for a fraction too long on the woman’s face.

"I don’t know her," Dana said at last.

"You do," Arthur said. "You just haven’t decided whether you want to."

She turned to him. "Arthur, if this is a case—"

"It isn’t a case. It’s a clue," he said. "And the clue is not the body."

Dana’s gaze dropped to the rails. "Then show me."

He knelt again and pointed to the point of the track where the spur split from the main line. The switch was old, rusted, and held in place by two steel clamps that had long since lost most of their shine. A thin line of dark grease ran from the lower rail to the top of the rail above, as if someone had oiled it that morning.

"That switch hasn’t moved in ten years," Arthur said.

"Maybe a deer got near it," Dana suggested.

"Deer don’t bring grease from a can." He looked up. "Who in this town owns a can of gear grease with a blue label and a white handle?"

Dana did not answer immediately. "The logging crew at the bottom," she said. "Or the guys who service the old water tower. Or the hotel maintenance man."

Arthur nodded slowly. "Then we’ll find out which one of them was up here before dawn."

Dana looked down at the body again. "Should I call it a suicide?"

Arthur almost laughed, but the laugh died in his throat. "Suicide is a story people tell when they don’t know what else to say." He reached into the dead woman’s coat, not to touch her, but because there was a small canvas pouch tied to her belt. He untied it carefully and lifted out a folded piece of paper and a brass token.

The token was heavy, and it bore the old Pinehaven railway crest: a mountain, a tree, and a train with its windows open to the sky. He had not seen that token in twenty years. It had been lost after the last train, or so everyone had believed.

"What is it?" Dana asked.

"A conductor’s token," he said. "Only three were ever made."

"Where did you get it?"

"I had one. The others were kept at the depot." He turned the token over in his hand. "This one was issued to the chief engineer. It hasn’t been seen since the line closed."

Dana’s hand went to the notebook in her coat. "The chief engineer?"

"Marcus Bell," Arthur said. "He retired the same day I did. He lives up on the ridge now, with his wife, if she’s still alive."

"She is," Dana said. "He was at the farmers’ market last week."

"Was he?" Arthur said. "He’s been gone since the line closed, or at least he never said goodbye. He just took his keys, his watch, and a box of papers and left. No ceremony."

Dana looked at him. "You didn’t like him."

"I respected him," Arthur said. "That’s worse."

She opened the note from the pouch. It was a strip of paper, thin and brittle, with a train schedule printed on one side. The dates were from 1974, when the line still ran two passenger trains a week. The schedule showed the morning train from the valley at 11:15 and the evening return at 4:40. Both entries had been crossed out in pencil, but under the crossed-out 11:15 someone had written a single word in small, careful letters:

LATE.

Beneath it, in the same hand, was a name.

Nora Vane.

Arthur’s throat tightened. He looked at the woman on the track. The name fit. Nora Vane had been a teacher at the schoolhouse below the depot, the kind of woman who could make a room quiet just by standing at the front of it. She had disappeared from town the spring after the line closed, and no one had talked about her since.

"You knew her?" Dana asked.

"I taught with her," Arthur said, before he could stop himself. "No. I didn’t. I knew her from the train. She rode every week to the valley to see her sister. She used to leave a book in the window seat. I used to bring it back to her in the morning."

Dana closed the notebook. "Her sister. Where does she live?"

"Denver," Arthur said. "She sent a letter once. It never got opened."

"Then we’ll find out what else she sent."

Arthur looked back at the body. The morning light was getting brighter, and with it came the first real birds, their voices thin and sharp in the cold. He noticed now that the woman’s lips were not the blue of cold death, but the dry, closed expression of someone who had been dead some time. There was no sign of struggle, no blood on the gloves, no bruise on the neck that would suggest a fight.

"The body was moved," he said.

"From where?"

"From somewhere with better light." He pointed to the abutment stone. "See the scratches on the rail? Not from the body sliding. From something being dragged across the steel. The angle is wrong."

Dana followed his finger. "You’re sure?"

"I’ve spent more than forty years watching this track. I know what a body does when it falls, and what it does when it’s carried. This one was carried."

She looked toward the ridge, where the first hints of snow still clung to the pines. "We’ll need a full team. Forensics. A dog."

"Do it," Arthur said. "But don’t move her until you take pictures. If she was placed there, the angle is part of the message."

Dana nodded. "You’ve been around, Arthur."

"I’ve been retired."

She almost smiled. "For a man who’s been retired, you still read the rails like a man who’s never stopped working."

He met her eyes. "Maybe I never did."

She reached into her truck and took out a roll of plastic evidence bags and a camera. As she moved to the body, a sound came from the ridge behind them, so faint he almost missed it.

A whistle.

One long note, clean and bright, rising through the fog and then cutting off. It was not a truck horn, not a bird, not the wind in the trestle. It was a train whistle, the old Pinehaven signal whistle, the one that had been taken off the locomotives when the line closed.

Arthur turned to the ridge. "Dana."

She had heard it too. Her hand was on her radio. "I’ve got it. What do you hear?"

"Nothing but the mountain."

She looked at him, then up the slope. "You sure?"

He was sure. He had heard that whistle every day of his life, and he knew its voice the way a mother knows a child’s. It came from the direction of the old service road, the one that led to the ridge and the abandoned water tower.

"Someone’s testing the rails," he said.

"Testing them how?"

"By making us think the track is still in use."

Dana stared at him. "Arthur, there’s no train up there. There’s no power."

"No," he agreed. "There isn’t. Which is why it matters."

He looked back at Nora Vane, lying on the rails with her white glove open in the cold. A train had not run on this line in ten years. A whistle that had been decommissioned for twenty years had just crossed the gorge. And a brass token that had been lost since the last train was in the hand of a woman who had vanished twenty years ago.

The mountain was no longer quiet.

Arthur took the token from his coat and set it on the rail beside the body, where the light could touch it. Then he turned to Dana.

"Bring me the schedule," he said. "And if you see anyone with a can of gear grease and a blue label, don’t ask where they were up here."

He paused before he went. "Ask if they’ve ever heard a train that isn’t there."

She looked at the body, the whistle, the old switch, and the token. For the first time since he had seen her wear a badge, she did not look like an officer managing a town. She looked like a woman who had just heard the first note of a song she had been trying to forget.

"Alright, Arthur," she said. "Let’s go find out who’s running the last train."

Chapter One: The Last Whistle

Arthur Calloway woke at 4:30 because the mountain had begun to cough. It was not wind, not fog, not the groan of frost in the granite, but a sound he had known since boyhood: the low, iron complaint of a rail cooling in the dark. The town of Pinehaven lay below him in a valley stitched by the river and the abandoned railway line that curled up the mountain like a scar.

He stood at the window, coffee cooling in his hands, and watched the first grey light lift from the pines. For forty-two years he had read the rails the way other men read weather. He knew which ties heaved in spring, which curves swallowed a whistle, which section of the cut had a loose stone that would sing under a slow train. He had retired two winters ago, when the mountain line was closed for the last time and the company sold its cars to a logging outfit in the valley. Now he lived above the old depot, where the smell of diesel and wet oak still clung to the beams.

The coffee was good. The mountain was quiet. And that was the problem.

He dressed in the same navy trousers and cardigan he had worn for three decades, put on his boots, and took a steel pole from the porch. The old spur to the trestle ran across the back of his property, and the leaves had come down thick over the rails again. If he could see the track, he could tell whether the river had swollen or a tree had fallen. It was a habit, not a need.

The spur had not seen a passenger train in ten years, but the company still mowed the grass at the junction in August. The weeds had grown taller this year, perhaps because the new landowner in town had stopped paying for the crew. The rails gleamed faintly in the early light, like bones pulled from snow. Arthur walked the line slowly, tapping the ties where he passed. One, two, three. His rhythm was automatic. He had once walked this stretch a thousand times, always with his eyes on the point of the track ahead. Now his knees complained when the ground tilted, but his memory did not.

At the trestle, the old wooden structure rose over a narrow gorge, its planks black with age and birds. Below, the river moved dark and quick. The body was there before he recognized it as a body.

It sat against the abutment stone, legs stretched out across the lower rails, as if the woman had been waiting for a train that would never come. Her coat was the color of dried blood, and her hair, pale as birch bark, hung over her face. One white glove hung open from her hand. The other lay a foot away, as if she had dropped it in surprise.

Arthur stopped.

The silence that followed was not silence at all. It was the sound of the river, the faint tick of cooling iron, and the blood moving in his own ears. A body found on an abandoned track in a small town was not an accident waiting to happen. It was a statement.

He crouched, keeping his weight low, and looked at the rails. The woman’s shoes were on her feet, but the toes were pointing away from the direction a train would have come. If someone had placed her there, they had made a small mistake. They had forgotten that a train from the valley would have run from left to right, and the driver’s foot would have pressed the brake on the near side. The body had been laid with the wrong shoulder to the track.

She had not fallen off a train.

Arthur straightened, and his pulse gave a hard knock against his ribs. He looked toward the valley. A dog barked. Somewhere else, a screen door slammed. The town was waking up.

The first sound of wheels was not a train. It was the Sheriff’s truck, its red and blue lights blinking against the grey at the entrance to the gorge. A moment later, Sheriff Dana Whitfield was climbing the embankment with the brisk, careful steps of a woman who had spent twenty years in a town that still tried to remember who was who.

She saw him before he saw her.

“Arthur?” she called. “You’re early.”

He did not answer at once. “You’ve seen it.”

She stopped a few feet away, hand on her shoulder holster without taking it out. Her face had the flat, polite expression she used when she did not want to alarm anyone, but there was a tightness under it that he recognized from thirty years of sharing coffee at the depot after the last train.

“Who found it?” she asked.

“The mountain did, I think,” he said. “It woke me up.”

Dana looked past him, then at the body. Her eyes moved quickly over the scene, but they lingered too long on the woman’s face.

“I don’t know her,” she said at last.

“You do,” Arthur said. “You just haven’t decided whether you want to.”

She turned to him. “Arthur, if this is a case—”

“It isn’t a case. It’s a clue. And the clue is not the body.”

Dana’s gaze dropped to the rails. “Then show me.”

He knelt and pointed to the point of the track where the spur split from the main line. The switch was old, rusted, and held in place by two steel clamps. A thin line of dark grease ran from the lower rail to the top of the rail above, as if someone had oiled it that morning.

“That switch hasn’t moved in ten years,” Arthur said.

“Maybe a deer got near it,” Dana suggested.

“Deer don’t bring grease from a can.” He looked up. “Who in this town owns a can of gear grease with a blue label and a white handle?”

Dana did not answer immediately. “The logging crew at the bottom. Or the guys who service the water tower. Or the hotel maintenance man.”

Arthur nodded. “Then we’ll find out which one of them was up here before dawn.”

Dana looked down at the body. “Should I call it a suicide?”

Arthur almost laughed, but the laugh died in his throat. “Suicide is a story people tell when they don’t know what else to say.” He reached into the dead woman’s coat and lifted from the belt pouch a folded piece of paper and a brass token.

The token was heavy, and it bore the old Pinehaven railway crest: a mountain, a tree, and a train with its windows open to the sky. He had not seen that token in twenty years. It had been lost after the last train, or so everyone had believed.

“What is it?” Dana asked.

“A conductor’s token. Only three were ever made.” He turned it over in his hand. “This one was issued to the chief engineer. It hasn’t been seen since the line closed.”

Dana’s hand went to the notebook in her coat. “The chief engineer?”

“Marcus Bell. He retired the same day I did. He lives up on the ridge now, if his wife is still there.”

“She is.” Dana’s voice was careful. “He was at the farmers’ market last week.”

“Was he?” Arthur said. “He’s been gone since the line closed, or at least he never said goodbye. He just took his keys, his watch, and a box of papers and left. No ceremony.”

Dana looked at him. “You didn’t like him.”

“I respected him. That’s worse.”

She opened the note from the pouch. It was a strip of paper, thin and brittle, with a train schedule printed on one side. The dates were from 1974, when the line still ran two passenger trains a week. The schedule showed the morning train from the valley at 11:15 and the evening return at 4:40. Both entries had been crossed out in pencil, but under the crossed-out 11:15 someone had written a single word in small, careful letters: LATE.

Beneath it, in the same hand, was a name.

Nora Vane.

Arthur’s throat tightened. He looked at the woman on the track. The name fit. Nora Vane had been a teacher at the schoolhouse below the depot, the kind of woman who could make a room quiet just by standing at the front of it. She had disappeared from town the spring after the line closed, and no one had talked about her since.

“You knew her?” Dana asked.

“I knew her from the train. She rode every week to the valley to see her sister. She used to leave a book in the window seat. I used to bring it back to her in the morning.”

Dana closed the notebook. “Her sister. Where does she live?”

“Denver. She sent a letter once. It never got opened.”

“Then we’ll find out what else she sent.”

Arthur looked back at the body. The morning light was getting brighter, and with it came the first real birds, their voices thin and sharp in the cold. He noticed now that the woman’s lips were not the blue of cold death, but the dry, closed expression of someone who had been dead some time. There was no sign of struggle, no blood on the gloves, no bruise on the neck that would suggest a fight.

“The body was moved,” he said.

“From where?”

“From somewhere with better light.” He pointed to the abutment stone. “See the scratches on the rail? Not from the body sliding. From something being dragged across the steel. The angle is wrong.”

Dana followed his finger. “You’re sure?”

“I’ve spent more than forty years watching this track. I know what a body does when it falls, and what it does when it’s carried. This one was carried.”

She looked toward the ridge, where the first hints of snow still clung to the pines. “We’ll need a full team. Forensics. A dog.”

“Do it. But don’t move her until you take pictures. If she was placed there, the angle is part of the message.”

Dana nodded. “You’ve been around, Arthur.”

“I’ve been retired.”

She almost smiled. “For a man who’s been retired, you still read the rails like a man who’s never stopped working.”

He met her eyes. “Maybe I never did.”

She reached into her truck and took out a roll of plastic evidence bags and a camera. As she moved to the body, a sound came from the ridge behind them, so faint he almost missed it.

A whistle.

One long note, clean and bright, rising through the fog and then cutting off. It was not a truck horn, not a bird, not the wind in the trestle. It was a train whistle, the old Pinehaven signal whistle, the one that had been taken off the locomotives when the line closed.

Arthur turned to the ridge. “Dana.”

She had heard it too. Her hand was on her radio. “I’ve got it. What do you hear?”

“Nothing but the mountain.”

She looked at him, then up the slope. “You sure?”

He was sure. He had heard that whistle every day of his life, and he knew its voice the way a mother knows a child’s. It came from the direction of the old service road, the one that led to the ridge and the abandoned water tower.

“Someone’s testing the rails,” he said.

“Testing them how?”

“By making us think the track is still in use.”

Dana stared at him. “Arthur, there’s no train up there. There’s no power.”

“No. There isn’t. Which is why it matters.”

He looked back at Nora Vane, lying on the rails with her white glove open in the cold. A train had not run on this line in ten years. A whistle that had been decommissioned for twenty years had just crossed the gorge. And a brass token that had been lost since the last train was in the hand of a woman who had vanished twenty years ago.

The mountain was no longer quiet.

Arthur took the token from his coat and set it on the rail beside the body, where the light could touch it. Then he turned to Dana. “Bring me the schedule. And if you see anyone with a can of gear grease and a blue label, don’t ask where they were up here.”

He paused before he went. “Ask if they’ve ever heard a train that isn’t there.”

She looked at the body, the whistle, the old switch, and the token. For the first time since he had seen her wear a badge, she did not look like an officer managing a town. She looked like a woman who had just heard the first note of a song she had been trying to forget.

“Alright, Arthur,” she said. “Let’s go find out who’s running the last train.”

# Chapter One: The Last Whistle

Arthur Calloway woke at 4:30 because the mountain had begun to cough. It was not wind, not fog, not the groan of frost in the granite, but a sound he had known since boyhood: the low, iron complaint of a rail cooling in the dark. The town of Pinehaven lay below him in a valley stitched by the river and the abandoned railway line that curled up the mountain like a scar.

He stood at the window, coffee cooling in his hands, and watched the first grey light lift from the pines. For forty-two years he had read the rails the way other men read weather. He knew which ties heaved in spring, which curves swallowed a whistle, which section of the cut had a loose stone that would sing under a slow train. He had retired two winters ago, when the mountain line was closed for the last time and the company sold its cars to a logging outfit in the valley. Now he lived above the old depot, where the smell of diesel and wet oak still clung to the beams.

The coffee was good. The mountain was quiet. And that was the problem.

He dressed in the same navy trousers and cardigan he had worn for three decades, put on his boots, and took a steel pole from the porch. The old spur to the trestle ran across the back of his property, and the leaves had come down thick over the rails again. If he could see the track, he could tell whether the river had swollen or a tree had fallen. It was a habit, not a need.

The spur had not seen a passenger train in ten years, but the company still mowed the grass at the junction in August. The weeds had grown taller this year, perhaps because the new landowner in town had stopped paying for the crew. The rails gleamed faintly in the early light, like bones pulled from snow. Arthur walked the line slowly, tapping the ties where he passed. One, two, three. His rhythm was automatic. He had once walked this stretch a thousand times, always with his eyes on the point of the track ahead. Now his knees complained when the ground tilted, but his memory did not.

At the trestle, the old wooden structure rose over a narrow gorge, its planks black with age and birds. Below, the river moved dark and quick. The body was there before he recognized it as a body.

It sat against the abutment stone, legs stretched out across the lower rails, as if the woman had been waiting for a train that would never come. Her coat was the color of dried blood, and her hair, pale as birch bark, hung over her face. One white glove hung open from her hand. The other lay a foot away, as if she had dropped it in surprise.

Arthur stopped.

The silence that followed was not silence at all. It was the sound of the river, the faint tick of cooling iron, and the blood moving in his own ears. A body found on an abandoned track in a small town was not an accident waiting to happen. It was a statement.

He crouched, keeping his weight low, and looked at the rails. The woman’s shoes were on her feet, but the toes were pointing away from the direction a train would have come. If someone had placed her there, they had made a small mistake. They had forgotten that a train from the valley would have run from left to right, and the driver’s foot would have pressed the brake on the near side. The body had been laid with the wrong shoulder to the track.

She had not fallen off a train.

Arthur straightened, and his pulse gave a hard knock against his ribs. He looked toward the valley. A dog barked. Somewhere else, a screen door slammed. The town was waking up.

The first sound of wheels was not a train. It was the Sheriff’s truck, its red and blue lights blinking against the grey at the entrance to the gorge. A moment later, Sheriff Dana Whitfield was climbing the embankment with the brisk, careful steps of a woman who had spent twenty years in a town that still tried to remember who was who.

She saw him before he saw her.

“Arthur?” she called. “You’re early.”

He did not answer at once. “You’ve seen it.”

She stopped a few feet away, hand on her shoulder holster without taking it out. Her face had the flat, polite expression she used when she did not want to alarm anyone, but there was a tightness under it that he recognized from thirty years of sharing coffee at the depot after the last train.

“Who found it?” she asked.

“The mountain did, I think,” he said. “It woke me up.”

Dana looked past him, then at the body. Her eyes moved quickly over the scene, but they lingered too long on the woman’s face.

“I don’t know her,” she said at last.

“You do,” Arthur said. “You just haven’t decided whether you want to.”

She turned to him. “Arthur, if this is a case—”

“It isn’t a case. It’s a clue. And the clue is not the body.”

Dana’s gaze dropped to the rails. “Then show me.”

He knelt and pointed to the point of the track where the spur split from the main line. The switch was old, rusted, and held in place by two steel clamps. A thin line of dark grease ran from the lower rail to the top of the rail above, as if someone had oiled it that morning.

“That switch hasn’t moved in ten years,” Arthur said.

“Maybe a deer got near it,” Dana suggested.

“Deer don’t bring grease from a can.” He looked up. “Who in this town owns a can of gear grease with a blue label and a white handle?”

Dana did not answer immediately. “The logging crew at the bottom. Or the guys who service the water tower. Or the hotel maintenance man.”

Arthur nodded. “Then we’ll find out which one of them was up here before dawn.”

Dana looked down at the body. “Should I call it a suicide?”

Arthur almost laughed, but the laugh died in his throat. “Suicide is a story people tell when they don’t know what else to say.” He reached into the dead woman’s coat and lifted from the belt pouch a folded piece of paper and a brass token.

The token was heavy, and it bore the old Pinehaven railway crest: a mountain, a tree, and a train with its windows open to the sky. He had not seen that token in twenty years. It had been lost after the last train, or so everyone had believed.

“What is it?” Dana asked.

“A conductor’s token. Only three were ever made.” He turned it over in his hand. “This one was issued to the chief engineer. It hasn’t been seen since the line closed.”

Dana’s hand went to the notebook in her coat. “The chief engineer?”

“Marcus Bell. He retired the same day I did. He lives up on the ridge now, if his wife is still there.”

“She is.” Dana’s voice was careful. “He was at the farmers’ market last week.”

“Was he?” Arthur said. “He’s been gone since the line closed, or at least he never said goodbye. He just took his keys, his watch, and a box of papers and left. No ceremony.”

Dana looked at him. “You didn’t like him.”

“I respected him. That’s worse.”

She opened the note from the pouch. It was a strip of paper, thin and brittle, with a train schedule printed on one side. The dates were from 1974, when the line still ran two passenger trains a week. The schedule showed the morning train from the valley at 11:15 and the evening return at 4:40. Both entries had been crossed out in pencil, but under the crossed-out 11:15 someone had written a single word in small, careful letters:

LATE.

Beneath it, in the same hand, was a name.

Nora Vane.

Arthur’s throat tightened. He looked at the woman on the track. The name fit. Nora Vane had been a teacher at the schoolhouse below the depot, the kind of woman who could make a room quiet just by standing at the front of it. She had disappeared from town the spring after the line closed, and no one had talked about her since.

“You knew her?” Dana asked.

“I knew her from the train. She rode every week to the valley to see her sister. She used to leave a book in the window seat. I used to bring it back to her in the morning.”

Dana closed the notebook. “Her sister. Where does she live?”

“Denver. She sent a letter once. It never got opened.”

“Then we’ll find out what else she sent.”

Arthur looked back at the body. The morning light was getting brighter, and with it came the first real birds, their voices thin and sharp in the cold. He noticed now that the woman’s lips were not the blue of cold death, but the dry, closed expression of someone who had been dead some time. There was no sign of struggle, no blood on the gloves, no bruise on the neck that would suggest a fight.

“The body was moved,” he said.

“From where?”

“From somewhere with better light.” He pointed to the abutment stone. “See the scratches on the rail? Not from the body sliding. From something being dragged across the steel. The angle is wrong.”

Dana followed his finger. “You’re sure?”

“I’ve spent more than forty years watching this track. I know what a body does when it falls, and what it does when it’s carried. This one was carried.”

She looked toward the ridge, where the first hints of snow still clung to the pines. “We’ll need a full team. Forensics. A dog.”

“Do it. But don’t move her until you take pictures. If she was placed there, the angle is part of the message.”

Dana nodded. “You’ve been around, Arthur.”

“I’ve been retired.”

She almost smiled. “For a man who’s been retired, you still read the rails like a man who’s never stopped working.”

He met her eyes. “Maybe I never did.”

She reached into her truck and took out a roll of plastic evidence bags and a camera. As she moved to the body, a sound came from the ridge behind them, so faint he almost missed it.

A whistle.

One long note, clean and bright, rising through the fog and then cutting off. It was not a truck horn, not a bird, not the wind in the trestle. It was a train whistle, the old Pinehaven signal whistle, the one that had been taken off the locomotives when the line closed.

Arthur turned to the ridge. “Dana.”

She had heard it too. Her hand was on her radio. “I’ve got it. What do you hear?”

“Nothing but the mountain.”

She looked at him, then up the slope. “You sure?”

He was sure. He had heard that whistle every day of his life, and he knew its voice the way a mother knows a child’s. It came from the direction of the old service road, the one that led to the ridge and the abandoned water tower.

“Someone’s testing the rails,” he said.

“Testing them how?”

“By making us think the track is still in use.”

Dana stared at him. “Arthur, there’s no train up there. There’s no power.”

“No. There isn’t. Which is why it matters.”

He looked back at Nora Vane, lying on the rails with her white glove open in the cold. A train had not run on this line in ten years. A whistle that had been decommissioned for twenty years had just crossed the gorge. And a brass token that had been lost since the last train was in the hand of a woman who had vanished twenty years ago.

The mountain was no longer quiet.

Arthur took the token from his coat and set it on the rail beside the body, where the light could touch it. Then he turned to Dana. “Bring me the schedule. And if you see anyone with a can of gear grease and a blue label, don’t ask where they were up here.”

He paused before he went. “Ask if they’ve ever heard a train that isn’t there.”

She looked at the body, the whistle, the old switch, and the token. For the first time since he had seen her wear a badge, she did not look like an officer managing a town. She looked like a woman who had just heard the first note of a song she had been trying to forget.

“Alright, Arthur,” she said. “Let’s go find out who’s running the last train.”
