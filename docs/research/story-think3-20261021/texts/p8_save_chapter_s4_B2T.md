# Chapter One: The City on the Shell

The first thing Mara knew each morning was the sound of the shell creaking.

It was a low, groaning resonance that traveled up through the stone floors of the Tenement Ward, through the soles of her boots, into the soles of her feet and up into the bones of her skull. The city of Oolathar shifted beneath her, settled deeper into the water, and the ancient turtle's breath fogged against the underside of the great plastron just below the harbor district. The whole world rose and fell with the creature's lungs, a slow tidal breathing that had no beginning and no end.

She stepped out onto the balcony of her narrow apartment, where the morning light was already turning the sky from the deep violet of deep sleep to a bruised gold. Below her, the harbor district churned with early activity. Fishing nets were being hauled over the curved, ridged edges of the shell—the same ridges that the city had grown around like barnacles on a living thing. The water lapped against the underscirt of rock and coral that the old builders had carved into the turtle's own shell two thousand years ago.

Mara pulled her wool cloak tighter around her shoulders. The air up here, in the middle of the shell's upper plateau, was always colder. Down in the harbor district, the warmth of the turtle's blood ran close to the surface, and the stink of the sea was replaced by the stink of the beast itself—sulfur, salt, and something older, something that tasted like deep earth turned over after a long sleep.

She had been a shellwright for three years. Not a builder. Not a mason. A *wright*, which meant she shaped the living parts, coaxed the new growth out of the shell's edges when the city needed new rooms, new bridges, new roads. The shell grew, yes. The old texts said it was as fast as a man's hair, but the people who lived in Oolathar knew it was slower than that, and faster than that, depending on the season and the turtle's mood. In the cold months, the growth was barely a whisper. In the warm months, the shell could add a room's worth of new stone in a single day.

The shellwrights were the city's heart. Without them, Oolathar would crack, would bleed, would slow to a stop and drag its dead weight to the bottom of the ocean.

Mara turned from the balcony and went inside to pack her tools: a set of chisels carved from the turtle's own shed fragments, a hammer with a handle of whale-bone, and a pouch of the special salt she'd been saving for weeks. Today was a day when the shell was due for its annual mending. The great crack that ran from the Seventh Ward to the Old Gate had widened over the winter, and if it split fully during the deep dive—when the turtle dove beneath the storm layers to drink from the ocean's lowest pools—the whole eastern quarter could slide off into the sea.

She would not be in the harbor district to watch the crack. That was Kael's territory. Kael, who'd been shellwright under her master before Mara took the seat. Kael, who'd stopped speaking to her since the last time he'd accused her of letting the salt run thin.

The crack. The thin salt. The two things that had split the city's most trusted pair of menders apart.

Mara descended the spiral staircase of the Tenement Ward and stepped into the long, vaulted corridor that ran the length of the shell's middle plateau. The walls here were smoother than the harbor district below, polished by two thousand years of feet and lanterns and the slow, patient friction of the turtle's own growth. The people of Oolathar lived in layers: the harbor folk in the warm, damp, stinking low, the middle warders in the polished middle, and the shell-singers in the highest reaches near the peak, where the shell thinned and the sky pressed close.

The shell-singers were the priests. They spoke to the turtle.

Mara had never heard one speak. Not truly. Only the old stories, the ones her grandmother used to whisper in the dark: *The turtle was old when the first city was thought. The shell-singers do not command. They ask.*

She reached the lower gate of the Middle Ward, where the air was thick with the smell of roasting fish and the murmur of a hundred people beginning their day. A woman with a basket of bread on her head stepped aside, nodding to her with the easy familiarity of the middle ward. Mara nodded back and pushed through.

Ahead of her, the street opened into the great circular square at the heart of the shell. At its center stood the Crack.

It was not what she expected.

She had seen it a dozen times in the winter, through the cold, through the long nights. From a distance, it looked like a seam in the stone, no wider than a finger. Up close, in the pale morning light, it was a jagged tear three feet wide that ran from the ground at her feet all the way up into the curved underside of the shell's upper plate, where the stone met the living, ridged shell of the turtle itself.

And it was leaking.

Not water. Not yet. But a thin, golden light seeped from the edges of the crack, pulsing in slow rhythm with the breathing of the beast beneath her. The light was warm where it touched, and where it touched the stone, the stone *grew*. Tiny, pale new formations sprouted from the edges of the crack like frost on glass, fragile and delicate and utterly alien against the dark, ancient rock.

Mara's breath caught in her throat.

She had not seen anything like this in three years.

From the far edge of the square, a man was already running toward her. Kael.

He hit the crack's edge before she could speak, dropped to his knees, and pressed both palms flat against the stone. The light pulsed once, twice, and then went dark.

For a long moment, the only sound in the square was the creak of the shell and the distant, muffled breathing of the thing that held them all.

Kael looked up at her. His face was pale. His hands were trembling.

"It's waking up," he said.

And beneath her feet, two thousand years of stone and salt and careful mending shuddered, and the turtle exhaled.
