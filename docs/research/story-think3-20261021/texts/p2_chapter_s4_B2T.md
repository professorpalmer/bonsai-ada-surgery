# The Last Mile

## Chapter One

The old station in Brier still held the shape of something useful, if you knew how to look at it. The roof sagged on the north side where the shingles had gone soft in the summer of '14, and the platform was a palimpsest of old scarves, old paint, old weather. But the tracks — the two parallel bones of the line that ran from here to the county seat and then, God willing, to the interstate — those were still level. Eli Voss knew that the way most men know the angle of their own shoulders in the dark. Twenty-two years on the 6:15, then three on the 6:47 after the company reorganized. Twenty-five years of walking that mile from Brier to Hargrove with a lantern and a pocket watch and the particular patience of a man who had to keep moving in a world that kept stopping.

Now he walked it because he had to. His knees did not negotiate. The 6:15 was gone, the 6:47 was gone, the whole line was a ghost that the county had declared too expensive to maintain and too sentimental to tear up. The town had learned to live without it the way a dog learns to live without a favorite bone: by chewing on the absence until it pained.

It was October, the second week, the air thin enough in the mountains to make a person feel they were breathing someone else's life. Eli came out of his house on Sycamore Row at six, before the coffee was good, because the coffee had been bad since his wife Marta had died in March and he could not, would not, fix it. He had the lantern on his shoulder — his mother's, iron and tin, though no one would believe it had a battery in it now — and he walked the track to the water tower at the far end of the line and back. It was forty minutes. He did it because his wife used to say *you walk like you're checking for a broken rail, and you've got no business checking for one* and he had not corrected her, because she was right, and because after she died the correcting had nowhere to go.

The water tower was a rust-colored hulk, fifty feet up, and from the top of it you could see the whole valley spread out like a hand with the fingers pulled slightly apart. The fall color was coming in early this year. The oaks on the ridge were the color of old pennies. The creek ran silver under a sky so blue it looked painted. Brier was three thousand feet up, population four hundred and sixty-one, and the last thing that had happened in it that mattered was the closing of the hardware store, which had been his wife's, which had been his, which had been the whole town's, and which had been a little too much to carry.

He was two hundred yards from the station, on the stretch where the grade dropped and the trees closed in on both sides, when he saw the figure.

Not a person. A shape. A lump of something on the right rail, at the low point where the track dipped between the two boulders that had been there since before the line was laid. Eli stopped. He did not walk faster. He walked, exactly, the way he had walked for twenty-five years: shoulders down, eyes level with the track, one foot ahead of the other, as if the ground were a passenger list and he was checking for a mismatch.

The lump was red.

It took him three steps to stop thinking of it as red and start thinking of it as a coat. A long coat, dark, open at the throat, and beneath it something that was not a coat. Beneath it something that was not a person. Beneath it a person who had been a person and was now a thing that lay on a rail in October with the leaves still green enough to be called green.

Eli put his lantern on the ground. He took off his gloves, which were leather and stiff, the kind that smelled like the inside of a rail car. He crouched, and he looked.

The face was up. That was the first thing. The face was up, and it was not a face he recognized, and that was the second thing. A man's face, maybe fifty, maybe sixty, the jaw strong and a little broken, the hair gray and wet and stuck to the skull. The eyes were open. The eyes were open and they did not look like they were seeing him. They looked like they were still on a train somewhere, somewhere east, somewhere where the light came through the windows at an angle that meant morning.

Eli did not reach for a phone. He did not reach for a bag. He stood, very carefully, and he looked at the track on either side of the body, and he looked at the sleepers, and he looked at the ballast, and he looked at the mud on the ground at the edges of the platform, and he did the thing he had done ten thousand times without thinking: he read the track.

The body was on the right rail, but there was no scuff on the sleeper. There was no drag line in the ballast. No one had been pushed, thrown, or dragged here. The coat had been pulled over the body after the body was placed. The placement was neat. The body was facing the station, which meant the person, or the people, had put it where it could be seen. The face was up. The eyes were open. The person had not been wearing the coat when they died, or had been wearing it for a long time. The collar of the shirt, white, was visible under the coat, and at the throat there was a small, neat line that was not a cut.

Eli stood still for a moment. The wind came up out of the gap in the trees, and the lantern swung once, and the whole world went quiet.

He knew, with the same flat certainty he had carried for twenty-five years, that something had gone wrong here. Not the way a signal goes wrong or a brake goes wrong, the mechanical way. The other way. The way a passenger gets on the wrong train and never gets off. The way a man who should have been on the 6:47 is instead a body on a rail in October.

He picked up his phone from his jacket. His thumb went to the screen, and the screen said *No Service* in the way it always said it, because the tower that served this stretch of the valley had been down since August and the county had not fixed it because the county had not fixed anything. He put the phone back. He walked, slowly, to the edge of the platform. From the platform he could see the house at the end of the line, and the house with the red door, and the gas station that sold everything from diesel to a single roll of duct tape, and the road that went up into the trees. He could see, if he looked very carefully, a black car parked by the gas station. He had seen that car the day before. And the day before that. And the day before that.

A man in a dark jacket was walking toward the platform from the road. He walked fast, but not running-fast. The way a man walks when he has been walking for a while and the road has gotten soft under him. The jacket was dark. The coat was dark.

Eli went inside the station. The door was unlocked. It was always unlocked. He had a key, but he had not used it in three years. The station was cold and smelled like old wood and the particular mustiness of a building that has not been heated in a long time. The bench was still there, the same bench, the same green paint, the same little dent in the wood where someone had sat for a long time and left a shape in the grain. Eli sat on it. He put his hands on the back of the bench and he waited.

The man in the dark jacket reached the station at a minute he had not expected. He stood on the platform. He looked at the track. He looked at the body. He did not move. He did not breathe, or Eli could not tell the difference.

Then the man turned and walked back the way he had come.

Eli went outside. He put on his gloves. He put the lantern on his shoulder. He walked, exactly, the way he had walked for twenty-five years, but the way had changed. The way had changed the way a signal changes: you cannot put it back. You cannot say the track was never read. You cannot say the body was not there.

He walked to the gas station. The man in the dark jacket was gone. The black car was still there, but the engine was cold. There was a coffee cup on the dashboard, a paper cup with a ring around it, and a phone, screen-down, and nothing else. Eli did not go in the car. He did not touch the cup. He stood in the gravel for a while and he looked at the track, and he looked at the body, and he looked at the trees, and he thought:

*You put me on a train. I am going to make you get off.*

He did not know yet what this was. He did not know yet whose the face was, or whose the coat, or why the eyes were open. He knew only the shape of it, the shape he had known for twenty-five years, the shape of a thing that had been moved along the track when it was not meant to be moved.

The man in the dark jacket would come back.

And when he did, Eli Voss would be on the platform, with the lantern and the pocket watch and the particular patience of a man who had been walking the same mile every day for longer than anyone in the world could remember, and who had finally, at last, found the broken rail.
