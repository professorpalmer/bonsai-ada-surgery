# The Long Table

The first thing a cook on the Meridian learns is that the ship has a mood. It is not a mechanical thing — the hull doesn't moody, but the air does. On the mornings when the gravity plates hum half a tone flat, the bread tastes of metal and the crew eats quickly, shoulders up. On the mornings when the hum sits at pitch, the soup tastes of the sun, which Mira has not seen.

She is in her sixtieth year, the thirteenth in the galley. Her grandmother cooked on the Meridian, and her grandmother's grandmother cooked before the launch, in the old kitchens of New Kyoto, and Mira has never tasted food that wasn't made with hands. That is the lie that keeps the whole ship honest. The seed banks grow in hydroponic towers, the algae vats run on their own, and the protein vats extrude flesh like cheese. But the flavor, the warmth, the small human accidents — the over-salted soup, the burnt edges of the cornbread, the bread that comes out in the shape of a face — those only happen with hands.

So Mira cooks.

The Meridian carries thirty thousand souls from Earth to Proxima, and she has never left the ship. The stars outside are not stars. They are the same stars the launch generation saw, only now they are in the middle of their run, no longer destinations but neighbors, and the crew has stopped saying "the journey" and started saying "the middle." The middle of the voyage is where the children are born who will not die at the arrival, and where the cook must decide what to feed them.
