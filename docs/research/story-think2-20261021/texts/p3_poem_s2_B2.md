The last morning smells of cut grass and peach fuzz,
my boots still stamped with orchard dust.
I pull the crate of sun-warmed plums to light,
and count the hours like a debt to pay.
The sprayer's shadow stretches over rows
where my shoulders learned their steady ache.
A magpie flicks its black-and-white warning
from the apple canopy above.
The picker’s basket dips with heavy gold,
each fruit a small, sun-bitten mouth.
I twist a stem that hesitates, then lets go,
and the basket holds its patient weight.
My coworker waves from the farthest row,
her voice thin as a summer thread.
The foreman’s whistle cuts the lazy air,
and the afternoon turns to honeyed heat.
We sit on overturned crates beneath a tree,
peeling a nectarine with split nails.
The juice runs down my wrist like warm rain,
and the sugar burns, then sweetens slow.
The trucks are lined up, white and waiting,
their doors swinging open to the sun.
I load the final basket with care,
pressing the bruised and perfect together.
The orchard breathes in one long, rustling sigh,
each leaf a coin the wind has spent.
I think of every hand that broke the fruit
and every mouth that tasted the year.
The sun drops behind the barn’s red roof,
and shadows lengthen like tired lines.
My shirt is stiff with sap and dirt,
my hands still carry the shape of crates.
The last peach falls from a low-hung branch,
a small red coin on the packed ground.
I pick it up and hold it to the light,
a day’s labor in a single weight.
The truck’s engine coughs and starts to roar,
and the orchard empties out around me.
I look back once at the endless green,
then climb away with the summer in my back.
