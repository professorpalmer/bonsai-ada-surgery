# The Letters from the Not-Yet

The first letter came on a Tuesday, folded into the salt-stiffened envelope that usually held only the weekly report and the inspector’s dry complaints. Elias Wren was polishing the brass lens when it arrived by the little boat that had never once been late in thirty-one years of service. The postmark was not a date but a tide: the word “soon” stamped in ink that smelled of rain. There was no name, only the address, written in his own hand, though he had never seen it before.

He opened it with gloves still wet. The page inside was cream, soft as cloth, and the words were in a child’s careful scrawl, the letters pressed hard enough to leave shadows. Dear Keeper, it began. I am learning the sea by heart. Please do not let the light sleep tonight. There was no closing, no signature, only a small drawing of a gull with one wing folded back.

The lamp burned all night. Elias walked the gallery while the wind pulled at his coat, watching the beam sweep the black water until it looked like a slow hand. He told himself he was being foolish. A prank from the mainland schoolchildren, perhaps, or a trick played by the tide on an old man’s loneliness. Yet the ink did not smell of the post office. It smelled of the nursery in his own house, where he had once kept a glass of water for his dying mother’s lips.

By the next week, there were six letters. They arrived without warning, sometimes beneath the door of the keeper’s cottage, sometimes tucked into the hinge of the lamp room, always on paper that felt warm at first and then cool as paper. Each was addressed to him, again in his own handwriting, though the slant of his letters had changed, as if someone had studied them until they became a familiar shape. Each bore the same tide-stamp: soon.

The second letter was longer. Dear Keeper, I know you are afraid of the fog. Not because it hides the ships, but because it hides you. You want to be seen, but you are not afraid of being seen. You are afraid of being forgotten.

Elias stopped at the edge of the gallery. The fog had not come. The sea lay open and silver under the moon. He read the letter twice, then a third time, until the words no longer felt like questions and began to feel like doors.

The third letter asked him to repair the west stair.

The fourth asked him to stop sleeping after two.

The fifth told him that the gull with the folded wing would return in spring, but only if he left the lantern door open.

The sixth asked nothing. It said only: I am not a girl yet, but I am a girl soon. I am learning your name the way a stone learns the shape of water.

Elias did not sleep well that night. He sat in the kitchen with the letters spread before him, the salt wind tapping at the windows like small fingers. He had served the light his whole life. He had loved it in the old, practical way that men love tools: because it answered back, because it kept the dark from closing in, because it gave him a purpose that did not ask him to explain himself. He had not thought about the future in a long time. The future, to a lighthouse keeper, was always the next watch, the next repair, the next ship that should not be lost.

But these letters were about a future that was not yet finished.

On the seventh day, the boatman, a young man named Corin who had begun bringing the mail after his father’s death, asked if there had been trouble.

“No,” Elias said.

Corin looked at the pile on the table. “Letters?”

“Old letters.”

“From your mother?”

Elias had not told him about the first letter. He had not told anyone. “Something like that,” he said.

Corin frowned. “My mother says old letters can be dangerous. They pull you back.”

Elias considered that. “Perhaps.”

“Do you miss her?”

“I miss the house. I miss the sound of the kettle. I miss the feeling of being needed by more than one beam of light.”

Corin did not answer for a while. Outside, the gulls were calling over the rocks, their voices thin and bright. “There is a girl in town,” Corin said at last. “My cousin’s child. She has not been born yet. The doctor says she is small and quiet. The mother says she will be loud. I do not know which is true.”

Elias looked out at the sea. “And who writes letters to her?”

Corin laughed, but not unkindly. “No one, sir. She is not born. She has no letters.”

After Corin’s boat had gone, Elias stood in the kitchen and thought for a long time about the word “yet.” It was a strange word. It held a door open. It meant not now, but also not never. It meant the world was not finished with him, and he was not finished with the world, even if the world had not yet found a shape to fill.

He began to repair the west stair that morning. The third step had cracked and split, a small thing that had bothered him for years. He had always intended to fix it and never had. Now he climbed to it with the lamp beside him and saw that the crack ran deep into the wood. He had no carpenter on the island, only a mallet, a set of screws, and a length of rope. He worked until the step was firm, though it was not pretty. It looked like a promise made by a man who did not know how to make things beautiful.

The next morning, the seventh letter arrived.

Dear Keeper, I am almost ready. I know your face now. I know that you frown when the wind changes direction. I know that you hum when you think no one is listening. I know that you are not lonely because you do not have people. You are lonely because you have one person too much in your heart, and you do not know where to put her.

Elias set the letter down on the table. His hands were shaking, though not from cold.

“Who are you?” he said.

The letter did not answer.

He looked at the drawing of the gull again. The one wing was folded back, as if the bird were waiting to be set right. He understood, then, that the letters were not trying to frighten him. They were trying to prepare him. They were not from the past. They were from the not-yet.

That afternoon, a storm came in from the west, hard and low, with rain that fell sideways. The light turned in its brass house, sweeping the black water with a steady hand. Elias climbed the stairs with the lamp and the logbook, his old knees aching. At the top, he paused and looked back at the west step he had repaired. It held.

A ship was out there, he knew. Not a ship he could see, but a ship that should not be lost. The light did not care about ships. It cared about the dark. It pushed the dark back with its circle, again and again, until even the sea remembered that there was a shore, that there was a keeper, that there was someone who had not yet been born and someone who was not yet done.

The storm broke at dawn. The sea lay flat and gray, as if it had been washed. The boat came in early, and Corin stood in the bow with salt in his hair and a bundle under his arm.

“Good weather to be born,” Corin said.

Elias looked at the bundle. “Is she here?”

“My cousin’s child. She’s here.”

Elias felt something loosen in his chest, a knot he had not known was tied. He did not ask for her name. He did not ask for a letter. He only nodded, and the nod was enough.

That night, the lamp burned, and the fog rolled in off the water, soft as wool, thick as silence. Elias did not go to the gallery. He stayed in the kitchen with the last of the letters stacked on the table. The paper had become cool to the touch, ordinary, unremarkable. He had stopped reading them in order. He read them when he was tired, or when the wind changed, or when he missed the sound of the kettle.

The last letter was not the longest. It was only a few lines, written in the same child’s scrawl, but the letters were less pressed, as if the hand had relaxed.

Dear Keeper, I am here now. I know your light. I know your name. I will be loud, but I will be kind. Please do not let the light sleep tonight.

There was no drawing of a gull. There was only a small mark at the bottom, a line that curved upward like a smile.

Elias read it once, then a second time. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows, but the light still turned. He went to the door, unlocked it, and let the lantern outside air spill into the room. The smell of salt and rain came in with it, and with it something else, small and warm and impossible: the scent of a nursery where a child had just opened her eyes.

He went back to the lamp. He wound the mechanism until it turned smoothly. He checked the oil, the glass, the lens, the beam. He did it the way he had done it for thirty-one years, but now he did it for someone who had not yet learned the shape of the world.

At the gallery rail, he looked out into the fog and thought of a gull with one wing folded back. He thought of a step that had held. He thought of a name he would learn before she spoke it.

Then he went inside, and the light burned on.
