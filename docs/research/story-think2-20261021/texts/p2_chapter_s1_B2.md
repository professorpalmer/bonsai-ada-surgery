# Chapter One: The 4:17

The fog came in off the Dunmora Range at dusk the way a slow freight comes in off the mountain switchback—steadily, without hurry, and with a weight that settled into your chest. Weldon Cates stood at the kitchen window of his house on Alder Street and watched the first bank of it swallow the pine treeline, then the second, then the town itself. By the time the kitchen light came on behind him, Harwick was a smudge. By the time the fog rolled up the rail bed, it was nothing at all.

He poured his coffee into the chipped blue mug he'd carried from the 2011 season—the mug with the chipped rim he kept touching like a man feeling for a step in the dark—and sat at the table. The house was too quiet. Not silent; quiet is different. Silence is the absence of sound. Quiet is the sound of a thing that has learned to be alone. His house had learned it well since September, when he'd turned in his conductor's cap at the station office and told old Mr. Finch that the rail was no longer a part of his week.

Twenty-three years. That was the number that sat on the mantel like a small, stubborn stone. Twenty-three years on the C-7, the last scheduled service on the Dunmora line, the one that threaded up through Harwick and over the carrow ridge to the logging town of Pinebriar and back. Twenty-three years of the same route, the same switches, the same rust-colored mileposts at 14, 15, 16. He'd learned to read the mountain by the way the rails caught the afternoon light, by the pitch of the whistle at the 12th crossing, by the particular way the fog thickened when a cold front was stacking up above the treeline.

He sipped his coffee. It had gone lukewarm. Outside, the fog was thick enough now that the streetlights had become soft white orbs, and the sound of the house itself—the tick of the pipe, the low hum of the refrigerator—had grown the volume of a thing that could be measured.

Weldon finished the coffee. He set the mug in the sink and went to the hall closet where the conductor's cap lived in a box on the top shelf. He didn't open it. He just stood there a moment, hand on the handle, and then let the closet door swing shut.

---

The body was found at the station.

Weldon heard about it the way he'd always heard things in Harwick: first through the radio in the kitchen, then through the doorbell, then through the person at the door. The station master, a young woman named Darla Fitch who'd taken over Finch's job two winters ago and who still, Weldon thought, treated the whole rail yard like she was in a museum where she had to be careful not to touch anything. She stood in the doorway in her rain coat, hair plastered to her face, and said, "You need to see this, Mr. Cates."

He did need to see this. He'd known before the knock that something was wrong, because the town's sound had shifted. Harwick was a town of small sounds: the church bell at eight, the school bus on the corner of Fifth and Main, the particular clatter of Mrs. Okafor's shopping trolley on the gravel in front of the post office. Those sounds were all still there. But beneath them, running low and steady like a second track under a first one, was a noise that wasn't. A sound that had no name yet.

They drove out in the station truck. The road to the rail yard was a single lane of blacktop that cut through the fog like a blade through butter, and Darla drove it fast, the wipers slapping in a rhythm that was just short of panicked. Weldon kept his eyes on the road ahead of him and on the hands on the wheel behind him, and he tried not to think about the number 4:17, which he'd been thinking about since the knock, though he couldn't yet say why.

The station was a low brick building at the edge of the yard, the same building Finch had worked in for thirty years and that would have stood there before Finch's father was born. The windows were painted shut in November, the way Finch had always done it, because the cold seeped through the glass and into the ledger. The yard itself was a tangle of overgrown weeds and rusted rails, the heritage cars sitting in a row like old horses in a stable, their paint flaking in the damp.

But the platform was different.

Darla killed the engine and they walked the length of the platform. Weldon's boots crunched on the gravel, and he noticed the tracks were clear of weeds for a good fifty feet. The last time the line had run had been October 14, the autumn heritage run, and since then the grass had been coming in fast. But here, at the 14th mile marker, the weeds were pressed flat. Stomped. The ballast was disturbed in a line that went from the track to the edge of the platform and back.

A man. He was a young man, maybe twenty-five, maybe twenty. Weldon's first read of him was the read a conductor gives a piece of equipment before it goes into the yard: what's he doing, what's he broken, does he need oil or does he need a scrapyard. He was lying on his side, face down, one arm tucked beneath his chest and the other stretched out with the palm up. His coat was dark, dark wool, the kind a man in his late twenties would wear if he had money and no use for it. His face was turned toward the tracks.

Weldon knelt. He did it slowly, the way a man his age has to, but he knelt, and he looked.

The young man's eyes were closed. His lips were slightly parted. There was blood at the temple, a thin line that had dried dark in the cold, and there was a bruise beginning to bloom along the jaw. But Weldon wasn't looking at the blood. Weldon was looking at the hand that was stretched out, palm up, and in that hand the young man was holding something.

A brass train whistle.

The kind they'd given each conductor on his last day. Finch had given Weldon his in '92, a small brass thing with a cracked horn, and Weldon had worn it on a chain until his wife's funeral, and after that it had sat in a drawer at the station office for thirty-one years. This one was newer. The brass was bright. And around the neck of the whistle, looped through the eyelet like a lanyard, was a small leather tag with an embossed number: C-7.

Weldon turned the whistle over in his fingers. The horn was intact. He had heard a hundred of these whistles from the cab, and he knew the sound. But he had never, in twenty-three years, heard one sound from a body in the yard at a closed line in November, held in a dead man's palm, tagged with the number of a train that hadn't run in three weeks.

Darla was at the body's head, her face pale, saying to no one in particular, "The sheriff's office is on the line, but the phones are—" She didn't finish. The fog had closed in, and the yard was a small room of white air.

"Who is he?" Weldon asked.

Darla shook her head. "No name. No ID in the coat. He was found an hour ago, maybe more. The gate on the rail yard was closed and chained, Mr. Cates. I checked the lock when I got here this morning, before I found him. The chain was still there. The gate is twenty feet from where he's lying."

Weldon looked at the gate. He could see it in the white.

"He got in some other way," Darla said.

"He was on the train," Weldon said.

It was not a question. He'd seen the disturbed ballast. He'd seen the line of pressed weeds. And he knew the gate, and the chain, and the fact that the lock had been fresh, and he knew that a man who was dead and cold would not have been walking in through that gate with his palm open.

Darla looked at him. "The heritage run was the fourteenth," she said, very carefully, as if she was reciting a timetable she didn't believe. "That's three weeks ago."

"I know what the heritage run is, Darla," Weldon said.

He knelt back down. He looked at the young man's face one more time. The bruise on the jaw was fresh, a purple-blue that hadn't faded. The blood at the temple was old. And the hand—his left hand—was open, and in the palm, cradled like a thing you didn't want to drop, was the brass whistle. And on the wrist, half hidden by the sleeve of the coat, was a watch. A small digital thing, cheap, the kind a twenty-year-old would wear. It was stopped.

4:17.

Weldon knew that number. Every conductor on the C-7 knew that number. The 4:17 was the time of the last stop at the 12th crossing, the one where the ridge narrows to a single switch and the rails bend hard to the right. It was the time when, if a conductor was still on the line, the next thing that would happen was the sound of the brakes at the station at 4:31. No one got off the 4:17. No one ever did.

The young man's wristwatch was stopped at 4:17, and the gate to the yard was chained, and the line had been closed for three weeks, and the body was on the platform, and the hand was open, and the whistle was brass, and the tag was C-7.

Weldon stood. His knees did not forgive him. He put the whistle back in the dead man's palm, gently, the way you would put a tool back on a workbench after you were done.

Darla was on her phone again. "I'm going to call the sheriff," she said. "Mr. Cates, please, you don't have to stay—"

"I'm not staying," he said. "I'm going home. I need to look at a timetable."

She looked at him. "It's November, Mr. Cates. The line is closed."

"I know." He walked toward the truck. His boots were wet where they'd touched the ballast, and the gravel was in his hair. The fog was so thick now that the station was the only thing he could see, a low brick shape in the white, and beyond it, the tracks disappearing into the mountain like a sentence that hadn't been finished.

He got in the truck. Darla sat in the passenger seat, still on the phone.

"You don't have to be here," she said, when she put the phone down.

"No," he said. "I don't."

He put the truck in gear. The wipers made their slow, slapping rhythm. The road ahead was white, and the road behind was white, and the fog pressed against the windows like a hand. Weldon drove home through Harwick, and the small sounds of the town went on without him, the church bell at eight, the school bus on the corner, the trolley of Mrs. Okafor on the gravel. He drove past the station office, where the light was on inside, and he did not stop. He drove all the way to the top of Alder Street, where his house sat, and he parked, and he sat in the driver's seat for a long time, and he thought about 4:17.

The 4:17 was the only time on the C-7 when a conductor could be alone on the line. The ridge narrows. The switch bends. The next stop is four minutes away, but in those four minutes, the conductor is in the cab with the mountain and the dark and nothing else. No one has ever been killed on the C-7. No one has ever been killed on the Dunmora line. It was a line that ran on schedule and on weather and on the slow patience of a man who knew every inch of it.

And then a man dies in the yard on a closed line, and his watch is stopped at 4:17, and he is holding a whistle tagged C-7, and the gate is chained.

Weldon got out of the truck. He went inside. He locked the door. He went to the hall closet and opened the box on the top shelf.

The conductor's cap sat on a layer of newspaper, and the brass whistle on the chain lay beside it, and the two of them had been sitting together for thirty-one years, and now there was a new one in a dead man's hand, and the number C-7 was a number that had no business on a body in November.

He put the cap on the mantel. He took the whistle off the chain. He set it next to the cap.

Then he went to the kitchen, filled the blue mug, and started the coffee.

The fog was still out there. It would be out there all night. And tomorrow the sheriff would come, and Darla would come, and the young man would be put in a bag and carried out to a van, and the platform would be swept, and the weeds would come back. And the line would stay closed.

But Weldon had a timetable in his head, and the 4:17 was on it, and the 4:17 had a name, and the name was his.

He sipped the coffee. It was still hot.

Somewhere beyond the fog, on the ridge where the rails bent hard to the right, the mountain was holding its breath.

And the 4:17 was waiting to be found.
