## final text

# The Tally Mark

## Chapter One

The 6:15 used to be the first thing I saw and the last thing I heard.

Thirty-two years I spent with that train, riding the crest of the Kessler Pass in a cab so cold your breath fogged the glass and then iced the glass before the fog could form. I know that because I remember every winter, and I know it because the other guys would stand around in the dining car afterward and say, "Walt, you didn't even notice it was forty under." And I'd say, "Forty under or forty over, I notice the rails. That's the job."

Now there's no 6:15. No 9:40. No midnight freight that hummed down the mountain like a second heartbeat under the sleeping town of Crestline. The Northern Consolidated shut the line down three winters ago, after the last of the logging crews moved out to the valley and the mountain had nothing left to ship but snow. They tore up the rails in the lower cut and left the upper stretch — two miles of track that climbs into the pine forest where the weather turns mean and the cell phones die — they left it standing. "Decommissioned," they called it. A polite word for *we don't care anymore.*

I live on Sycamore Street, three doors from the old station where the platform still overhangs the ballast. The station itself is a museum now, or rather, a room inside a museum. The town put it up as a heritage exhibit. They put the old ticket windows and the conductor's booth in glass. They did not put the smell of the booth in glass. You can't.

My wife, Margot, passed two years ago in that house, in the kitchen where she kept her hip on the counter and peeled apples while I watched the mountain. She said I stared at the window. "You're not looking at the pines, Walt," she said. "You're counting the branches." I told her I was checking for ice. She laughed, but I didn't. I was checking for ice, and I was counting, and I was listening for the horn, and I would do the same thing if I were alive.

I am alive. The pines are bare. I was having my coffee at 5:40 on a Tuesday in November when the doorbell rang.

I was already halfway to the door. I don't know why I was. I know I was already halfway. My legs moved before my mind finished the sentence that would have stopped them.

The man on the porch was young, maybe twenty-five, in a state police uniform that was too big in the shoulders. The rain had the kind of flat, gray quality that only comes up in the mountains — not a storm, just the sky remembering it has water and deciding to pour it without ceremony.

"Mr. Kessler?" he said.

"You got a warrant?"

He blinked. Not unkindly. Just the way a person blinks when a question is so foreign to their framework that they don't know how to file it. "Sir, I'm with the Crestline PD. We need you to come to the upper spur."

"The upper spur is a dead line," I said. I should have said it softer. I didn't. I was still in the kitchen, where Margot's counter was still cool and the apple peels had been rinsed into the sink. I was still in the booth. I could feel the cold of the glass in front of me.

"Someone's on the tracks," he said.

I went outside. I put on my boots. I put on my coat. I did not put on a hat because it was only November and I was being a man in a small town and not a man in a story. The young officer — he said his name was Reyes, and he had a kind face, the kind of face that gets used up by a certain amount of grief — walked me to the cruiser and I let him.

The road up the pass narrows after the second switchback and the trees lean in from both sides until the sky becomes a long, thin coin of gray. I'd driven that road a thousand times in the service car before the line closed, and a thousand times after, walking it with Margot when she was still walking it. The upper spur begins at the old water tower, a skeleton of rusted iron that used to fill the tank for the engines before the diesel days. From the water tower, the two tracks fork. One goes down to the station. One goes up into the pines, where there is a switch, a long straight stretch of about three-quarters of a mile, and then a wall of rock where the mountain simply ends and the rail line ends and the world ends.

I knew the rail. I knew every tie, every joint, every spot where the ballast had shifted in a slide and we had to pack it back. I knew it the way you know the grooves in your own palm. I had put my hands on it so many times that I could close my eyes and feel the exact height of each rail from the roadbed.

They found the woman on the straight stretch, about six hundred yards from the switch.

I don't want to describe it fully. I want to describe the thing that made my blood go quiet, the thing that was not the body. I want to describe the thing that made the young officer's kind face go hard, because that was the thing that mattered, and that thing was in the ties.

Between the two rails, about three feet from the body, someone had scratched a mark into the wooden tie with something sharp. A single diagonal line, thick as a finger, going from the top of the rail down toward the sleep. A tally mark. One stroke. The kind you make when you're counting, and you don't know what else to do with your hands while you're counting.

I stopped. I crouched. I put my hand on the tie. The wood was dry under my palm even though the rain had been falling for an hour. I knew that. I knew the wood was dry because the scratch had been made recently, and the person who made it had been on the inside of the ties, the side facing the cut, and the rain had been coming from the north and the cut had been facing east, and the inside of the ties was shielded.

I stood up. I didn't look at the body. I looked at the young officer.

"Who is she?"

"We don't know. No ID. No phone. No wallet. She's maybe thirty. Wearing a jacket, a dark coat. Her shoes are work boots, the kind with the steel toes. They don't match anything on the town's population file. She's not from Crestline."

I was already walking back down the straight, and he was behind me, and I didn't tell him. I was counting. I was counting the ties. From the switch to the body. I was counting because I had driven this stretch a thousand times and I could do it in the dark and I could do it without looking and I was doing it now with the rain in my face and the cold in the air and something in my chest that was not fear and was not anger but was something worse because it was recognition.

The tally mark was my mark. Not the exact scratch — I'd never made a scratch like that. But the *angle*. The way the line fell from upper left to lower right, the way the pressure built at the start and tapered off at the end. I had made a thousand marks like that on the sides of the booth, on the door, on the ticket book, every time I had one extra minute between runs and nothing to do with the extra minute. One stroke for each mile I'd done that day, or one stroke for each day I was on the line. I didn't know which. I never did. I just made the mark and it was done.

I stood at the switch. I put my hand on the rail. I could feel the metal was cold but not frozen. Not the cold of a body that had been here since morning. The metal was the cold of a thing that had been touched an hour ago and then left.

"Officer," I said.

"Yes, sir?"

"Who was the last crew to be on the line this week? Not the town crews. The state maintenance crew. They do a walk-through every quarter. Who walked it?"

He hesitated. He looked at me. I think he saw the thing I saw. The thing in the ties that made the whole town's quiet little geography rearrange itself, the way a single scratch on a record makes you hear the whole song differently.

"I don't know," he said. "We'll check."

I put my hand back in my coat pocket. I pulled out the small, worn notebook I carried in my breast pocket, the one Margot had filled with recipes and I used for nothing but the miles. I opened it. I looked at the last entry. I had written it that morning, in my small, cramped hand, in the kitchen with Margot's counter still cool in my memory.

*Nov 14. No trains. Counted the pines on the west slope. 4,217. Four missing. They fell in the August slide.*

I closed the notebook. I looked at Reyes.

"Keep her jacket," I said. "Don't let them bag it in the cruiser. Put it on the ground. The rain's still coming and if they bag it in a bag, the water seeps in and the fibers go. And check the boots. Steel toe, but what brand. And check the hands. Not for prints. For the calluses. She was a worker, but a specific kind of worker, and the calluses will tell you what."

Reyes stared at me.

"You want me to talk to the maintenance crew?" he said.

"I want you to ask the wrong questions," I said. "Because the right questions are the ones I'm not ready to ask."

I walked back down the road. The rain was getting heavier. The pines were black and wet and silent, and the mountain was full of water and the air was full of the smell of wet granite and the distant, thin smell of the creek that ran down the other side of the pass and never made it out to the town because the cut swallowed it every year in October.

I drove home. I put on the coffee. I sat at the table and I held my hands under the hot water of the kettle and I listened to the mountain and I did not think about the tally mark.

I did not think about the fact that the line had been dead for three years.

I did not think about the fact that the scratch was fresh.

I thought about Margot's counter. I thought about the way she used to peel apples and the way the peel came off in one long, unbroken ribbon and she'd let it fall into the bowl in a spiral, and she'd say, *Walt, you're doing it again.*

And I would say, *Doing what?*

And she'd say, *Counting.*

And the kettle would whistle and I would take it off the heat and I would pour the coffee and I would sit in the cold kitchen and I would not count.

Not yet.

Outside, the rain went on. The pines stood. The old station on Sycamore Street had its glass and its booth and its smellless glass, and on the upper spur, two miles up in the pines where the cell phones die, the tie held its one small scratch like a man holds his mouth closed in the dark, keeping something in.
