# Chapter One: The Last Departure

The mountain had a voice that Elias Vance knew better than any human mouth. It spoke in the groan of the trestle at dawn, in the tick of cooling steel, in the hiss of snow sliding off the rails of the abandoned Kettle Ridge line. For thirty-one years he had listened to it as a conductor, and for the last eleven he had listened as the only person in Alder Creek who still knew the difference between a good snowfall and a dangerous one.

At seventy-two, Elias was no longer the wiry man who had once raced the mail from Pine Hollow to the terminus with a brass bell in one hand and the conductor’s ledger in the other. His back ached when the barometer fell, his left knee protested when the wind came off the river, and his eyes needed stronger light to read a timetable. But his ears, and his memory, were still exacting.

The old depot stood at the end of Main Street, its paint curled like dry leaves, its ticket window dark. Inside, Elias kept what he had not called a private office for a long time, though a small sign above the door now read: Vance Investigations. No one in town understood what that meant, which suited him. He found lost dogs. He located missing cattle. Once, he had convinced the mayor that the ghost in the water tower was a raccoon with a grudge. Lately, though, his work had begun to lean toward the darker side of the mountain, and the word “investigations” felt both too large and too small.

The phone rang at 6:12, before the sun had cleared the eastern ridge. It was a black handset in the old rotary style, the only modern thing in the depot except the coffee maker Elias had installed after his last divorce. He let it ring twice.

“Vance.”

“Are you coming?”

It was Sheriff Ada Crane. She did not use small talk the way city women did, not because she was cold but because in Alder Creek there was always work to do. “We’ve found a body at Black Arch.”

Elias set the cup back on the counter as if it had become suddenly hot. Black Arch was the short tunnel at the bend in the Kettle River, the place where the old line disappeared into the mountain and reappeared with a cough of stone dust. No one ran trains through Black Arch anymore. The tunnel had been condemned after the last derailment, but the mountain kept its own traffic: hikers, loggers, the occasional drunk with a dog, and, in winter, the snow itself.

“Who is it?” he asked.

“We don’t know yet.” The pause was brief, but in the pause there was something he recognized. Ada was afraid of saying the wrong thing. “She’s a woman. Young. There’s a whistle around her neck.”

Elias reached for his coat. “A train whistle?”

“That’s what it looks like.”

He was already moving. “I’m on my way.”

By the time Elias reached the river bend, the town had begun to gather. Alder Creek was small enough that a body could make the whole valley feel occupied, and this body had already produced a knot of cars, boots in the mud, and the particular stillness of people who knew what they had found but did not want to be the ones to explain it.

Sheriff Crane met him at the mouth of Black Arch. She was wearing the same tan jacket she had worn since the summer she became sheriff, and her dark hair was pinned up with a pencil. Behind her, a search-and-rescue volunteer held a body bag over his shoulder like something he might change his mind about.

“Mr. Vance,” Ada said. “Thank God you’re here.”

“God isn’t involved in mountain deaths,” Elias said gently. “That’s usually the work of weather, negligence, or other people.”

She gave him a look that might have been amusement, but her eyes were not amused. “You heard about the line?”

“I live with it.”

The tunnel opened into the morning like a wound. Snow lay in drifts along the abandoned rails, the sleepers black beneath the white crust. The river below had swollen to a deep blue-brown, its current pulling at the rocks with the patience of a thing that had no intention of being stopped. In the center of the track, half a step above the frozen ground, lay the woman.

Her coat was gray, city gray, the kind that looked expensive until it got wet. Her boots were red, new. Her hair, dark and damp, fell across a face so pale it seemed to belong to someone else. She was perhaps twenty-five, maybe twenty-seven. Her mouth was open, not in shock, because the cold had already taken all surprise from her. Her hands were folded across her chest, one wrist turned up so the inside of the sleeve showed, and around her neck hung a small brass whistle on a leather cord.

Elias stopped at the edge of the scene.

The whistle caught the early light.

It was not the kind of whistle a city girl would carry for luck. It was not polished, not bright, not decorative. It was the old Kettle Ridge model, with the rounded mouth and the cracked leather fitting, the kind that had been used for decades before the railroad went to diesel and then to memory. He had carried two of those whistles in his life. One had belonged to him. The other had belonged to the man who had taught him to walk the line without stumbling in the dark.

“Don’t touch anything,” Ada said.

“I’m not.”

“You should be.”

“I’m listening.”

That seemed to make her uneasy.

The volunteer who had been holding the body bag lowered it carefully onto the snow. “Found her around half past four,” he said. “By the tunnel mouth. There was a note on her coat.”

“Who read it?”

“Me. Then Sheriff Crane. Then I felt like I should stop.”

Elias nodded. “Read it again. Out loud.”

The volunteer swallowed. “It says: The 7:14 never left.”

The words sat in the air like frost.

Ada crossed her arms. “You know about the 7:14?”

Elias did not answer at once. He looked at the woman on the track, then at the tunnel, then at the river. The mountain was quiet except for the water, and even that seemed to be holding its breath.

“I know a lot about trains,” he said. “Most of them are gone.”

“No,” Ada said. “The old 7:14. The passenger that went through Black Arch twenty years ago.”

Something moved in Elias’s chest, a small cold thing, like a key turning in a lock he had forgotten he owned.

“I remember it,” he said.

“You were on it.”

“I was a conductor.”

“Elias—”

“I was the conductor.”

The sentence came out harder than he intended. Ada’s expression softened, but not much. In Alder Creek, softening was a rare weather, usually limited to the first week of spring.

“Twenty years,” she said. “The line’s been closed. Nobody runs through Black Arch.”

“People still walk through it.”

“Yes, but not trains.”

“No,” Elias agreed. “Not trains.”

The volunteer cleared his throat. “The medical examiner’s coming up from the county. She’ll be here in about an hour.”

“Good,” Ada said. “We can wait.”

Elias did not wait. He stepped closer to the body, still keeping his distance, and studied her. A woman’s face, but not the face of a local. He did not know the valley’s women by the lines on their cheeks or the way they set their jaws, but he knew the difference between someone who had lived on a mountain all her life and someone who had only recently come to visit it. Her skin was too smooth. Her hands were too clean. She had not worked with her hands, or if she had, it had been the kind of work done in a city studio, not the kind that left splinters under the nails.

“There’s a camera,” he said.

Ada knelt. “Where?”

“Her coat pocket.”

The volunteer opened the outer pocket with gloved fingers and brought out a compact digital camera, the kind that had been popular in the late 1990s but was still in use by the stubborn or the poor. Elias took it from him with a nod.

“She’s a photographer,” Ada said.

“Or a journalist.”

“Same thing out here.”

Elias looked at the camera. The lens cap was off. In the back pocket of her coat was a folded paper, damp at the edges. He did not take it without looking at Ada first. She nodded.

The paper was a timetable, yellow with age, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. It showed the Kettle Ridge Railway passenger schedule for the morning of November 17, 1999. The 7:14 from Alder Creek to Pine Hollow. The 7:58 from Pine Hollow to the terminus. A small line beneath the times read: All trains subject to mountain conditions.

Elias turned the page.

The next page was blank except for a single pencil line in the center:

She was waiting at the platform.

His fingers went cold.

Ada saw his face. “What is it?”

“A name,” he said. “It’s not her name.”

“No, it’s not.”

“It’s my sister’s.”

The volunteer looked as if he wanted to step back into the tunnel, but the snow had packed too solidly for that, and he stayed.

Ada’s voice was very low. “Your sister died in the 7:14.”

“She was on the 7:14.”

“She was found in the wreck.”

“She was found in the wreck.” Elias said the words the way a conductor checks the brakes: slowly, carefully, to be sure they are not already failing. “The tunnel collapsed behind the train. Two passengers. A woman and a girl. They were in the second car.”

Ada looked at the body. “Clara Wexley. That’s her name. County ID came through twenty minutes ago. She’s a freelance photographer from Denver. She came into the valley two days ago to shoot pictures of the old line.”

“Why would she come to Black Arch?” Elias asked.

“No one knows. She was seen near the depot the day before. She wanted to photograph the tunnel mouth.” Ada’s eyes narrowed. “And this note?”

Elias looked at the pencil line again. His sister’s name. His sister, Lila. Twenty years. The date of the accident was November 17. He had not thought about it in months, not since the anniversary passed without a candle, without a drink, without the particular ache that came when he passed the tunnel on foot.

“The whistle,” he said.

Ada followed his gaze. “What about it?”

“It’s not just a whistle.”

The volunteer shifted. “Mr. Vance?”

Elias crouched, not close enough to touch the body, but close enough to see the leather cord at the whistle’s neck. It was worn through in one place, frayed, the knot tightened so many times it had cut a groove into the material. He had seen that knot before. He had tied it himself.

“It’s from the caboose,” he said.

Ada waited.

“The 7:14 had a caboose in the fall of ’99. Temporary, because the freight service had just been cut. The whistle was lost when the second car went into the tunnel. The wreck crew didn’t find it. I remember because I stood at the platform and watched them sweep the tracks for two hours.”

“You were there,” Ada said.

“I was the conductor.”

The words came out again, quieter this time, but not softer.

The volunteer looked from one of them to the other. “So the whistle that was gone for twenty years is on a dead woman’s neck.”

“Is it the whistle from the 7:14?” Ada asked Elias.

Elias looked at the brass mouth, the small dent on the side from a fall, the faded red enamel lettering around the rim: K.R.R. He had seen that dent a hundred times in his memory. He had cleaned it once in the depot, in the blue hour before the morning train, while Lila had been laughing at something outside the ticket window.

“It’s the whistle,” he said.

Ada’s jaw tightened. “Then someone took it from the wreck.”

Or someone found it, Elias thought, but he did not say that. Not yet.

The river moved below them, indifferent.

“Who else knew she was coming?” Ada asked.

Elias considered. “The depot owner. The newspaper. A few people on the trail.”

“The depot owner is old Mr. Halloway. The newspaper is the Alder Creek Courier. The trail is a mess of hikers, dog walkers, and people looking for their fathers.”

“Someone wanted her here.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Ada did not answer.

Elias looked back at the body. There was a dark stain at her throat, thin and faint, not enough to be dramatic, but enough to make the town’s usual chatter about weather and property lines feel suddenly obscene. He wanted to ask whether the wound was old or new, but he did not ask. He was not a doctor. He was not even, officially, a detective, though the sign on the door had convinced enough people to say the word.

He turned the camera over in his hands. On the back, a small sticker had been peeled away, leaving a faint rectangle of lighter plastic. He pressed his thumb against it and felt a raised seam.

There was something inside.

“Your sister,” Ada said.

“Yes.”

“She was a passenger.”

“She was a passenger.”

“And she was waiting at the platform.”

Elias looked at the timetable. His sister’s name, written in pencil, as if someone had been keeping a record of her. Lila had not been a passenger who waited. She had been a woman who ran late to almost everything, who always arrived with her scarf tied one way in the cold and another way in the rain. She had been the kind of passenger a conductor liked because she never made a scene, and because she always tipped the ticket taker even when the fare was the same.

“She didn’t wait at the platform that day,” Elias said. “She was on the train.”

“But the note says she was.”

“Then the note is wrong.”

Ada studied him. “Or the note is true about someone else.”

The words landed with a small, hard force.

Elias looked at the tunnel mouth. The darkness inside was not the same darkness he had seen it in before. It had changed, or perhaps he had. There was a line of ice across the opening, and in the snow at the base of the tunnel he saw something that made his stomach tighten.

Footprints.

They were not deep. The snow had been too soft, too fresh, but the prints were clear enough to follow. They began at the tunnel mouth and ran out along the track for perhaps twenty steps before disappearing into the drifts. He crouched again, closer this time, and traced them with his eyes.

The woman on the track had red boots. These were not red. They were work boots, wide-soled, the tread worn on the inside edge. They stopped at the track. There were no footprints going back into the tunnel.

“How long has she been here?” he asked.

Ada checked her watch. “We don’t know. The cold will help the medical examiner.”

“No,” Elias said. “I mean how long after she died.”

Ada’s expression changed. “The body’s cold. But not rock cold.”

Elias looked at the footprints again. The man in the work boots had walked out of the tunnel, stood on the track, and then had gone somewhere else. The snow around the body was undisturbed except for the small disturbance at her shoulders, where she had been moved. Someone had placed her there. Someone had tied the whistle around her neck with the old knot.

“Ada,” he said. “When was the last time the 7:14 timetable was used?”

She blinked. “What?”

“The schedule. The one she had in her coat. When was the last time it was printed?”

Ada looked at the timetable in his hand. “I don’t know. It’s old.”

“It’s not just old. It’s the original run. After the accident, the county burned most of the schedules. I remember because I kept one.” He touched the edge of the paper. “This one wasn’t burned.”

The volunteer made a small sound. “Do you think someone’s been in the tunnel?”

Elias looked at the footprints. “Yes.”

“Did you find them?”

“No. I’m only just finding them.”

Ada crouched beside him. “Then we should mark them.”

“Do it.”

She nodded to the volunteer, who went to fetch chalk from his gear. Elias stayed where he was, looking at the body, the tunnel, the river, and the whistle around Clara Wexley’s neck. The brass caught the sun again, and for a moment it shone so bright it seemed to have a pulse.

Lila had always said mountains were just the earth dreaming. Elias had told her that was nonsense. Mountains were not dreams. They were schedules. They ran on time whether or not anyone remembered the route.

The whistle had been lost for twenty years. It had been found, or recovered, or stolen, and now it was around the neck of a stranger. The note said the 7:14 never left. The timetable said she was waiting at the platform. The footprints said someone had been to the tunnel and had not gone back in.

And the body said someone had been waiting for him.

Elias stood.

“Ada.”

“Yes?”

“Who else in this town knows about the whistle?”

Ada’s face went still. “I don’t know what you mean.”

“The whistle from the wreck. The one nobody found.”

“I don’t know.”

Elias looked at her for a long moment. He had known Ada Crane for six years. She had been honest when she said she didn’t know. But he had also learned that in small towns, knowing and saying were not always the same thing.

“Then ask,” he said. “Ask the people who were there when the wreck happened. Ask who took the whistle, if anyone did. And ask why a woman from Denver is dead with it around her neck.”

Ada stood, brushing snow from her knee. “The medical examiner will be here soon.”

“Yes.”

“And the body goes to the county.”

“Yes.”

“And you come with us.”

Elias looked back at the tunnel. The mouth of Black Arch had not changed, but it had. It was no longer just a hole in the mountain. It was a place where something had been hidden, and something else had been returned.

“I’m going,” he said. “But not to the county. First, I’m going back to the depot.”

Ada frowned. “What will you find there?”

Elias did not answer. He did not yet know. But the mountain had given him a clue, and in his experience, the mountain never wasted its words.

When they walked back to the cars, the volunteer asked him a question he had been waiting to answer.

“Mr. Vance, do you think she was killed here?”

Elias looked at the footprints.

“She was moved here,” he said. “That’s different.”

The volunteer swallowed. “Does that mean she wasn’t killed here?”

“Maybe.”

“Maybe is a good word,” the volunteer said, “for things that aren’t safe.”

Elias almost smiled. “It is.”

They got into the sheriff’s cruiser. Ada started the engine and let it idle while the snow dusted the hood. Elias sat in the passenger seat, his hands in his lap, his mind moving through the old timetable one more time.

Alder Creek to Pine Hollow, 7:14.

Pine Hollow to the terminus, 7:58.

All trains subject to mountain conditions.

The 7:14 had never left.

Not that morning, perhaps. Or perhaps it had left and never arrived. In the old days, that would have meant a derailment, a snow slide, a bridge failure, a man falling asleep at the wheel. It would have meant a story the town would tell in winter, a story with a name attached to it.

Lila’s name.

His name.

The mountain’s.

Ada looked at him in the rearview mirror. “You’re thinking about your sister.”

“Yes.”

“About the accident?”

“Yes.”

“Elias, we need to be careful. If this is connected, the town’s going to have questions.”

“I know.”

“You’re the only one who knows the line.”

“I’m the only one who still remembers it.”

Ada turned the car toward Main Street. “Then we’d better be careful not to remember it wrong.”

The cruiser pulled into the depot lot, tires crunching on snow. The depot stood dark and quiet behind them, the sign above the door pale in the morning. Elias opened the door and stepped out, then stopped.

On the ticket window glass, where the snow had begun to gather in a thin line along the sill, something had been written.

He had not seen it from the car.

It was written in pencil, small and neat, the kind of handwriting that belonged to someone who had learned to write on a timetable without smudging the ink.

THE LAST DEPARTURE IS STILL BOARDING.

Below it, a single number:

7:14.

Elias stood very still.

Behind him, the cruiser engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down Main Street, a door opened and shut. A dog barked once and stopped. The mountain held its breath, and for the first time in twenty years, Elias Vance heard it waiting.
