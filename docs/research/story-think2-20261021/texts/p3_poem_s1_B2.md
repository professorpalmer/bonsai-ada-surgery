## final text

I lace my boots to the same dirt I know by heart,
the way the path bends where the peaches lean heavy,
the way August has been thickening on the branches
all summer long. The sun is already a coin
pressed flat on my shoulder. I lift the crate
and it's lighter than it was when I started—
or maybe my arms have just forgotten how to ache.
I cut a pear at the shoulder of the branch,
the stem gives with a soft, green whisper,
and the fruit drops into my palm like a secret
I didn't need to keep. I drop it in the bin
alongside the peaches I've carried since the east
of the orchard, where the old man's house
sits under its fig tree like a rumor
of something warmer than this field.
By ten, my shirt is a dark map of salt
and my back has a word for the hour
that no English syllable could hold.
But the rows keep their long green sentence
and I am just another mark in it—
a comma where the apples bow their heads.
At lunch, under the walnut's thick black umbrella,
we share a thermos of cold lemonade
and someone says, "You coming back next year?"
and the whole table goes quiet in the way
a field goes quiet when a hawk circles low.
I say I don't know. I mean it. I mean it.
The afternoon sun slants through the leaves
and lays gold on the dirt between the rows,
and I pick what I pick because my hands
remember before my mind can argue.
The last peach is a bruise of orange-red.
I close the crate at five. The gate swings shut.
I set the last crate on the truck bed
where the afternoon light is doing that
long amber slow-motion on the paint.
The orchard breathes its one long, sweet breath
through the screen of leaves I'll be dreaming in
all winter. I walk out
into the cooling light.
