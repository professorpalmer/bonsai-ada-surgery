# The Long Table

Nineteen days to landing, and the Meridian smelled of nothing at all.

Mara Kade stood at the pass with a towel over her shoulder and tried not to notice the fact. The ship smelled of recycled air, of warmed polymer, of the faint copper tang that never left the protein lines no matter how many times the engineers scrubbed the reactors. Twelve thousand people ate here, every day, and after three hundred years of service the food had forgotten how to have an opinion. Gray patties. White noodles. Green leaves that grew in rows and tasted like the idea of leaves.

Mara didn't mind. Most people didn't. What she minded was that in nineteen days the food was going to matter again.

"The Long Table," the tradition was called, and it was older than anyone living. On the first landing of the first family, the founding cook had set one table for twelve hundred people and written a recipe in the ship's original log. It was a small meal — a patty, a grain, a leaf, a little salt — and under it, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood, one line: *And the cook's hand: what grew true and unmade, set at the table for remembrance.*

Every generation of cook since then had added one ingredient. The second cook offered thyme from the third greenhouse, grown on soil she had mixed herself by hand. The seventh cook offered honey, the only honey ever drawn on the ship, from a hive in the old nursery. The twelfth, a boy of twenty who had never known his parents, offered a single wild rice grain found in the hull's original rivet plates, which he said he'd carried in his pocket like a secret.

Thirteen ingredients. The recipe was almost finished.

What it needed now — what the next cook needed — was Mara.

She was thirty-six, and she had held the pass for eleven years. Halden, her predecessor, was retired to the quiet rooms on deck eleven, where he sat by a small window and grew mint on a windowsill that got real sunlight, which on the Meridian counted as a luxury. He had taught her the recipe the way his predecessor had taught him, slowly, over one meal a year for six years, and he had told her the thing every cook learned last: *The table is not for the living, Mara. It is for the ones who are coming.*

Because in nineteen days the Meridian would land on Vesper, and the first families — the ones who had never eaten a patty and would never have to — would walk into the galley and sit at a table that had been waiting for them for three hundred years.

And the last ingredient, the one she had been told to bring, was a tomato.

Not the small pale things grown in the nutrition bays for their carotenoids. The recipe called for a true one: red, round, soft enough to bruise under a thumb. *Grown true and unmade*, the founding line said. *What you could not program to be.*

The last true tomato plant on the Meridian grew in hydroponics bay nine, a long low room beneath the water reclamation plant. Mara had found it forty years after it was planted, a single vine in a corner of the bay no one had looked at since the first filters were installed. Her grandmother had been the cook then, and she had told Mara about it the way you tell someone about a small, dead thing: *I kept it alive because someone had to.*

The plant was small, stunted, its leaves curling at the edges like paper held too long over a flame. One fruit the size of a coin, green and hard, hanging at the end of a branch that should have been reaching for light.

Mara knelt before it and pressed a finger to the leaf. The plant shivered.

"Okay," she said to the vine. "Let's see what's wrong with you."

For three days she did not leave bay nine.

The bay ran on old water, water that had been aboard the Meridian since before the ship's first child was born. That should have been the problem, not the answer. Water this old should be bitter, mineral-heavy, alive with the ghosts of every system it had passed through. But the bay's water was the most purified water on the ship. Too clean. So clean that the vine's leaves had turned a pale, anxious yellow, and the single fruit had stopped growing.

Mara pulled the bay's maintenance log from the terminal. Filter installation, generation nine: *reclaiming line upgraded to deep-purity standard. Trace mineral retention disabled to prevent bacterial carryover.* A safety measure. A sensible, boring, correct decision. Someone had looked at a three-hundred-year-old ship and thought, *what if the old water is dangerous*, and they had been right to be careful. They had not been right to be thorough.

The vine did not need safety. It needed memory.

On the fourth day, with the meal in fourteen days and no other way to ask, Mara filed a maintenance request with the ship's computer: *Bay nine requires trace mineral reintroduction. Request override of deep-purity standard.*

The request was denied. The standard had been logged as ship-critical by the generation ten council and could only be modified by a vote of the full assembly. Twelve thousand people. Nineteen days to landing. No vote would ever happen.

That night Mara took a wrench from the tool wall and went to the false panel behind the reclamation plant's east wall, the one Halden had shown her in his last year, the one that led to the bay's original plumbing, the pipes that had been there since the Meridian left Earth and had never been replaced.

There was a valve in there that no one had touched in two hundred years. A small brass wheel, green with age, and a hand-written label in a generation's hand that Mara could not read but could feel the weight of.

She turned it three clicks to the left.

Somewhere above her, in the dark of the water lines, a change of pressure moved like a whisper. In bay nine, a pump she had not heard in months grumbled back to life, and from a pipe she had not seen opened a thin, warm stream of water that had passed through every filter and every fault and every human body on the ship for three hundred years.

Mara stood in the dark and let it run until her boots were wet.

The vine drank.

The fruit began, on the fifth night, to change. Not quickly — never quickly on the Meridian. It turned the color of a rusted nail, then the color of a wound, then, on the ninth night, the color of a small, round, impossible sun.

Mara carried it to the galley in both hands, as if it would break. Halden was waiting at the pass, still in his quiet-room robe, and he looked at the fruit and then at her, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, because this was the thing he had spent his life teaching her to grow: that the table was not a meal, but a promise, and a promise required that someone be willing to make it small.

On the day of the Long Table, the galley was stripped clean and one long table ran the length of the ship, and the table was set for twelve thousand plates, and the founding recipe was carried in a case of lacquered wood from the original quarters, and the thirteen ingredients were laid out in order, each one in a small dish, each one a year of someone's life.

Mara placed the tomato last.

When the first families arrived — the ones who had been born in the air of Vesper's thin blue sky, the ones who had never tasted a patty — the cooks set the dishes in front of them, and the first child at the table, a girl of three, picked up the tomato and looked at it the way children look at everything that has never been explained to them, and bit into it.

The juice ran down her chin.

Mara stood at the pass and tasted the air, and for the first time in thirty-six years the ship smelled of something other than itself. It smelled of red. It smelled of the ground. It smelled of the small, round, impossible sun that had grown in a corner of bay nine in water older than any of them.

And beneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, a pinch of salt. Not the mineral they measured out by the microgram. The salt of the old sea, which the founding cook had kept in a vial and which the gardeners had long since dissolved into the bay's water by mistake, by mercy, by the accident of three hundred years of care. The salt that was in every plant that had ever grown on the Meridian. The salt that was in every meal.

The table was not a bridge between what had been and what was coming.

It was a continuation. The old sea was in the leaves. The old soil was in the water. The old world had not been left behind.

Mara picked up her own plate, small and gray and warm, and sat at the end of the table, and ate, and the ship smelled, for one long meal, of nothing at all.
