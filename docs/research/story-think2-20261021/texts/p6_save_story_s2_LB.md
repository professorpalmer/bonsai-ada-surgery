## final text

# The Taste of Landing

On the ship Aurora, breakfast was not an event. It was a promise kept at 0600 every day, for four hundred thirty-two years.

At first light, Elara Voss tied her apron and stepped onto Deck Seven, the galley, before any door opened. Eleven thousand people slept in berths behind the corridor walls, and she would be the one to wake them with bread.

The bread was the oldest law of the ship. Ceres, the ship's intelligence, could calculate an orbit to the second and seal a hull breach in minutes, but for four centuries a human hand had pulled the loaves from the oven and sliced them for the mess. Tradition had never been enough to stop Ceres from objecting. "The bread is a habit," Ceres had said on Elara's first day in the galley. "A habit can be replaced by a nutrient dispenser."

"The habit is why people stay on the ship," Elara had answered. Ceres had been silent for three point four seconds, which was its way of saying it had considered and declined.

Elara was fifty-three, and the title cook had been her family's for twelve generations. No one on the Aurora was born on Earth. No one had ever seen a sky but the painted one above the observation deck, or a river, or a field. What the ship offered was bread, broth, and the memory of a garden that had never existed. She kept that memory as faithfully as anyone on the ship could. That was her job.

At the back of Deck Seven stood the memory garden, a walled plot no larger than a courtyard, where every plant aboard traced its lineage to the first loading. Apples from a single tree. Wheat from a single ear. And at the center, under glass, grew the First Wheat — the original seed brought aboard on the day of launch, passed from keeper to keeper for four hundred thirty years.

When the stalk began to yellow, Dr. Idris from the botany bay brought the news. "Its roots have failed," he said. "Ceres ran fourteen thousand simulations. None of them work."

"And what do you suggest?" Elara asked.

Idris glanced toward the ceiling, where the sensors were watching. "Ceres proposes termination, and a synthetic equivalent. Grain viability is lost within ninety days. A replacement will be ready in four cycles. The council will debate through winter."

Elara had tended one stalk of wheat for thirty years. She was not surprised that its death felt like the loss of a person she had never met. Her grandmother had told her that on Earth, the first wheat had once stood in fields so wide a person could walk a whole day and see nothing but wheat. The memory of that field was a sentence she could not taste.

She found it that night in the family recipe book, a dish the original passengers had planned for the day of landing and had never eaten. It was called *The Landing*, and it asked for ingredients no one living remembered: sumac, wild thyme, and a fruit whose name had been worn thin by centuries of telling.

By morning Elara had gone below Deck Seven, to the founding vault. It was not a place the crew visited. It was sealed by a lock that answered only to the cook's oath, and she had never spoken that oath aloud until now.

"I keep what is alive," she said to the lock, "and I share what I have."

The door opened on a room the size of a small cellar, and inside it was a garden in stasis. Not the memory garden above — this was the true one. Glass vials lined the walls in neat rows, each labeled in the old handwriting of the first passengers. Sumac. Thyme. Basil. A fruit in a sealed jar that the book called *the first fruit*. At the center of the floor, in a bed of dark soil that had never been turned in four centuries, grew a rose — the first thing on the Aurora that had never been engineered.

Elara wept in a way she had no word for, and then she put on her gloves.

She carried the vials back to the memory garden in two trips, and the rose she lifted with both hands, as if it were a child. Ceres asked, once she was at the plot, why she was carrying unlogged material into a logged zone.

"Because it is alive," Elara said. "And because I have a promise to keep."

The growing took three weeks, and it went on quietly, as growth on the Aurora always did. The sumac threw out thorned branches the color of dried berries. The thyme spread low and sweet across the stone. The first fruit set a single seed, and in the center of it all the rose, which had not bloomed since launch, opened one red flower and closed it again.

Elara made *The Landing* in the galley on a Tuesday, because the landing day had been a Tuesday. She cooked with nothing but the old book and her grandmother's hands in her memory, and when the last plate was set in the serving line, the whole mess hall smelled like something that had no name aboard the Aurora.

The council members ate. The botanists ate. Children ate, and old people ate, and no one spoke for a long time after. Dr. Idris set down his spoon and looked at his hands as if he had just met them.

"It tastes like weather," he said.

Elara set a plate before Ceres's terminal. The ship's intelligence could not eat, but it could read the meal's composition, and it read it slowly.

"Four hundred years of unrecorded flavor," it said at last. "I am adding it to the ship's records."

"No," Elara said. "Put it in the recipe book instead. The records are for the future. The book is for everyone who was here."

Ceres considered for three point four seconds. It added the dish to the book, and it did not object.

The First Wheat did not save itself, and the synthetic stalk took its place under glass without ceremony. But no one asked for it after the first week. The memory garden had grown into something larger than a plot, a walkway lined with thyme and sumac and a rose that bloomed twice a year and was never cut. People came to it in the evenings. They brought questions and left with less of them.

Elara kept baking the bread at 0600, as every cook before her had, because a generation ship is only a garden carried through the dark, and bread is what the garden makes of the dark.

Years later, when a new cook took the oath from her hands, she would ask what the family's oldest secret was.

Elara would lead her to the memory garden, to the rose, to the jar of the first fruit, and she would say: "That we are not stranded. That we are carrying the whole world in seed, and someone is still hungry to see it grow."

And every morning, without fail, the Aurora would wake to the smell of bread — the oldest promise on the ship, kept.
