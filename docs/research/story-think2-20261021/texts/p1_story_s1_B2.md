# The Thursday Post

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was wrong, because Elias knew by now that every letter in the world, even the ones meant for a lighthouse on a bare rock at the end of the world, came on a Thursday. He found it wedged under the iron door of his cottage, half-swallowed by a tide that had come in past the stones where it usually stopped. The envelope was cream-colored, unmarked except for a postmark in the lower right corner. He held it to the kerosene light and read the date.

It was fourteen months in the future.

The postmark said: *Vesselin, 11 October 2026.* Below it, in ink the color of a bruise, a name: *Elias, Keeper.* No street. No town. Just the date and the name.

He opened it. The handwriting was a young woman's, slanting and hurried, the letters joined as if she had written them while walking. *Dear Elias. The fog today was thick as wool. I stood at the rail and watched the horn cut through it, and I thought of you. You always adjust the wick a little before the gale. You always. I know it because I have watched. I am not here yet. I am sorry for that. I will be here on the fourteenth, and I will have a dog with one torn ear. I hope the light is still turning. I hope you are still here. — Maren*

He set the letter on the table. He did not sleep that night. He sat with his hand on the wick wheel, feeling its slow rotation, and he told himself the date was a mistake, a printing error, a joke by the postman in the next village. He told himself this until the coffee in his pot went cold and the sea began its low, patient grinding against the base of the tower.

The second letter came on a Thursday, as expected. The date was the same, fourteen months out. This one was longer. Maren described the lighthouse from the inside in a way that was almost unbearable in its precision: the particular crack in the third stair that Elias had been meaning to fill for nine years. The way the oil in the lamp tank always ran low on Wednesdays because the valve was sticky. The smell of the cottage in November, which he knew was the smell of beeswax and old wool and the faintest trace of the peppermint he kept in the stove pipe to keep the mice out. *You keep peppermint in the pipe,* the letter said. *I know because I breathe it when I am small, and it is the only sweet thing in the house.*

There was no Maren. Elias had checked. He knew every face within a hundred miles. He had kept this lighthouse for twenty-three years, and in those twenty-three years, no girl named Maren had ever been born within sight of the coast. No Maren in Vesselin, no Maren in the next village, no Maren in the three towns that made up the whole of this stretch of coastline. He called the district office in the city. The clerk listened and said the number he had given him was correct and the address was real and the postmark was valid, and then said, *But I cannot find any resident by that name.* The clerk paused. *Have you been there before, sir? Do you know the family?* *There is no family,* the clerk said, after a moment. *That address has always been the same. But I suppose it is only a house.*

Elias hung up. The wind was picking up. He went to the lamp room and turned the light, and the beam swept out over the black water and he thought, for the first time in years, that he was not alone in the world. That was a terrible thing. The sea was not enough. The light was not enough. The solitude he had chosen, the solitude he had earned after the drowning of his wife and the years of silence that followed, had become the shape of his life, and now something was pressing against it from the other side.

The letters came every Thursday. Fourteen months. Fourteen months of a woman writing from a future that had not arrived, telling him small true things about a life that had not happened. She described the dog, who was brown with a patch of white over one eye, who slept at the foot of the bed and woke with a yelp at every thunderclap. She described the house at 37 Vesselin Street, which was not a house Elias had ever seen, though the clerk had confirmed it existed. She described the sound of the lighthouse horn in her childhood, the way it made the milk in her plate tremble. She described the day her mother had died, and she described it the way Elias had described his wife, with the careful, level tone of someone who had been told, many times, to be brave.

And then, in the thirteenth letter, the tone changed.

*Dear Elias,* it said. *The winter is coming early this year. The forecast says a low system in the second week of January. I am telling you this because I have seen it. The cliff at Kettle Point will give way on the ninth. I am eight years old that night. I am walking back from the school run, and my shoes are wet and I am thinking about nothing, and the ground gives, and the dog is there, and the dog is there, and—*

The letter stopped. The rest of the page was a single, hard line: *I am not supposed to be here. But I am here. I am writing this because I will die if you do not go.*

Elias read it four times. He put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where it lay warm against his ribs like a coal. He thought about the cliff at Kettle Point, which he had walked along a hundred times, which was solid and ancient and had never given way. He thought about a girl who was not yet eight years old, a girl who was not yet born, standing at the edge of a cliff that had been safe for a thousand winters.

He went to the cliff. He went with the lantern and the rope and the flare gun. He climbed the whole length of the headland and he looked for the crack, the hairline fracture that a geologist might point out on a survey map but no one could see with the naked eye. He found nothing. He walked the length of the rock and he found nothing. And he stood at the top and he watched the sea and he felt the particular, sickening lightness that comes when you believe something impossible and are forced to live in the belief.

The storm came on the ninth. Not a low system. Not a gentle winter gale. A black wall of wind that came off the north sea and hit the lighthouse like a fist. The light went out at half past ten, and Elias was in the lamp room with the wick wheel in his hands and the wind screaming through the gallery, and he was thinking, with a calm that surprised him, *if Maren is real, the cliff is real, and I am late.*

He took the flare gun. He took the rope. He took the dog, which was Maren's dog, which had not yet been born, which he had seen in the letters so many times that it was no longer impossible, and he set him in the cart and he went. The wind was so bad that the cart's wheels ground in the mud and the dog whined and the flare gun was cold in his hands. He reached the cliff at two in the morning.

The ground was there. Solid. Ancient. Unchanged.

He stood on the headland in the wind and he looked at the cliff and he looked at the sea and the dog sat at his feet and the light from the lighthouse swept the headland once, twice, and the cliff did not move.

And then the wind stopped.

Not slowly. It simply stopped, the way a held breath stops, and the sea lay flat and silver, and on the cliff face, ten feet above the waterline, there was a crack. A new crack. A crack that had not been there an hour ago and would not have been there an hour from now. Elias walked to the edge. The rock below the crack was damp and dark and had the texture of something that had only just decided to break.

He stood there for a long time. The dog licked his hand.

In the morning, the post brought no letter. But at the base of the lighthouse, under the iron door, half-swallowed by the tide that had come in past the stones, Elias found a small, cream-colored envelope. The postmark was a date that had already passed. Three years ago. The name inside was Maren. The handwriting was a child's, large and round and confident, the kind of handwriting a seven-year-old learns from a schoolbook.

*Dear Elias,* it said. *Thank you for going to the cliff. I was at school that night and I am eight and I am alive. The dog is here. He has one torn ear and he sleeps at the foot of the bed and he is very brave. I am coming to see you in the spring. I have been waiting for a long time to meet you. — Maren*

Elias put the letter in his breast pocket, over his heart. He went to the lamp room. He adjusted the wick a little, just in case of the gale. The light turned. The beam swept out over the black water and into the dark, and somewhere out there, in a house that was not yet built, in a year that had not yet come, a girl with a torn-eared dog at her feet was sleeping, and the sea was calm, and the light was still turning, and Elias was still here.
