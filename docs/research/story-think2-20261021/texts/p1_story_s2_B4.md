## final text

# The Unwritten Letters

The first letter reached the lighthouse on a Tuesday in March, folded twice inside an envelope the color of weak tea. There was no stamp, no address, and no name except Wren, written in blue ink that smelled faintly of salt and camphor. The postman, Ned, had set it on the stone step with a shrug. No one named Wren lived in Halloway. No one, Mr. Elias Finch was told, had lived there in forty years.

Elias was sixty-three, thin as a rope, and had kept the light at Blackmere Point for nineteen years. The lamp room was a cold glass room at the top of a white tower that leaned a little to the west, as if trying to see the sea. He cut the letter open with a knife and read the words.

“Dear Mr. Finch, my name is Wren. I am not yet born. Please keep the lamp lit on the night the storm comes from the west. I will come to the light when I am ready.”

He laughed, but the laugh went out of him like smoke. Then he put the letter in his breast pocket and went down to make tea.

For weeks nothing happened. The sea did not send a storm, and Wren did not appear on the beach or at the lighthouse door. But a second letter came on the following Tuesday, and then a third, and soon the envelope arrived every week, always the same pale paper, always blue ink, always with no stamp and no return address.

The letters were never long. They spoke in short lines, as if the writer had learned the language only a little while ago.

“I saw the gull with the torn wing yesterday. You have not mended it. I am sorry for your grief.”

“I know the north step cracks when the wind turns. Do not step there after dark.”

“I have counted the windows in the lamp room. There are nine. I am not yet born, but I can see them.”

Elias read each sentence twice. He went to the window and looked for the gull. It was there, one wing held crooked, watching him from a spar. He had not mended it; he had only watched it suffer.

He began to write back. He put his letters on the table by the door, where the postman might take them, but Ned never collected them. Once he carried one to the village and asked the minister if a woman named Wren had written anything. The minister frowned. “No such woman,” he said. “No such child. There have not been many births.”

“What if she is not a woman?” Elias asked.

The minister’s face tightened. “Then she is a ghost.”

Elias did not like the answer, but it fit.

That winter the weather turned hard. The sea rose in black ridges, and the wind had a voice like a broken hinge. Elias climbed the stairs to the lamp room with oil in one hand and a rag in the other. The clockwork had seized, and the great lens was going dark. He could hear the mechanism complaining, a sound he had learned to know as well as his own heartbeat.

He remembered a line from a letter that had arrived three weeks earlier, dated for that very night.

“When the glass breathes, turn the brass key three times and place your palm on the cold iron. Do not let the light die.”

He should have thrown the letter away. He should have told himself it was a trick, a joke, the work of a child who had stolen paper from the schoolroom and imagined a lighthouse keeper with a crooked tower. But the lamp was dark, the wind was at the door, and the brass key sat in its place as if waiting.

He turned the key once. The lens shuddered.

He turned it twice. The gears groaned.

He turned it third, and laid his palm flat against the iron. It burned him, then cooled. A beam swept out over the water, slow and certain, and below, on the black line of the rocks, he saw a boat struggling through a swell. Its lights flickered and went. He kept the lamp turning by hand, his breath fogging the glass, until dawn came up gray and thin behind the clouds.

The next morning the boat was found, empty but intact, its hull scarred by the stones. The fishermen said the man inside had jumped into the sea to save his cargo. No one could explain why the cargo had been there, or why the boat had been so badly damaged. Elias said nothing. He only polished the lens until it looked like a window into another room.

Letters kept coming.

“I was born in a house with a broken chimney,” one said. “My mother could not name me for two days. The midwife gave me the name Wren because the sky was gray and there was a bird on the wire.”

Elias read it at the table by the window. He had not meant to cry, but the sea sound outside had become very close, as if the tower were standing in a shell. He wrote: “Welcome, Wren. I hope the chimney is mended.”

Another letter came a year later.

“I am seven. I know you are in the lighthouse. I do not know how I know it. The woman who finds the old books says a man once kept the light there for someone who did not exist yet. I want to go there.”

Elias wrote back that he would leave a chair by the window.

The years after that were strange. He grew older, but the letters made him feel both younger and more fixed, as if his life had become a narrow path through a field. He wrote to Wren about the gulls, the fog, the new keeper who came to inspect the tower and called his methods outdated. He wrote about the minister’s death, about Ned’s hands shaking, about the winter the sea came close enough to touch the door.

Wren wrote back about school, about a boy who laughed too loudly, about a dog they brought home from a shelter, about learning to drive, about a marriage that ended, about a daughter named Lark. Elias never asked if any of it was true. It did not matter. To receive a letter was to believe the writer was real, even if the writer was only a future shape.

One autumn, he received a letter that made his hands stop.

“I am old now,” Wren wrote. “My hair is white. I am coming to Blackmere Point. The lighthouse is no longer a lighthouse. It is a museum, or a room inside a museum. There is a tower where people can climb but are told not to touch anything. I am going to touch things.”

Elias looked out at the sea. The tower still leaned west. The north step still cracked. The gull was gone, or perhaps it had simply learned to fly without pain.

He wrote: “Be careful on the stairs.”

That was the last letter that arrived before he died. He died in the winter, alone in the lamp room, with the light turning. The new caretaker found him slumped beside the lens, his hand still on the iron, a small smile on his face. On the table were two envelopes, one addressed to Wren and one to no one.

The letter addressed to Wren said: “I kept the light for you. I never met you, but the light held. If you are climbing the stairs, do not look for me. Look for the beam.”

The other envelope was addressed only “To whoever reads this.” Inside was a single line: “The lamp needs oil when the glass breathes.”

Years passed. The lighthouse was restored, then sold, then opened to visitors as part of the old harbor museum. The tower was white again, but cleaner, and the stairs were lined with railings. The old logbooks were kept in a locked case, and the caretakers were told not to remove them.

Then a woman came.

She was tall and gray, and she wore a coat the color of the sea at dusk. She climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the railing, and stopped often to rest. The caretaker watched her from below, uncertain whether to stop her. She did not seem to be looking at the exhibits. She seemed to be counting the windows.

In the lamp room she paused before the glass. It was cold. The old lens stood behind it, dark and clean, with a brass key still fitted in its place. She turned the key once. Nothing happened. She turned it twice. The gears groaned.

She placed her palm on the iron.

A beam swept out over the water.

For a moment, the whole tower filled with light.

Outside, on the black line of the rocks, a small boat was struggling through a swell. Its lights flickered and went. The caretaker ran to the window and called out to the coast guard. By the time help came, the sea had carried the boat clear of the stones.

The old woman did not speak when she was asked if she knew the keeper who had written the logbook. She only touched the glass, where the breath of the cold had left a mark that looked, for a second, like a handprint.

“Thank you,” she said.

Then she climbed down the stairs, and the light went on behind her, turning and turning, keeping its promise to no one and everyone, as if the future had finally come home to the place where it had first been written.
