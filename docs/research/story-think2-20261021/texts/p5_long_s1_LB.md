# The Station That Wasn't

It started with a brass token.

Maren found it on a Tuesday, wedged in the lining of a coat that had hung in the attic for eleven years. The coat was her mother’s — a charcoal thing, long-sleeved, with the pockets of a woman who had once kept her hands warm in a signal box. Maren had been clearing it out the day after the funeral, working in the grey November light that does not so much come through a window as seep through it, and she had not expected the attic to contain anything that could still surprise her. After eleven years, a dead woman’s coat should have been just a coat. But her mother had been a woman of small, deliberate secrets, and the secret came out of the left inside pocket on a Tuesday, cool and heavy, with the grain of a stamp pressed into it: a station sign, and beneath the sign, in letters no bigger than a fingernail, the word *Hollow Hill*.

The token was a train ticket, though not a paper one. It was brass, the size of a coin, and when Maren held it up the grey light passed through the embossed window in its centre and turned the colour of weak tea. On the back, in ink that had faded to the colour of dried blood, were two lines:

*For M. Vane. Take the 11:47. Do not look at the clock. Bring the token. Say her name when it asks.*

Maren sat on the attic floor with her back against the window and turned the token over until the edges cut into her palm. She knew what the 11:47 was. Every train in the city had a number, and the 11:47 — the last of the night, the one that left the central station with only the night porter and the cat as passengers — was the one the old-timers in the reading room called *the ghost line*. It ran to a destination that had not existed since 1974, when the eastern line was cut and the old station at Hollow Hill was pulled apart, brick by brick, in the space of a single summer. Maren had been four years old. She remembered the sound of the demolition — a long, low tearing, like a book being opened all the way down — and she had asked her mother why the building was being taken down. Her mother had put a hand on her small head and said, *Because it has finished its work*, and that was all.

But the token said the work had not finished.

For three days Maren did nothing but stare at it. She set it on the desk in the archive, beneath the reading lamp, and she sorted her files around it as if it were a document of some importance, because perhaps it was. She was an archivist. It was her job to believe that the past was made of paper and that paper could be trusted, so long as it was legible and properly catalogued. But paper could be forged, and this was not paper.

On the fourth night she took it with her.

She told no one. She told no one because she did not fully know what she was going to do, and telling someone would have made it a decision, and decisions had edges. She put the token in her pocket and went to the central station at ten-forty, which was early, because the last trains ran late and the early ones were crowded, and she wanted to sit down before the 11:47 came and be in a position to act. The station was the old one, the one with the high ceiling and the painted beams and the smell of wet wool and coffee gone bad. She sat on a bench by the platform and watched the trains come and go, and she thought about her mother, who had been a stationmaster at the eastern line for twenty-two years before the line was cut. She thought about the way her mother had spoken about the station not as a building but as a living thing, the way you might describe a house you loved: the particular creak of the third step, the way the light came in at four in the afternoon and made the signal room look like the inside of a lantern. She thought about how, in the last year, her mother had been growing quieter, and had begun to leave small things in places — a button on the mantel, a folded note on the kitchen table — as if she were trying to leave her behind in pieces, so that when she was gone there would be something to hold.

The 11:47 came in at 11:41, which was early, and it was not the 11:47. It was a different train, with different lights, and it pulled up and ran out again before the clock could say it was the 11:47. Maren was standing by the platform edge, watching, when she noticed the man.

He was old in the way that some people are old — not wrinkled but thinned, as if the years had been poured off him like water. He stood at the far end of the platform, with his back to the station, and he was watching the empty line with the patience of a man who expected something and had never been surprised by its lateness. When Maren looked at him, he turned, and his eyes were the grey-green of the tea that the token made the light look like.

"You are here," he said. It was not a question.

"I don't know what I'm here for," Maren said.

He looked at the token in her pocket, where it was visible through the thin cotton. "Your mother gave you that."

"How do you know about my mother?"

He smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not reach his eyes. "Her name was Elise," he said. "Her name is not a thing she would have given to the station. The station only keeps what it is given. I was the conductor. I have been the conductor for a very long time."

Maren wanted to tell him that Hollow Hill was a station that had not existed for fifty years. She wanted to tell him that the eastern line had been cut, and that the building had been demolished, and that her mother had been a stationmaster at a place that was now a car park. But before she could say any of this, she looked at the platform clock, and she saw that it was not running. The hands were still, and the face was dark, and she understood, with a feeling that was not quite fear but something older and less useful, that the 11:47 was not a train but a *place*, and that she had been waiting for it to arrive for a very long time.

The train came in at 11:47.

It was dark, and it made no sound. It pulled up to the platform with the slow, certain manner of a thing that knows exactly where it is going, and the doors opened, and the interior was lit by a warm light that was not the same as the station light — it was the light of a lamp in a room, the light of a kitchen at night, and it smelled of tea. Maren looked at the man, and he nodded once, and she boarded.

The carriage was empty. There were two seats by the window and a table between them, and on the table was a glass of water and a small plate with a biscuit on it. The window showed the dark, but the dark was not empty. It was a landscape, and the landscape was moving, and the landscape was made of the city — but not the city Maren knew. She saw the river, and the river was where the river had been, before the bridge, before the road, before the water had been coaxed into a channel and given a name. She saw the fields, and the fields were green, and the green was not the green of autumn but the green of something that had never been cut. She saw the station, and the station was there, at the far end of the platform, a small building with a high roof and a lit window, and in the lit window there was a figure, small and still, and Maren knew, before the figure moved, that it was her mother.

The train stopped.

The doors opened, and the platform was there. It was the platform of Hollow Hill, and the platform was empty except for the old man, who stood at the far end with his hands behind his back, and the station, which stood in the middle of the dark with its light on, and the figure in the window. Maren got off the train. She did not know why she got off the train. She only knew that she had to see the figure, and that she had waited eleven years for the right moment, and that the right moment was not a thing you chose but a thing that happened to you.

She walked across the platform. The air was cold and it smelled of rain and of the particular scent of a place that has been waiting. She reached the window, and the figure in the lit room turned, and the figure was her mother, and the figure was not old, and the figure was not dead.

"Elise," Maren said.

Her mother looked at her, and her face did something that Maren had never seen it do in eleven years — it opened. "Maren," she said. "You came."

"You knew I would come."

"I hoped you would. I have been waiting for you to be old enough to understand what this is, and young enough to choose whether to stay in it. The station asks a name every night at 11:47. It has been asking yours for eleven years. I am the answer it gave, and I have been the answer it gives, and I am tired of being an answer."

Maren did not understand. She stood at the window and she looked at her mother, and she saw that her mother was not a ghost. Her mother was a memory, and a memory that the station had kept, and the station had kept her because she had asked it to, and the station had asked her to because she had given it a token, and the token was her, and Maren was the token, and the token was the only thing that could leave.

"I saved a man," her mother said. "The night the line was cut. A man was on the train. He was dying, and the train was going to Hollow Hill, and there was no one at the station to stop it, and I stopped it. I stopped it and I pulled him off and I put him on the ground and I waited for the ambulance that never came because the road was cut and the line was cut and the station was going to be taken down and I had no one to tell him. I had no one to tell him his name. The station asked for a name that night, and I gave it mine, and I have been its answer ever since."

Maren looked at her mother. Her mother looked at her. And Maren, who had been an archivist for six years and had believed in the reliability of paper and the trustworthiness of the past, understood that the past was not a thing you kept. The past was a thing you *paid* for. You paid with your name, or with your time, or with the people you loved, and the past gave you what it was given, and it did not give you anything more.

"I can take your name," Maren said.

Her mother's face changed. "You do not have to."

"I know I don't have to. I'm doing it anyway."

Her mother reached across the glass and put her hand over Maren's hand, and the hand was cold, and the hand was warm, and the hand was the hand of a woman who had been a stationmaster for twenty-two years and who had given her name to a station that did not exist and who had waited eleven years for her daughter to come and take it back. "Say the name," her mother said.

Maren said the name.

The station went dark.

The light in the window went out, and the figure in the window disappeared, and the platform went quiet, and the dark came in, and the dark was not empty but it was not a landscape either, and Maren stood at the edge of the platform and she felt the train behind her, and the train was there, and the train was not going anywhere, and the train was the only thing in the dark.

She got back on the train.

The doors closed. The lights came on. The old man was still standing at the far end of the carriage with his hands behind his back, and he was looking at her, and his face was not a smile and it was not a frown, and it was the face of a man who had been waiting for a very long time.

"The station is closed," he said.

"I know."

"It will open again in a hundred years. Or in a thousand. Or never. That is not a thing that can be decided. That is a thing that happens."

Maren sat down by the window and she looked out, and the dark was there, and the dark was not a landscape, and the dark was not empty, and the dark was the colour of a room at night when the lamp is on and the window is open and the air is cold and the world is very far away. She put the token in her pocket and she felt its edge against her palm, and she thought about the paper in the archive, and the reliability of paper, and the trustworthiness of the past, and she thought that she had been wrong, and that the past was not a thing you kept, and that the past was a thing you paid for, and that the price was a name, and that the name was her mother's, and that her mother was free.

The train stopped.

The doors opened. The station was there, and the station was the central station, and the station was the same as it had been an hour ago, with the high ceiling and the painted beams and the smell of wet wool and coffee gone bad. The platform was empty, and the platform was not empty, and the platform was the platform of the 11:47, and the 11:47 was the last train of the night, and the night was over, and the night was going to be morning, and the morning was going to be a day, and the day was going to be a life, and the life was going to be long, and the life was going to be hers.

Maren got off the train. She walked to the ticket office and she put the token on the counter, and the ticket clerk looked at it and put it back in the drawer, and she did not ask where it had come from, and she did not ask where it was going, and she did not ask anything at all, because some things are not things that are asked about, and some things are things that are just left in a drawer for a long time, and some things are things that are waited for.

She went home. She went to the attic. She took the token from the drawer and she put it in the coat, and she hung the coat on the back of the door, and she went to sleep, and she did not dream. She did not dream because she had been asleep for eleven years, and she was going to sleep for a long time, and she was going to wake up, and she was going to be old, and she was going to be a stationmaster, and she was going to give her name to a station that did not exist, and she was going to wait, and she was going to be the answer, and she was going to be tired, and she was going to be free.

The token was in the coat. The coat was in the attic. The attic was in the house. The house was in the city. The city was in the dark. And the dark was waiting.

The station asked for a name.

The station was patient.

The station had time.
