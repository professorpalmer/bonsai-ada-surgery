## final text

The letters began to arrive in November, when the gulls had already learned to hate the light. Each one came sealed in a glass bottle, the cork black with salt, the paper thin as a wing membrane. Inside, a single line of blue ink: My name is Lila Vane. I am not yet born.

Aldous Wren read the line at the kitchen table, where the lamp made a circle of gold on the bread. He had been keeper of Gull’s Point Light for nine years, and before that he had been a man who measured his days by the turning of a beam and the filling of a kettle. He knew every island for two miles, every ship’s name that had passed his window. He did not know a Lila Vane.

Outside, the sea was doing its usual work against the rocks, and somewhere beyond the headland a trawler’s horn answered the fog. Aldous turned the paper over. There was no postmark, no address, no signature except the line above, written again in smaller letters. I will write when I can. He set the bottle by the door. That night, as he climbed the spiral stairs to the lamp room, he felt the old loneliness lift and press, as though something small had opened an eye in the dark.

The next bottle came four days later. It was heavier, and the cork had been replaced by a scrap of green cloth. The letter inside said, I am in the water now, or in the space the water makes when it is cold. I know what you keep: three jars of lamp oil, a tin of tea gone soft at the edges, a photograph of your sister that you do not look at unless the rain is long. I know the crack in the west step. I know you whistle the old hymn when the beam turns slow, even though the words are wrong and the tune has no name.

Aldous read this standing in the lamp room, the great lens breathing above him. The second paragraph went on to ask whether he had repaired the door hinge. He had. It had been repaired on a Tuesday, with a screwdriver and a curse that had no place in a prayer. He had not told anyone. The third paragraph said, The girl on the island who bakes bread for the fishermen? Her name is Marta. She is not kind, but she is patient, and she will be glad if you tell her the light has been mended.

He did not tell her. He put the letter in his coat pocket and walked to the kitchen. The bread was warm from the oven, though he had not baked it. On the table, where no one had been, a small child’s drawing lay flat: a circle for a face, two slits for eyes, and a long white line beneath it, like a beam cutting across a black page. Aldous stared at the drawing until his tea cooled. For the first time in nine years, he thought that the light was not only a warning to ships, but a promise made to someone not yet in the world.

The letters kept coming, though not often. Sometimes a week passed before another bottle was hauled up from the rocks, and sometimes two arrived in one morning, as if the sea had been impatient. Lila’s handwriting changed from letter to letter. In one it was neat and childlike, with loops that slanted upward like waves. In another it was sharp, adult, almost hurried, as if the writer had been interrupted by a sound she did not name. In a third it was barely legible, the letters smudged as if written by someone whose hands were wet and trembling.

She told him about a ship called the Cressida. She said it would come through the narrow channel on the night of the great storm in December, and that its engine would fail at the point where the rocks show their teeth to the sea. She said there would be a woman aboard who had lost her first child in a winter that no one else remembered. She said that woman would carry another life inside her, a life that had not yet been given a face. She said, If you do not sound the fog horn when the Cressida enters the channel, I will not come. If you do sound it, I will come, though I am still only a name and a shadow in a future that has not learned my name.

Aldous did not believe her at first. He was a practical man, and the light required no miracles. But the storm came, and it came sooner than the fishermen’s calendars promised. The wind rose out of the north like a fist, and the sea climbed the rocks with such fury that the lamps in the keeper’s cottage guttered and went out one by one. Aldous climbed to the lamp room, where the great lens still turned, and he began to write down what he heard.

At first it was only the storm. Then, far out beyond the channel, he heard a horn, weak and broken, like a man trying to speak after a long silence. He ran for the fog horn, but the engine was dead, choked with salt and cold. He struck it with a wrench. He struck it again. It gave only a thin, dying moan. In the darkness, he heard the sound of the Cressida’s hull scraping against stone, or perhaps he imagined it. The light swept over the sea and found no answer.

Lila’s voice, which he had never actually heard, seemed to come from the glass of the lens. Sound the horn, she said. Not because it will save them, though it may. But because you will have done it. Because the act will make a place in the world large enough for me to enter. Aldous stood there, his hand on the wrench, and felt the old fear that had lived in him since his sister’s death: the fear that nothing he did was enough, and that nothing he did mattered at all.

He thought of the drawing on the table. He thought of the photograph he did not look at. He thought of the years he had spent keeping the light for ships who might never return, for fishermen whose wives had died before he arrived, for a town that had begun to forget that the island needed a keeper. He thought of Lila Vane, who was not yet born, and who had asked only that he make a sound in the storm.

He struck the horn.

The first blow was small. The second was weaker. The third came up as a long, shuddering note, raw and terrible, and it rolled out across the water and the rocks and the dark. Somewhere beyond the channel, the Cressida answered with a horn of its own. Aldous kept striking, and kept striking, until his shoulder was torn with pain and the horn seemed to belong to the sea.

Dawn came gray and thin. The storm had passed, leaving the world washed and strange. A rescue boat came in with the Cressida’s crew, and with them a woman wrapped in a blanket, her eyes closed, her hand resting on her belly. She did not speak. She only nodded to Aldous, once, as if recognizing him from a distance he could not cross.

In the village, they said the Cressida had been saved by the old fog horn, though no one could explain why it had worked after all those years. Marta brought bread to the keeper’s cottage and set it on the table without a word. Aldous ate a little, but it tasted like ash and salt.

That night, he found another bottle on the rocks. This time there was no letter inside. Only a single word, written in blue ink so dark it looked almost black: Thank you.

He held the bottle to the window and watched the light turn. The sea was calm, but the air still smelled of rain. He thought of Lila, who had not yet come into the world, and he wondered what it would be like to know someone before knowing them. He wondered whether the future could send letters back to the present, the way the sea sends shells to the shore. He wondered whether a person who did not yet exist could be waiting for him, as patiently as a ship in a harbor.

He did not write back. He had no way to do so, and no address to which a letter could go. Instead, he cleaned the lens, trimmed the lamp, and checked the oil. He polished the small brass fittings until they shone. He repaired the crack in the west step, not because it was dangerous, but because Lila had known it was there, and he wanted, if only for a little while, to be worthy of the knowledge.

When winter deepened, the fishermen began to tell stories of a girl who had been born on the island, though no midwife remembered her coming into the world. They said she had arrived during a storm that no one else remembered. They said that when the light turned, she would open her eyes, though she had never seen the sun. They said her name was Lila.

Aldous did not go to the village to hear more. He stayed at the light, where the sea and the rocks and the turning beam made a life simple enough to bear. But every morning he looked for a bottle on the rocks, and every evening he set an extra cup out in case the future had learned to come home.

Sometimes, in the small hours, when the lamp was quiet and the house was still, he thought he could hear a voice not quite a voice, as if someone were learning to speak in a place where sound had not yet been allowed to exist. It did not say anything. It only waited.

And Aldous Wren, keeper of Gull’s Point Light, kept the light burning.
