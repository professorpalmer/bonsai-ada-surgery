Mira looked. The sea was wrong. Not the color—the mist sea was always the color of a bruise in morning light. She meant the water line. Where the shell met the water, the waves used to break in a white curtain that stretched three hundred steps to either side of the city. Today the waves curled and died short. The water sat back, as if something out beyond the mist was holding its breath.

"That's not how it does," she said.

"No," Old Fen said. "It is not." He was not looking at her. He was looking at the water with the face of a man who had heard something he didn't want to believe.

Mira had grown up knowing the stories. The elders said the turtle was the last of a kind, older than the kingdoms below the mist, older than the names people still used for the stars. Its name, if it had one, had been swallowed by the sea. Its shell had become the island where the city's founders had landed three hundred years ago, fleeing a war no living person remembered. They had nailed the first chapel to the shell, and the second chapel, and the third, and slowly, the whole city had grown out of the living hide, until the houses were like barnacles and the streets were like the ridges of the shell's ancient patterns.

People were born here, grew up, and died. The turtle walked, but it walked slowly—a mile in a day, they said, and only when the mist thinned. Children played in the shell's crevices without ever knowing they were playing on something that could, if it chose, sink an entire continent.

But this morning, the water line had changed.

Mira went down the spiral steps to the street, and the city was already talking. The market stalls had opened, and the smell of roasting kelp and honeybread hung in the cold air, but no one was buying. Merchants stood by their carts, watching the horizon. A fisherman had tied his boat to the dock and was staring at the water the way people stare at a thing they suspect is staring back.

"Mist-thin," a woman said. "The old ones said that means the turtle has seen something it remembers."

"Who is the old one?" someone asked.

"Does it matter? What matters is what the sea is doing."

Mira found her mother in the bakery on Bell Street. Her mother was kneading dough, and her hands were steady, which was the worst kind of quiet.

"Mother?"

"Your father would be angry," her mother said, not looking up. "He always said the sea changes first."

"Mother."

Her mother set the dough down and washed her hands at the basin. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and her voice was flat, as if she had already rehearsed what she was going to say.

"Nothing," her mother said. "They've ordered everything."

"What did they order me to do?"

Her mother looked at her for a long moment, and something in Mira's chest tightened.

"Nothing," her mother said. "They've ordered nothing. That's the problem."

Outside, the harbor bell rang. Not the breath-bell—the other one. The deep one, the one that used to ring only once a year, on the day the mist was thinnest. Mira ran to the window, and below, on the quay, people were already moving, running, not to the shelters but to the high places, to the shell's rim, to the eastern edge where the sea fell away.

On the eastern platform, Old Fen was still there, and this time he was not leaning on his staff. He was looking up, not at the sea but at the sky, and his mouth was moving without sound.

Mira ran to him. "Fen. What?"

He turned, and his glass eye caught the light. "The mist is not thinning, child. It is being lifted."

Far beyond the city's edge, beyond the waves that had died short, something was rising out of the sea. Not a wave. Not a ship. A shape, vast as the horizon, moving with the slow patience of something that had forgotten what urgency was.

The shape did not rise all at once. It rose the way the morning rises—not in a moment, but in degrees, until what had been a smudge on the horizon became an outline, and what had been an outline became a mass so large that the mind kept sliding off it, trying again, trying again, never holding.

The harbor bell rang a second time.

And deep beneath the cobblestones, beneath the houses and the churches and the roots of the street, the thing beneath the city—the thing they called the Shell, the thing they had built their lives on, the thing they had named nothing because it was older than naming—turned its head.

The city did not shake.

It groaned.
