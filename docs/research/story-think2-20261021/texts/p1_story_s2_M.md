## final text

Elm Harbor had been a place for ships that were leaving, and for men with no reason to stay. In October, the gulf wind rose from the north like a long complaint, and the lighthouse at Black Cap turned its beam over water dark as iron. Elias Wren climbed the hundred and forty stairs nightly, lighting the lamp though now only a ceremony. He still polished the brass, oiled the gears, and checked the logbook. Beneath oil drums and spare wicks, there was a letter that did not belong to anyone who lived. It had arrived again three days earlier.

He opened it on the kitchen table, under a lamp that gave off the smell of beeswax and old paper. The ink was blue, fresh and confident, and the handwriting slanted forward as if the writer had been in a hurry to become real. The letter was addressed to him in pencil, in the same careful hand. It began with a warning about a storm. It ended with a name he did not recognize: Celia Wren. There was no return address, only a drawing of a lighthouse with a door in the tower and a girl standing on the gallery.

His wife had died twenty years ago, taking with her the only child he had ever known. Celia was not a word he had spoken since he was a boy. He put the letter on a shelf by the door, where wind could not reach it, and tried not to think that someone had written a girl into the world who had not yet been born. The storm the letter warned of was due in four days. The bureau had said only a chop. Elias believed the sky first. He believed the sea, and after that he believed the letter.

On the third morning, a boat brought a parcel from the mainland. The postmark was from Black Cap itself, a place where no post office had opened for forty years. Inside was a second letter. This one was not blue but red, and the ink smelled of salt. It told him that Celia would be born on the winter solstice, in a house that did not exist yet, and that she would spend her first year listening to the tower. It asked him to keep the lamp burning. It asked him to leave the small door unlocked for her still.

Elias did not believe in prophecy, but he had lived long enough to know that the sea could be a kind of oracle. Every winter, the fog came in at night and swallowed the rocks. Every spring, the gulls returned to the same broken branch in the pine tree. If a letter could arrive before its writer, maybe the world was not a straight line but a circle, and the tower stood where all the circles touched. He read the red letter again. He read it until the words began to move like small fish in a deep blue room.

The next letter came with no boat. It was wedged in the doorframe, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a twist of copper wire. The paper was soft, as if it had been handled by many hands. It spoke of a child named Celia who liked to climb the tower and count the steps, though she never knew there was a hundred and forty. It spoke of a lighthouse that would be automated in ten years and would then be a museum, a ruin, a story. It spoke of the storm as if it had already happened and been forgiven.

He should have thrown the letters away. Instead, he made tea and read them to the lamp, as if the light might answer. The storm came two days late, as Celia's red letter had predicted, with a wind that struck the tower like a fist and made the glass sing. Elias kept the lamp burning. He did not sleep. He walked the gallery in the rain and called out over the sound of the gale, though no one could hear him and he could not say what he was calling for: a child who was not yet born to him.

After the storm, the sea lay flat and silver under a sky scrubbed clean. A small boat appeared at the base of the rocks. It carried a woman with a coat the color of winter and a child asleep in her arms. The child had black hair and a calm face that belonged to sleep. The woman looked at the tower, then at Elias, and said, "She says you kept the door unlocked." Elias felt the floor tilt. "What do you mean?" he asked. The woman smiled, though her eyes were very old. The child opened one eye, so softly.

Elias looked at the child and understood that the letters had not come from the future. They had come from a room inside the future that had not yet been built, a place where time stood still and waited for a door. The woman introduced herself as Mirren, the keeper of the house that would be raised on the hill in ten years. She said Celia had been born on the winter solstice, in a room that faced the sea, with a storm still moving beyond the glass. The child looked at Elias and said nothing to him that day.

Mirren told him that Celia would spend her life asking questions about the tower. She would ask why the light was never dark, why the keeper had no name in the records after 1940, and why the sea sometimes carried letters to a man who had already died. Elias died, in that story, at the end of a long winter, with snow on his eyebrows and one blue letter still in his pocket. Mirren said he would not mind. She said he would prefer the storm to the silence. Elias did not know whether to be afraid or grateful then.

He took them down to the gallery, where the wind could read them if it wished. The letters were still in his pocket. He could feel their edges against his coat like small cold teeth. Below, Mirren tied the boat to the stone ring, and Celia lifted her face to the sky. The child had no fear of the sea, which seemed impossible, because the sea was the thing that had eaten every promise before Elias could reach it. He thought of his wife, and of the room where a daughter might have learned to whistle and live on always.

Later, Mirren brought him a bottle of brandy and a map of the hill. She said the house would be built with white walls and a blue door, because Celia would choose both colors. She said the lighthouse would be sold to a museum, but Mirren would keep the small room above the gallery for letters that arrived before their writers. Elias drank the brandy slowly. He thought of Celia's letter, the one that had warned him of a storm, and he asked, "Will she know I kept the door unlocked for her?" Mirren nodded, and the wind answered first.

Elias spent the winter writing letters of his own. He wrote to Celia before she existed, describing the tower, the smell of salt, the way the light passed over his face when he stood on the gallery. He wrote of his wife, of the empty room, of the years he had kept a flame for no one. He signed them with a name he had not used since childhood: C. Wren. He did not know where to send them, so he tied them to the lantern frame and left them to the gale. The next morning, one was gone again.

Spring came slowly, as if the sea were deciding whether to forgive the rocks. The tower was sold by a government that had forgotten Elias's name, but Mirren came on a morning of pale sun to take the letters from the room above the gallery. She had collected them all, folded into a blue book with a silver clasp. Celia stood at the door, older now, with a face that held Elias's stubborn jaw. She did not smile, but she touched his hand for a second, and the touch was the only proof he would need. Celia was real now.

After she left, Elias climbed the tower alone for one last night. The lamp was new, automatic and bright, but he lit a small candle beside it, just to keep a place where his hands could rest. On the gallery, the wind brought a sound like a child's voice calling a name that was not yet spoken. He opened the door, as every keeper should, and stepped into the light, where the sea and the future were the same dark water, and the letters were no longer lost. Celia stood in the beam, waiting for her father's light at last.
