# Chapter One: The Last Switch

The fog in Cull’s Hollow never arrived all at once. It came off the Black Pine ridge in slow gray fingers, first over the roofs of the upper houses, then over the fence lines, then into the street where the old depot stood like a tooth in a jaw of mountain.

Arthur Bell stood at the window of his front parlor and watched it swallow the railroad tracks.

For eleven years the line had been closed, but the rails still ran, rusted and weed-choked, through the town and under the mountain tunnel. Arthur had walked that route every day for thirty-one years, first as a helper, then as a conductor, then as the man who knew where every switch was buried under snow or silence. He had retired at the depot, handed his lamp to a boy who did not know which way the track curved, and told him the mountain would tell you if you listened.

Now Arthur was a private detective in name only. The town had too few problems for a real office and too many for a phone. He took cases when they came: a missing goat, a disputed fence line, a widow asking whether her husband’s last letter had been in the mail. His desk was a folding table in the back room; his file cabinet was a tin biscuit tin; his badge was a brass token he had carried as conductor, polished until it shone like a small moon.

It was on a Tuesday, with the fog at its thickest, that the mountain spoke.

He heard the siren before he saw the cars. It was not the long, mournful tone of a fire truck. It was the short, ugly bark of police lights cutting through the gray. Two cruisers came up the main road, their blue lights stuttering in the mist like nervous birds. They stopped in front of the old station, where the ticket window had been boarded for a decade and the platform still sagged toward the tracks.

Arthur buttoned his coat. He did not know why. He had not been called to a death in five years, and the last time he had seen a body it had been a man who had fallen into the river behind the sawmill. The river had taken him before the fog could. This was different. The fog had been waiting.

By the time he reached the tracks, the town had already begun to gather. They came out in wool hats and work jackets, some in church clothes still half-buttoned, as if they had been called away from a Sunday that had not happened yet. There were farmers from the upper valley, the postmistress with her keys still in her hand, and old Mr. Pritchard, who had once complained to Arthur about the new crossing signal as though it were a personal insult.

A black sedan sat behind the cruisers, its hood up. A young woman in a field jacket stood beside it, her notebook open. She looked at the crowd the way Arthur had once looked at a switch that did not want to be thrown: directly, and without much hope.

“Mr. Bell?”

He turned.

“You must be the man who used to run the mountain line,” she said.

“I was a conductor,” Arthur said. “Not a line.”

She nodded as if that were a distinction. “I’m Detective Lena Voss. Cull’s Hollow and the county have had a bit of a problem.”

“Problems don’t come down the mountain all by themselves.”

“No,” she said. “They usually have legs.”

She stepped aside and gestured toward the tunnel.

The Widow’s Cut had been the longest tunnel on the old line, carved through the heart of the ridge so that trains could pass through the mountain instead of climbing over it. The tracks inside had gone cold and black in the years after the last freight. The entrance opened into a shallow gully, where the stone arch looked like a mouth half-open after a long scream.

Arthur stopped short of the gully.

He could see the body before the police tape did.

The man lay on his back at the foot of the tunnel, a few feet from the rails, his coat dark with something that was no longer dark. His hands were bound in front of him with wire, and his mouth was closed as if someone had pressed the secret back inside him.

A third police car had already left, its lights still spinning. The town stood silent, the fog pressing down over them until their hats looked like snow.

Arthur walked forward.

Lena Voss followed, but not close enough to touch.

“He was found this morning,” she said. “By the man who owns the timber lot above the tunnel.”

Arthur knelt. Not because the body made him feel like a detective, but because his knees remembered the platform. He had spent more years bending, reaching, and listening to wheels than he had sitting in a chair.

The dead man’s face was gray-blue, but the eyes were closed. A cut ran from his temple to his jaw, clean enough to suggest a blade, dirty enough to suggest a struggle. The wire around his wrists had been twisted tight, leaving the skin raw and red. In his left hand, even now, he clutched a small object.

Lena crouched beside him. “Do you recognize anything?”

Arthur shook his head, then looked down.

The object was a brass conductor’s token, round as a coin, warm from the dead man’s grip. On one side was an old railroad crest, a pine tree beneath a rising sun. On the other, in faded letters, was a number: 417.

Arthur’s breath stopped.

He knew that number.

Every conductor on the line had carried one, a brass token that could be read by the engineer at the engine and by the conductor at the cab. It was part of the chain of authority that kept a train from becoming a murder machine. It said: I am here. I am allowed. I am watching.

His number had been 417.

He had worn it for nineteen years.

“No,” Lena said quietly. “That’s not possible.”

“Why?”

She looked at him. “Because you’re alive.”

Arthur did not answer. He set the token down on the stone, though his fingers were clumsy. The body’s hand fell open a little, as if it had let go of something that had nothing to do with it.

“Who is this?” Lena asked.

Arthur stood. “I don’t know him from the front.”

“He was a local. Or he was supposed to be. Name’s Victor Hale.”

Hale. The name settled over the tunnel like a cold hand. Victor Hale had owned the timber company up the mountain and had spent the last three years trying to buy out the old rail right-of-way. He had stood in the depot once, before the place had gone quiet, and said that trains were a memory and memories were expensive.

Arthur had told him a train was only as good as the man who could hear the next switch coming.

Hale had laughed.

Now Arthur was laughing inside, though his face did not move.

“Where did they find him?” Lena asked.

“At the mouth of the tunnel. Face down, actually. They turned him over when they came to check his pockets. There was something in his coat pocket too.” She pulled a small envelope from the inside of the field jacket. “They found a note.”

Arthur took the envelope. The paper was soft at the edges, as if it had been carried in a pocket for a long time. He opened it.

Inside was a single train schedule, folded four times, yellow with age. The dates ran from the year the line was still running. Arthur unfolded it carefully.

The last row was circled in red pencil.

October 14. Black Pine and Summit Line. Last scheduled departure.

Arthur looked at the tunnel.

October 14 was the day the last train had left. He had been the conductor. The engineer had been a man named Dorey, who used to whistle a note too high for the fog. The last passenger had been a widow carrying a cat in a basket. They had all gone down into the mountain together, and the mountain had not come out for eleven years.

But the body had only just been found.

“What else is in his pockets?” Lena asked.

Arthur had already pulled from the coat: a wallet, a key ring, a crumpled photograph of a woman with black hair, and a small brass switch key. The key was not from the old line. It was new.

He turned it over. On the handle was stamped a single letter: T.

Lena saw it too. “Third?”

Arthur’s eyes went to the track. “The third track used to branch off here, behind the station. It went to the old freight yard before the town closed it. Nobody remembers it anymore. I remember it.”

“Because you used the line.”

“Because I listened to it.”

A long silence fell between them. The fog moved through the gully in thin ribbons. Somewhere up the mountain, a bird cried once and then stopped.

Lena opened her notebook. “The man who found the body said there were scratch marks in the stone at the entrance. He thought the body had been dragged out.”

Arthur crouched again. “Not dragged out. Dragged in.”

Lena looked at him.

“If you want to know how a man dies,” Arthur said, “you have to know where he was before he died. The body doesn’t lie, but it doesn’t tell the whole truth either. It tells you where he ended. The mountain tells you where he started.”

He pointed to the tunnel arch. “The stone here is worn smooth on the east side. Water runs over it in the winter. If the body had been dragged from the tunnel to the outside, the drag marks would be on the outside stone. They’d be shallow. The scratch marks you saw are inside, just under the arch. That means this man was put in here after he was killed.”

“After?”

Arthur straightened. “Or before he was killed. It depends on which way the killer wanted you to think.”

Lena’s pen stopped moving. “You think this was staged?”

Arthur looked down at the token in his hand. “I think someone wanted a conductor’s number on the victim. And if I had to guess, I’d say they wanted mine.”

The words hung in the fog.

Lena did not laugh. “That puts you where?”

“Exactly where I don’t want to be.”

“Then why are you still standing here?”

Arthur should have answered that. He did not.

Instead, he looked back at the body. The dead man’s mouth had begun to stiffen, and the cut at his temple had stopped being a cut and become a line, like a crack in old ice. There was something else on his face: a smear of black tar under one ear, the kind that came from old brake shoes and engine grease.

Arthur leaned closer.

The tar was not fresh. It had dried to a hard rim.

Lena saw the movement in his shoulders. “What is it?”

“A train,” he said.

“No,” she said. “There’s no train.”

“The last train was eleven years ago.”

“But something ran this tunnel recently.”

Arthur wiped the tar from the stone with his thumb and smelled it. Pine tar, sulfur, and underneath both, the faint sweetness of old oil. “Not a train. A vehicle. Something with rubber tires. They used the tunnel to move him without being seen.”

Lena’s eyes narrowed. “A car can’t fit inside.”

“A truck can.”

“The tunnel is only wide enough for a single rail track.”

Arthur looked at the arch again. “Not if the tunnel’s not all the tunnel. There’s a service cut through the mountain, behind the west wall. I used to go up there with the maintenance men when the water came in. The cut is just wide enough for a small truck.”

Lena wrote it down. “You’re telling me someone drove a truck through a hidden service tunnel, killed a man, put him in the Widow’s Cut, and left a conductor’s token.”

“I’m telling you that’s what the scene looks like.”

“And you recognized the token.”

“I recognized my token.”

Lena closed the notebook. “Then why is it here?”

Arthur did not answer for a moment. The dead man’s hand lay open beside the track. The fog curled over the rails and disappeared.

“I buried it,” he said.

Lena’s head lifted.

“Three years ago. I found it in an old coat in the attic. I hadn’t seen it in years. It looked like something the dead had left behind. I took it out to the yard, dug a hole behind the old water tank, and put it there with a stone on top. I didn’t want anyone to think I was still carrying the line.”

“Then how is it on this man?”

Arthur looked at the token. “That’s the part that makes me not want to go home.”

Lena studied him. “Are you going to call it in?”

“I am calling it in. I’m standing here with you and you’ve got a notebook.”

She almost smiled. “Good. Because I’d rather have a dead man and a tunnel than a retired conductor and a confession.”

Arthur turned back to the body. “Victor Hale.”

Lena followed his voice. “Yes?”

“Hale wasn’t trying to buy the rail line for timber.”

“How do you know that?”

Arthur looked at the tunnel, at the service cut, at the track that disappeared into the dark. “Because the last man who owned the old freight yard told me, ten years ago, that the line wasn’t worth the iron. It was worth the tunnel.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means the tunnel is not just a tunnel.”

Lena’s pen hovered over the page. “Arthur.”

He held up a hand. “There’s more.”

He turned the brass token over again. On the back, beneath the number 417, there was a mark he had never seen before. A scratch, thin and sharp, running from the edge to the center.

It was not a scratch.

It was a letter, cut into the metal with deliberate care.

A.

Arthur looked up. “Lena.”

“What?”

“He didn’t kill me for the token.”

“No, he didn’t.”

“No. He killed someone who was going to kill me, and he left the token as a warning.”

The fog thickened. The tunnel mouth darkened. Somewhere far up the mountain, the wind found a crack in the ridge and made a sound like a whistle, low and patient.

Lena looked at the sound, then back at Arthur. “You’re saying Victor Hale was protecting you.”

“I’m saying someone put a conductor’s token on a dead man because a conductor’s number was supposed to be found. And if that number was mine, then the message was not for the police.”

“Then for whom?”

Arthur looked at the body one last time. “For whoever is still riding the line.”

The wind blew again, and for a moment the old station groaned as if the mountain had shifted in its sleep.

Lena closed her notebook. “Then we’d best find out who’s riding.”

Arthur picked up the token. The brass was cold now, though it had been warm in the dead man’s hand. He looked at the letter cut into the back.

A.

The first letter of his name. The last letter of his old life. The beginning of something else.

“Take the body to the station,” he said. “Not the morgue. The old waiting room. I want to see where they put it.”

Lena paused. “Why?”

Arthur turned toward the town, where the fog was beginning to thin over the roofs. The people were still there, standing in the street like a row of stones that had not yet decided whether to be a wall.

“Because if this man came from the tunnel,” he said, “then someone left a light on.”
