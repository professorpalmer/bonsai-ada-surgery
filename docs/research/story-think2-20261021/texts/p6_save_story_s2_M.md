The smell of the galley was the first thing Mira learned to love.

Bread.

She took the tomato seeds out. She took the basil. She took the thyme. She took the wheat.

"Thank you," she said.

The smell of the galley was the first thing Mira learned to love.

Before the ship's lights came on, before the deck plates cooled, before the morning bells rang across the Meridian's corridors, the smell came first: warm metal, yeast, and the faint green ghost of hydroponic basil.

She was twenty-seven, but her hands were a cook's hands: calloused at the fingertips, the left palm bearing a burn scar shaped like a comma. Her mother, Elia, had called the scar a comma. In a good recipe, Elia said, a comma is only a pause. You are not supposed to end a sentence there.

"Stove one," the intercom announced, in the same flat voice it had carried for nineteen years. "Stove two."

The galley occupied one end of the central corridor. Its walls were worn by two centuries of steam and flour and the hands of cooks who had never seen the sun. Above the stoves hung a line of hooks, and from one of them a single copper pot, the only thing brought aboard from Earth. No one remembered who had hung it first, or whether it had ever actually been used.

Mira wiped her hands and turned the dial. The Meridian was a generation ship. She had been born aboard it, and her children would be born on a planet with a real sky, provided the navigation team's calculations held and the star they had been chasing was still there. But inside the ship, she was in charge of the one thing that made the thousand and twenty-four souls aboard feel less like cargo.

Bread.

Each morning she worked the dough the way the galley worked it, the way the first cook in the vault had worked it: knead, fold, knead, fold. The recipe was written in the ship's logbook in a hand so careful it might have been printed. Flour, water, salt, a packet of wild yeast, and a pinch of something the author had labeled "time." For two centuries no one could explain the final line. Mira understood it. You cannot measure time in a gram. You can only give it.

At 0900 the alarm came.

"Galley," the engineer's voice crackled through the comm, stripped of its usual courtesy. "Hydroponics bay four. Blight. Spore count climbing. I'm sealing the bay."

Mira stopped in the middle of her kneading. Bay Four was not the largest bay, but it was the bay where the tomatoes grew, the bay where the Deck Nine children pressed their faces to the glass and asked her when they could eat them. Three years ago she had planted the first seedlings there, seeds sealed in the vault since launch.

"I'll have it sealed in an hour," the engineer said. "Containment takes time."

She closed her eyes and tried to breathe. She thought of the tomato plants, the first living thing the Meridian had ever grown from seed, the first green in the ship that had not been recycled from before. She thought of the meal she had promised the Deck Nine children: tomatoes on a warm plate with basil and bread. *Not today*, she thought. *But not never.*

"Mira," a voice said in the doorway. Commander Ilya, tall, with the set jaw of a woman who had been responsible for too long. "The protein vats are compensating, but the crew is asking. They always ask. Tell them what you can."

"I can tell them the bread is still rising."

Ilya looked at the half-shaped loaves and something in her face loosened. "That is more than most cooks on Earth could say."

That night, after the crew had slept, Mira opened the vault.

The vault was a small room behind the ship's original navigation console, built by the first crew to preserve what the launch could not: seeds, recipes, a single volume of poetry, and the logbook of the first cook. The first cook had been a man named Davi. No one knew much about him. He had been a cook in a city Mira had only read about, a city with a name like a word for "warm."

She opened the logbook. The pages were brittle. The handwriting was that same careful hand. She read the entry dated forty-three days after launch, and it went like this:

"The blight came from spores in the tomato soil. It was in the seed. I killed the bay. I am sorry. But I did something I had not done before. I took the seeds from the reserve case and planted them in clean soil. They are growing. Slowly, but they are growing. The crew asked what to eat. I told them: we will eat what we can grow and what we remember. Bread is not just bread. It is a promise that there is a tomorrow to be hungry for. If you are reading this, you are the keeper. Do not let the recipe die. The recipe is the ship."

Mira thought about the word *keeper*. It was a strange word for what it meant. Not a guardian. Not a librarian. A keeper, like a gardener who knows the names of the seeds and the names of the days. She looked at the logbook again and saw that the last page was blank. In the corner of the page, in that careful hand, a single line: *I am starting it again.*

Mira sat in the dark and read it again and again.

She went to the reserve case. It stood in the far corner of the vault behind a panel no one had opened in two centuries. The seeds were still there, in small glass vials labeled in that careful hand. Tomato. Basil. Thyme. Wheat. Peas. Peppers. Herbs with names she knew. Herbs with names she did not.

She took the tomato seeds. The basil. The thyme. The wheat.

In the morning she told Commander Ilya that the blight was contained. She told her backup strains had been planted in clean soil in Bay Four and would take root within the week. She did not tell her about the logbook. She did not tell her the first cook had been afraid. She did not tell her that the tomato seeds in the reserve case had been sealed away by the same hands that wrote the promise.

On Deck Nine the children pressed their faces to the glass and watched the new seedlings. Mira passed, knelt, and put a hand on a girl's shoulder. "They're growing," she said.

The girl looked at her. "Like the bread?"

Mira smiled. "Like the bread."

That night she baked a loaf. Knead, fold, knead, fold. She thought of the comma on her palm and the pause it was and the sentence it had not yet finished. She thought of Davi, planting in the dark and calling it a promise. She thought of a thousand and twenty-four people sleeping in their bunks, dreaming of a sky they had never seen.

The loaf was not perfect. The crust was a shade too dark, the crumb a little tight. But it was alive.

The mess hall was the largest room aboard. By the time Mira set the table, the first of them were gathering: an engineer with dirt under his boots, a child from Deck Nine with flour on his nose, a doctor who had been aboard for twenty years and who knew, Mira thought, the tomatoes were still small. They sat. They watched the table.

She set out the loaf with the first of the reserve harvest—tomatoes, still small, still bright—and a dish of basil oil and a bowl of thyme. The crew ate. They ate in silence first, and then, one by one, they began to speak. They spoke about taste. They spoke of their mothers and fathers and grandmothers. They spoke of a world that was not here. But they were here, the bread was warm, the tomatoes were red, and the basil was green, and that was enough.

Ilya sat at the end of the table. When she finished, she stood and paused at the door.

"Thank you," she said.

Mira looked up from her plate.

"For the bread," Ilya said. "And for the rest. For not letting us forget what we are."

Mira looked at the half-eaten loaf and the crumbs and the green leaves on the table. She thought of the vault and the logbook and the first cook who had been afraid and planted anyway. She thought of the week until the tomatoes returned. She thought of the sky they would one day see.

She wiped her hands on her apron and picked up the last crust and ate it. It was not perfect. It was alive.

The story of the Meridian would go on. The seeds would grow. The bread would rise. The comma in Mira's palm would remain a comma, and the sentence it belonged to would be written on by every hand that touched the dough, every hand that folded it, every hand that opened the door to the next morning and said: *Stove one. Stove two.*
