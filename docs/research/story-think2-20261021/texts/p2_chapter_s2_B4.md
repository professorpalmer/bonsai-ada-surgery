# Chapter One: The Last Schedule

The mountain woke before the town did.

Elias Marlowe heard it first in the tick of the water clock above the kitchen sink, then in the long low groan of ice loosening on the rafters of the Depot House. At sixty-nine, he rose before the sun and before his knees complained, which was more than he could say for the town of Pinehaven. It stirred when it needed to be stirred, and otherwise it lay in the blue shadow of the peaks, holding its breath.

He lived above the old station where, for forty-one years, he had counted passengers, checked tickets, and stood on the platform while the Mountain Flyer slipped away into the pines. Now the rail line was a ghost of gravel and rusted ties, and the station was a shop of clocks, lanterns, and railroad memorabilia. Elias sold none of them to himself; he kept the old timetable above the register, the 1998 schedule, because even after the last train, he still believed the mountain obeyed the hour.

The house smelled of beeswax and cold coffee. On the table lay a small brass key, the key to the conductor’s office. It had not opened anything in eleven years. He polished it anyway, the way a man polishes a tool he expects to use. Outside, fog lay in the valley floor, thick as wool, and the single streetlight at the depot flickered like a candle in a draft.

At 6:47, he was pouring coffee when the siren cut through the valley. Not the old locomotive whistle, but the sheriff’s engine, thin and bright. Elias set down the mug. He knew that siren the way a sailor knows a change in wind: it meant something had broken. He did not put on a coat. He knew it would not matter.

Deputy Nora Hale came up the steps with frost on her boots and her face the color of weak tea.

“Mr. Marlowe,” she said. “Someone’s dead on the old line.”

Elias looked past her, out over the depot yard. A black county car sat at an angle near the fence. Two men were already walking toward the rail bed. “Black Pine Cut?” he asked.

“Near the tunnel portal.” Nora’s voice dropped, though there was no one to overhear. “A ski patrol member found her in the early run. She’s not a local. I know the name, though. Clara Voss. New teacher at the school. Twenty-four.”

Elias had heard the name. Clara Voss. The young woman who had taken over the middle school science class, who had smiled too brightly at the harvest supper and asked his daughter about the old railroad. His daughter had moved to Seattle two winters ago, leaving the house to Elias and a box of photographs he had not opened. He had liked Clara. Teachers, in small towns, were often the ones who listened before they asked questions.

He followed Nora to the old rail line.

The November light came sideways through the fog, thin as a blade. The mountain stood black and patient above the cut, its face split by the mouth of a tunnel that had once swallowed the Mountain Flyer on its way to the high country. The tunnel was the oldest part of the line, a Romanesque arch of stone and iron, built in 1889, when the railroad men had believed Pinehaven could be a place where the future came through. Now the arch was boarded and chained, a black square set in the mountain. Beyond it, the tunnel was a throat closed for years.

Clara Voss lay on the old rail bed, not far from the platform where the conductor had once stood with his token in his hand. She wore a blue flannel shirt, a dark skirt, and hiking boots that were too clean for a person who had fallen. The snow around her had been disturbed, but not as if by a struggle. It was as if someone had laid her there after the wind had stopped and before the town had woken.

The county lab tech, a heavyset man named Dale, was kneeling near her. “No obvious defensive injuries,” he said to Nora. “Could be blunt trauma, but we’ll know more later. The watch stopped at 4:03.”

Elias crouched, ignoring the cold that moved through his hip. He looked at Clara’s face. Her eyes were closed. Her mouth was slightly open, as if she had been trying to remember the time of something. Her fingers were curled, but not tightly. They held two things.

A brass token.

And a folded page of paper.

“Don’t touch it,” Nora said.

Elias did not move. “Who found her?”

“Ski patrol. A kid. Said she was facing the tunnel and looked like she’d been posing.”

Elias looked at the tunnel. “She wasn’t posing.”

The token in her hand was the kind he had carried every day for four decades: an oval of brass, stamped with the Pinehaven Mountain Railroad crest, a pine tree over a wheel and a rail. Most conductors wore theirs on a cord around the neck, or kept them in the same pocket as the ticket punch. This one was worn smooth, the edges rounded by years of handling, as if it had belonged to someone who had carried it not just as a badge, but as a habit.

On the edge of the brass was a number: 1998.

Elias knew that number. Every year the Mountain Flyer ran, the conductors received new tokens in the spring. The number on the face of the token was not a serial number. It was the year of issue. 1998 was the last year the train ran.

“The paper,” Nora said. “What does it say?”

Elias did not touch it, but he turned it slightly in the air, careful, so the lab tech could photograph it. It was a page from the old timetable, yellowed at the edges, torn from a book. He recognized the type. The 1998 Mountain Flyer schedule.

The 9:15 a.m. train had been crossed out in red marker.

Below it, in handwriting that was not his own, were three words:

She knows.

Nora looked at him. “Who wrote that?”

Elias did not answer. He was thinking of the watch. 4:03. On the old schedule, there had been no train at 4:03. The Mountain Flyer’s last departure from Pinehaven every evening was 6:58, and its first departure in the morning was 6:12. But in 1998, during the winter storm season, there had been an extra service, a special run up to the summit camp, used only by the company. It left Pinehaven at 4:03 and did not stop until the high pass. The company called it the Night Schedule. No passenger had ever seen it. The town called it the ghost train.

Clara Voss had been teaching for three weeks. She had not been on that train. She had not been born in the year the train ran.

Nora crouched beside him. “Do you know that schedule?”

“Better than most people,” Elias said.

“Then what does it mean?”

For a moment, he thought of his hands on the conductor’s rail, the click of the ticket punch, the way a train could vanish into a tunnel and the town would go on as if it had never been there. He thought of the last time he had seen the tunnel mouth, eleven years ago, when the county had finally nailed the boards across it after the landslide. The nails had been fresh. The boards had smelled of pine and machine oil.

“It means someone wanted her to be seen,” he said at last. “But not seen as a teacher.”

“What else?”

“As a conductor.”

Nora frowned. “What do you mean?”

Elias pointed to Clara’s hands. “Look at her fingers. The way they’re bent. She isn’t holding the token naturally. She’s holding it the way a conductor held the last train’s token when he had to signal the engineer at the cut. Thumb over the top edge, two fingers across the face.”

Nora stared at the dead woman’s hand.

Elias kept staring. “And the mud.”

“What about it?”

“It’s on the inside of her boots. Not the snow, not the gravel. Mud.”

Dale looked up. “We got some here. From the cut.”

Elias shook his head. “Not this mud. It’s darker. It’s the kind you find at the base of the tunnel where the water comes down from the rock. The kind that gets into the rails only when the spring thaw runs the tunnel dry.”

The wind moved through the pines and sent a small cloud of frost across the old platform.

Nora looked at the tunnel. “You’re saying she was in there.”

“I’m saying someone put her here with something from there.”

“What about the note?”

Elias looked at the crossed-out schedule one more time. “That’s not a note. That’s a message.”

“From whom?”

He did not know. He was beginning to understand why he had not opened the box of photographs his daughter had left behind. Some doors, once closed, kept their own schedules.

A third county car pulled into the yard. Sheriff Tom Reed stepped out, a man with a face like worn leather and eyes that had seen too many small-town problems for the comfort of strangers. He walked toward the body without looking at Elias.

“Who’s this?” Reed asked Nora.

“Mr. Marlowe. Retired conductor. Lives above the station.”

Reed stopped. “You know something?”

“I know where the tunnel ends,” Elias said. “I don’t know what’s in it.”

Reed looked at him for a long moment. “That’s not an answer.”

“No,” Elias said. “It’s a beginning.”

From inside the tunnel, something moved.

At first Elias thought it was only the wind catching the chain on the boarded portal. Then he heard it again: a short, dry scrape, like a boot heel dragging over stone.

Nora’s hand went to her radio.

Elias did not move. He was not looking at the tunnel. He was looking at Clara’s face, at the faint smear of dark mud near her left cheekbone. It was not blood. It was the same color as the mud inside the tunnel, black and wet and smelling faintly of iron.

Someone had touched her face after she was dead.

And the person who did it had been in the tunnel.
