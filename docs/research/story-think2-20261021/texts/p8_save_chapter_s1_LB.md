# Chapter One: The Tide Below the City

No one in Veranthos looked down.

That was the first rule the children learned before they learned to read: *the sky is up, the streets are here, and there is nothing worth seeing below the rim.* The second rule was the same rule with a different edge—never, ever step on the mossy stone at the western wall, because the moss was warm, and warm moss meant the beast was breathing.

Mara knew this. She knew it the way she knew the taste of salt in the morning air and the creak of the great anchor-ropes that swayed from the harbor towers when the deep water passed beneath the shell. Veranthos hung on the back of something ancient, and the city had learned to be still.

The tide came at dawn, as it always did.

It rose first as a sound. A low, rolling moan that started somewhere far below the streets and climbed up through the foundations of the houses, through the soles of the sleeping, through the floorboards of the watchtowers. The sailors in the harbor lowered their lanterns and stood at the rail, watching the water curl and stack itself against the great curved lip of the shell. The tide here was not a tide as the lowland townsfolk imagined it. It did not retreat and return in a gentle rhythm. It surged, and it swallowed the horizon, and when it receded it left behind a foam that glowed faintly green, as though the sea remembered something it had swallowed a long time ago.

Mara stood at the window of the rope-smith's shop with her back to the door and her hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago. She was twenty, thin, with ink stains under her fingernails from the maps she drew for the harbor master and a scar across her jaw that no one had the courage to ask about. She had not slept in two days.

That was because of the scratching.

It had begun three nights past, deep in the walls of the old quarter. Not rats—rats were small and quick and scurried in straight lines. This was different. This was slow, patient, and it moved in circles, like a compass needle seeking north. And it came from below the foundation stones, which meant it came from the shell itself.

The door opened, and old Fen walked in without knocking. Fen was the city's memory, or so everyone said, though he had been the harbor master's clerk for forty years and mostly kept a record of how many barrels of smoked eel the trade ships brought in. He carried a lantern under his arm and his eyes were bright and watery in the way that meant he had seen something and was waiting to see if anyone else had noticed.

"You heard it," he said. It was not a question.

"I heard it."

"Three nights. Every dawn. Like a fingernail on a bell." He set the lantern on her workbench and wiped his hands on his coat. "The old stories say the Beast wakes only once a century. The scholars in the High Quarter have their own theories—thermal expansion, they call it. Stone moving as the water shifts. But stone does not scratch, Mara. Stone does not make the whole city hum."

She looked at him. "What do you think it is?"

Fen smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "I think," he said, "that the city is built on the back of a living thing, and something has begun to think back."

The tea in her cup trembled, though there had been no wind at all.

Outside, the tide was beginning to recede. The green foam crept back over the rocks, and the great curves of the shell glistened in the morning light, vast and brown and ancient, and the people of Veranthos began their day as they always had: hanging laundry, sweeping the streets, calling out prices at the market, and looking, never once, down.

But Mara did look.

She had begun to look. Every morning since the scratching started, she climbed to the rim at the western wall, where the moss grew thick and warm, and she leaned over the edge and watched the water far below. And for three nights, in the dim green glow, she had seen something moving in the depths. Something with a shape that was almost, but not quite, like a city. Something with lights that pulsed in slow, deliberate rhythms, like breathing.

She had told herself it was the reflection of her own lantern. She had told herself it was the eels in the harbor glinting. She had told herself a great many things, because the truth was a door she could not bear to open.

But Fen had heard it too. And Fen was old, and Fen remembered.

"Do you remember the First Founders," he asked, and his voice was very quiet now. "Do you remember the story the priests tell?"

Mara shook her head.

"They did not build a city on the back of a turtle, Mara. They found a turtle, already dying, already sinking into the deep. They built on its back because it was the last dry ground left in the world, and they swore an oath to keep it alive. To feed it, to care for it, to never let it remember what it had been before it was a city."

The scratching came again, slow and deep, and the floorboards of the shop groaned.

"And that oath," Fen went on, "has been kept for three hundred years. Until now."

Mara picked up her map—her own drawing of the harbor, with all the small, careful lines of the docks and the ropes and the lantern posts—and she looked at it for a long moment. Then she folded it carefully and put it in her coat.

She went outside, where the green foam was retreating and the air smelled of salt and iron and something else, something old and warm, like the breath of the beast itself.

Somewhere far below the rim, far below the mossy stones and the anchor-ropes and the cold tea going cold in the shop behind her, the scratching stopped.

And, very softly, from the deep, something answered.
