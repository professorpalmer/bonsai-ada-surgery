For the first three years, Tomas Wren told himself the light was for ships.

He told it the way you tell a child a story you half-believe, in the flat, reassuring tone of a thing that is not quite true. *Yes, the light is for ships. Yes, the sea is only water and weather. The island was called Kestrel Rock, and it had not been part of any nation for longer than Tomas could remember. A few hundred steps from the shore to the keeper's cottage. A cottage of black stone with a door that stuck in the cold. A window that faced the sea and a second that faced the sky. On the highest point of the rock stood the tower, a spiral of glass and iron, and in it burned the light.

Tomas had kept it for fourteen years now. Fourteen years since the storm that took Anais from the fishing boat *Selkie*, four years since... no. Fourteen years since the storm, and the boat, and the sea that had not given her back. He had been a surveyor before that. He had drawn the coastline of this very rock with a compass and a chain, measuring its moods in fathoms. After the drowning, he had done nothing with his hands except wind the clockwork of the lamp, trim the wick, polish the lens, and walk the gallery in the rain.

The ships did not come often. The trade route curved two hundred leagues south, and the only vessels that passed were the occasional trawler, or the small white boat that brought the post every ninth day, when the wind permitted. So the light, most of the time, was a thing without an audience. A voice in an empty room. Tomas had made of it a penance, and the penance had done its work: it had emptied the man and left the shape of the man.

He was old now, or it seemed so. His hair was the color of the lichen on the rock, and his hands were knotted like driftwood, and when the fog came in the mornings and swallowed the horizon, he would stand at the top of the gallery and look out into the nothing and feel, as he had always felt, that the nothing was looking back.

It was into this nothing that Lior came.

The light burned on, and the ships came, and the post came, and the world moved on, and Tomas Wren kept the light, and he did not speak of Anais, not in words, but in the way he spoke to the lens, and in the way he watched the small pale lights that sometimes appeared in the deep, and in the way he kept, in the cellar, a bottle of gold smoke, and a name, and a memory, and a love that had been too much, and had been let go, and had come back, not as it was, but as it needed to be.

And Lior grew up on the island. She learned the tower. She learned the light. She learned the story. And when she was old, she would sit by the lens and speak to it, and the light would listen, and the deep would be still, and the sea would forget, and the light would hold, and the world would go on, and the story would not end, because the light was not a thing that ended. It was a thing that held. And it held. And it held. And it held.
