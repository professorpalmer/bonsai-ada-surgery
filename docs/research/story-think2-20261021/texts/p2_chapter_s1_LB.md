## final text

# Chapter One: The Last Whistle

The fog came up out of the canyon at five forty, the way it always did, sliding in from the east and filling the switchback curves of the old rail line with a slow, white patience. Silas Voss stood on the porch of his house on the third rise of the hill, watching it eat the pines one by one. He had a thermos in one hand and a mug of black coffee in the other. The thermos was for the coffee he wasn't drinking yet, the one that would keep warm for the walk.

He'd been doing that walk for twenty-three years, ever since the day he'd handed his cap to the railroad office in Flagstaff and driven south with a cardboard box of personal effects and a pension check that arrived in the mail like a formality. Twenty-three years of the same route: the porch, the gravel path down the south slope, the gate at the top of the rail line, and then the slow trudge along the ballast to the town. He didn't run the line anymore. He didn't even step foot on the tracks. But his legs remembered the distance, and the distance remembered his legs, and the fog made everything into a gray corridor with no edges, so the walk became the only thing in his life that still had a schedule.

Harrow's Bend sat at the mouth of the canyon, four hundred people on a payroll that was mostly tourism and the occasional logging crew. The rail line had been a narrow-gauge, single track that wound up the mountain for eleven miles to a depot in the pines at Nine-Thousand. They'd killed the passenger service in '91, sold the rolling stock to a heritage railway out west, and kept the tracks for the summer tourists who paid forty-five dollars to stand on a caboose and watch the fog roll off the cliff. The gate Silas walked through each morning was the last one before the town, and it still stood because the summer crew needed a way in.

He finished his coffee on the porch, washed the mug, and stepped inside to put on his boots.

The house was quiet in the way houses get when the people who used to make noise have died or moved on. His wife, Elise, had been gone four years now, and the quiet in the rooms had a shape to it, like a body that had been there a long time and was finally, only now, being acknowledged. He moved through it the way he'd moved through a caboose in his career—efficiently, with purpose, without sentiment. Coffee for the walk. Boots. A jacket. He'd had the same jacket since the day of his last run, a wool thing the color of dried leaves.

He stepped back out into the fog, which by six was so thick he could feel it on his face, damp and cold, like breathing through a wet shirt.

He was halfway down the path when he heard the siren.

Not a car. Not a fire truck. The siren on the Sheriff's Deputy cruiser, that old thing they'd bought from the county surplus and kept running with prayer and WD-40, screaming its one broken note up the canyon. It was a sound that, in all his years of living in Harrow's Bend, had only ever meant one thing: someone was dead, or about to be.

Silas slowed his walk. Then he stopped. He stood in the middle of the path, listening.

The siren looped, rising and falling. Then, two minutes later, a second one. Closer.

By seven, Deputy Marsh was at his door.

Silas opened before the knock finished. Marsh was a young man, maybe twenty-eight, with a jaw that looked like it had been carved out of the same granite as the mountain behind it and a face that hadn't yet decided what shape grief was supposed to take. He wasn't wearing his uniform. He was in a flannel shirt and jeans, and his hands were in his pockets, and he was looking at the ground instead of Silas.

"Mr. Voss," he said.

"Marsh."

"I need you to come with me. There's a body. Up at the tunnel."

Silas didn't ask which tunnel. There were only two, and the one at the mouth of the canyon—the big one, the one the old line went straight through, the one with the name carved into the stone—was the one everyone called the Tunnel. The other one was a short cut near the old lumberyard, and nobody walked that way anymore.

"Got a key?" Silas asked.

Marsh looked up. "For what?"

"For the gate at the east end of the Tunnel. The big gate, the one that's been rusted shut since the line closed. You can't get the body out unless you get that gate open. And nobody in town has a key to it that I know of but me."

Marsh nodded once, hard. "Right. That's why I'm here."

They got in the deputy's car, and Marsh drove up the switchback road at a speed that was legal but not kind to the car, the siren still on, still wailing its broken note into the fog. Silas watched the world blur past—pines going dark, then gray, then a wall of white that swallowed everything ahead. He put his hands on the dashboard and held them there, palms flat, as if the car were a train and the dashboard were the edge of the platform, and he was waiting for something to pull in.

The tunnel mouth was a black rectangle in the side of the mountain, and the fog was pouring out of it, a cold stream that made the air in front of the car taste like iron. Marsh parked. They got out.

Three people were already there. Sheriff Boyd, the older man with the gray beard and the eyes that had been watching the town for forty years before Silas ever stepped onto a train. Two EMTs with their yellow jackets and their radio chatter. And, at the base of the tunnel, a body.

It was a woman. Lying on her back on the ballast, arms at her sides, one shoe gone. Her hair was dark, long, tangled with gravel. She was maybe thirty. She had a pack by her head, a small one, the kind a journalist or a researcher might carry. A notebook lay open near her hand.

Silas stopped at the edge of the light and looked.

Then he went back to the gate.

The gate was a heavy steel thing, four feet wide, with a padlock on the chain. He pulled his keys from his jacket pocket. The key was old, brass, worn smooth by three decades of use. The lock took two tries, and when it finally gave, the sound it made was a low, metallic groan, like a train releasing its brakes in a dark tunnel.

Silas walked through.

He saw the body again from the other side, and this time he saw it the way he used to see a caboose: from the angle, from the line of sight, from the distance that mattered. And he saw three things that made his stomach drop.

First: the body was positioned on the track bed, not beside it. Not on the ballast where someone would naturally fall or be placed by a panicked hand. On the exact line of the rails. Centered. Both feet in line with the ties. As if she'd been laid down with a ruler.

Second: the shoe she was missing was not the one you'd expect. The right shoe was still on her foot. The left shoe—the one missing—was sitting neatly beside her head, laces tied, toes pointing toward the tunnel. Someone had taken it off carefully. And tied the laces.

Third: the notebook. The open page. He could read the heading from this distance. *MASON'S GAP, 1977. THE NARROW-GAGE CONTRACT.*

Silas turned to Marsh.

"She was asking questions," Silas said. His voice was flat. Not angry. Not afraid. Just a fact, stated the way you'd state a departure time on a board. "That tunnel was built in '41. The contract that authorized the construction—the original one, the one that went out to the rail company—was never filed with the state. It's in a box in the railroad office in Flagstaff. She was looking for it."

Marsh looked at him. "How do you know?"

"Because the heading is in the same font as the cover letter I wrote in '77 when the state finally asked us to produce the records. I used the same letterhead. The same type. I don't know why she'd care about that, but she was in the right place asking the right kind of question. And someone here in Harrow's Bend knew she'd be coming. And they put her in the Tunnel."

He looked back at the body.

"She wasn't killed here," Silas said. "She was placed here. On the track. Centered. The left shoe off and laced. That's not a panicked placement. That's a *presentation*. Someone wanted the person who found her to see her exactly where she was laid down. And the person who found her was going to be *us*." He nodded at Marsh. "They wanted me to find it first."

Marsh was quiet for a long time.

"Mr. Voss," he said carefully. "Did you know her?"

"No."

"Could it be someone in town who wanted the records destroyed? The 1977 contract. Whatever was in it."

Silas looked at the body one more time. He looked at the shoe. He looked at the fog pouring out of the dark mouth of the tunnel, and he thought about the trains he'd run through that tunnel for thirty-two years, the sound of the steel on the steel, the way the air pressure would change in your ears as you dropped into the dark, the way the world would go black and then white and then the fog again.

"Ask me that after I get the gate closed," he said.

He went back to the gate. He locked it. The sound of the lock engaging was small, almost gentle, like a door closing in a house where no one is home.

Behind him, Sheriff Boyd's voice carried down the short stretch of ballast, steady and low: "Marsh. Get a tarp. And get me a call from Flagstaff. I want the records pulled before noon."

The fog was still moving. It hadn't thinned. It never did, in the morning. It just got older, and thicker, and more patient, the way the mountain had been patient for a hundred years, waiting for something to happen that would make the fog worth the trouble.

Silas put his thermos in the car. He got in the passenger seat. He looked at the tunnel once more, and then he looked out the window at the road, at the pines, at the gray, and he thought: *someone's off the schedule.*

And for the first time in twenty-three years, the walk he'd planned for the afternoon was no longer just a walk.

The siren was still on.
