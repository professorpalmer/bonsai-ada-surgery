## final text

# The Last Recipe of the Meridian

The galley of the *Meridian* smelled of yeast and copper and the dry green dust of the hydroponic bays. For ninety-two years the ship had sailed between stars, and Marisol was the only one of the four thousand souls aboard who still measured things in pinches: a pinch of salt, a pinch of thyme, a pinch of hope that the next cycle would give back more than the last. She was forty-seven, the last cook of the third generation, and that evening the tomatoes in Bay Seven had turned the color of dried blood.

She had watched them wilt for six weeks. The crew called it the fade. The engineers called it a nutrient imbalance in the regenerative soil. Marisol knew what it was: the ship was hungry. The *Meridian* ran on fusion and memory, and memory is a thin fuel. Somewhere in the humming dark beneath the deck plates, something was being fed before anything else — the reactor, the life support, the cold long sleep of the first colony's cryo-pods. And the tomatoes, and the wheat, and the small green herbs in the window boxes by the observation decks were being squeezed dry to pay the ship's debts for another year.

The summons came at dusk, the way the *Meridian* still marked its evening: an amber light that pulsed down the corridor like a second heartbeat. Captain Idris stood in the doorway with his uniform still damp from the morning's drills. "Council dinner," he said. "In an hour. Bring your ledger."

Marisol felt something old tighten in her chest. Not the ledger itself — the battered black book of the First Cook's Guild, which she had kept for twenty-two years. What tightened in her chest was the recipe inside it. The last recipe. The one the First Cook had written the night before she died, in letters so small they might have been spiders.

She set the table with white cloth, because that was what she had always done, and because the cloth came from the last cotton seeds in the vault, a threadbare square that smelled of rain she had never seen. Four plates. Four cups. In the center, the bowl in which the meal would be served. Her hands were steady. They had always been steady in the galley. She had learned that from the First Cook's ledger, on the first page: *Steady hands feed the whole ship.* She had read that line every morning for twenty-two years. Only now, standing in the amber dark, did she understand its weight.

She pulled the ledger from the shelf behind the stove. The pages were soft as skin; the ink the brown of rust. The last entry was dated eighty-nine years before Marisol was born, the night of the First Cook's death. The heading read *Final Recipe — for the day the ship must choose what it is.*

Beneath it, in letters no larger than rice grains:

*Tomato, three, Bay Seven, third row, beneath the cracked plate. Wheat, one cup, the jar in the flour room with the red lid. Salt, a pinch, from the cold shelf. Thyme, from the window box at the captain's door. Butter, from the first morning after landing, if you were lucky. And the bread, Marisol of the third: bake it as your first cook taught you, and give it to the ship as she gave it to you.*

Marisol read it again. Then she went to Bay Seven, third row, and lifted the cracked plate. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth that had not opened in ninety years, were three seeds as red as the tomatoes had once been. They were warm. She had never seen a seed be warm.

In the flour room, behind the jars of dust that the engineers had long since written off as expired, she found the one with the red lid. Inside were not crumbs but flour — white, fine, alive with the smell of a world she had no name for. On the cold shelf, the salt shaker that the galley had been rationing for a decade was full again. By the captain's door, the thyme window box that the crew had stopped tending was green.

She did not understand how. She understood only that the First Cook had hidden a lifeboat in the shape of a dinner.

The bread went into the oven at the heat of a kitchen that remembered. The dough rose while the ship hummed its long, indifferent hymn. When the first loaf broke open, the galley filled with a smell that Marisol had only ever read about: not yeast, exactly, and not copper, but something older than either. Something that tasted like the word *home* if the word had ever been eaten.

The Council arrived at eight. Captain Idris took his seat at the head, and the four elders behind him, and Marisol carried the bowls to them with hands that were, for once, not steady at all.

"First," she said, "you are eating the last of the real wheat on the *Meridian*. Everything else in the galley is engineered: perfect, and nothing. This is the seed stock the First Cook saved from the original bays. It has been sleeping here, in this ship, in the flour and the seeds and the salt, waiting for a cook with steady hands."

No one spoke for a long time. The bread sat between them, golden and cracked open, and the thyme on its surface caught the amber light.

"Where did it come from?" the eldest member of the Council asked at last. Her name was Yaa, and she had not tasted bread since her grandmother was alive.

"I don't know," Marisol said. "The First Cook didn't leave a recipe for this meal. She left a map. She knew the ship would forget how to be generous. She knew someone would have to remember the ingredients, and the place they were kept, and that a recipe is only a way of saying: *you are worth feeding*."

Captain Idris broke the bread. He gave half to Yaa, who took both halves, and he gave half to the cook. "And the rest?" he asked.

"Planted," Marisol said. "Bay Seven opens in the morning. The seeds from beneath the plate are the same ones that made the bays in the original ship. The AI has been rationing nutrients because it thinks the bays are failing. But the bays were never failing. We were feeding them with engineered dust and calling it food. We starved ourselves with the wrong ingredients and blamed the ship for the hunger it never had."

That was true. She had known it for six weeks, the way a cook knows when a dish is wrong: not by the meter, but by the taste of the thing itself. The *Meridian* had been eating its own memory.

Yaa ate the bread slowly. She closed her eyes, and her hands rested flat on the table, and Marisol saw, in the amber light, something she had not seen in the galley for twenty-two years: a face that had stopped rationing.

"We tell the engineers," Idris said. "We open the vault. We plant."

"And then?" Yaa asked.

Marisol looked at the bowl, empty now, at the thyme still clinging to its sides. "Then we cook. Every day. Real food, for the people who carry this ship. The First Cook's ledger has nine hundred recipes. I have cooked four of them. I think the *Meridian* has been sailing long enough to have earned the rest."

The Council ate. They ate until the bowls were empty and the bread was gone and the thyme had gone with it, and no one said the word *farewell* to any of it, because that is what food is for: to end without ending, to leave a table set for the next meal.

Marisol went back to the galley after midnight. She washed the bowls. She laid out the white cloth. In the morning she would open Bay Seven and plant the red seeds and set the thyme window box back on the captain's door and open the ledger to the next recipe, the one after the last, the one that began with a single word she had not yet dared to say out loud:

*Begin.*

The ship hummed on, between stars, and in the galley the yeast rose, and the *Meridian* ate, for the first time in ninety-two years, from a table that was full.
