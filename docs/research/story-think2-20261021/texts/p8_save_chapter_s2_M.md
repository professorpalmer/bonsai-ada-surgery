# Chapter One: The Shell of the World

The city of Veranthos breathed with the turtle.

Every morning, the cobblestones shuddered once, deep and slow, like the first groan of a sleeper turning in bed. The spires of the upper tiers creaked. The great bronze bells in the Temple of the Old Currents tolled themselves — not with a chime, but with a low, resonant hum, as if the bronze remembered being poured into a molten world. Children learned to walk before they learned to read, and the first thing their mothers taught them was the rhythm: *step, step, pause.* The pause was for the turtle's breath. The step was for the city to keep its footing.

Maren Kell stood at the edge of the Ninth Step and watched the tide.

Below her, the world was not a flat, forgiving plane but a sloping shelf of barnacled stone that plunged away from the turtle's flank into a grey-green sea the size of nothing and everything. The sea did not lap. It *heaved*, in great slow swells that matched the creature's heartbeat, and the whole of Veranthos rose and sank with it, a ship made of granite and ambition riding an animal older than the concept of names.

She pressed her thumb against the rail of the observation terrace. The bronze was warm.

It was always warm. That was the first thing every new citizen was told, and the first thing the old ones never stopped thinking about. *The shell is warm because the blood beneath it is warm, and the blood beneath it is warm because the thing is alive and will stay alive until the last stone of this city crumbles and the sea takes the rest.*

Maren was twenty-three and had spent her entire life on the back of a creature she could not see.

She had never seen the head. No one in Veranthos had. The turtle — they called it the Deep One, or the First Mother, or, in the tavern songs, the God Who Chose to Walk — was a thing of pure myth and geological fact. Its head was somewhere below and forward, a smudge of legend on the horizon that the cartographers had never dared to mark. Its tail vanished into the far southern shallows, a rumor of shadow. What they had was the shell. Four hundred feet wide at its broadest. A living plateau of keratin and bone that the city had carved, carved, carved until the ridges were streets and the scutes were districts and the great natural grooves in the shell were channeled into aqueducts that fed the fountains and the forges.

It was beautiful. It was also, Maren thought, a very large animal with a very small idea of its tenants.

"You're staring again."

She didn't turn. The voice behind her belonged to Cael, who had the particular talent of appearing from no direction at all, as if he had simply decided to be there.

"I'm thinking," Maren said.

"Certainly. You're thinking very loudly, though. I can hear the gears turning."

She turned then. Cael was lean and sharp-featured, the kind of young man who looked like he'd been drawn with too many straight lines. He wore the grey coat of the Survey Office, the one that made him look perpetually underdressed, and his boots were caked with the pale calcium residue of the lower tiers.

"What are you doing here?" he asked, not unkindly.

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

She pointed down. To the sea. To the slow, terrible rise and fall of the water against the city's base. "To the thing we're standing on."

Cael followed her gaze. "It's been rising for an hour."

"I know."

"That's not a good hour. The Deep Currents are shifting. You'll feel it in the lower districts by nightfall. The water in the cisterns will go cloudy. The fish in the Lower Tiers' nets will turn silver and swim upward, and the old women will say the turtle is waking."

"Does the turtle actually wake?"

Cael looked at her the way you look at someone who has just asked if the sun is, in fact, on fire. "Maren. I have been on the back of a turtle for twenty-two years. I have studied the seismology of its spine. I have mapped the thermal currents through the shell's deepest strata. I have never once measured a change in its vital signs that suggested it was not in a state of continuous, profound sleep."

"And yet."

"And yet what?"

"And yet it breathes," she said. "It breathes, and it shifts, and the whole city sways with it, and I don't know anymore if it's a mountain with a pulse or an animal with a mind. And that terrifies me more than the sea does."

Cael was quiet for a moment. He ran a hand along the rail, where the bronze was warm under his palm. Then he looked at her.

"The Survey Office wants a report by the morning. The tide markers on the Seventh Step are reading high. If the shift continues through the night, we'll need to reroute the southern aqueduct. Which means the Lower Tiers lose water for three days. Which means the forges go cold. Which means—"

"Which means I lose the heat in my flat," Maren finished.

He smiled. Just slightly. Just enough.

"You're going to be very good at this," he said.

"Good at what?"

"Being the one who notices." He touched the rail where her thumb had been. The bronze was still warm. "The turtle moves, Maren. It has always moved. But nobody in this city talks about what it means when it moves in a direction it hasn't moved in a thousand years."

She looked past him, toward the southern horizon, where the tail-shadow of the Deep One should have been, where the old maps went blank and the cartographers had drawn a simple, honest line and written: *here the world continues.*

"It's not moving in a new direction," she said.

"No," Cael agreed. "It's moving toward something."

The sea heaved. The whole city creaked. Somewhere deep below, in the black architecture of bone and ancient blood, something turned in its sleep.

Maren gripped the rail and, for the first time in her life, did not step, or step, or pause.

She simply stood, and listened, and felt the warmth beneath her feet pulse once, twice, like a question being asked.

And the city, for all its stone and bronze and careful, ordered life, breathed with the thing beneath it, and did not yet know what was coming.
