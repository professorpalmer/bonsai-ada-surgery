# Chapter One: The Shell of the World

The city of Veranthos breathed with the turtle.

That was the first thing every child learned, before reading, before counting, before the names of the four great rivers that carved the plateau's spine from one edge to the other. You pressed your palm flat against the warm stone of the lower terraces and felt it. Not an engine. Not a furnace. A slow, tidal pulse, vast enough to shake the foundations of the Merchant's Row and yet so gentle it never woke the sleeping in their beds. The great beast beneath them slept, but it breathed, and its breath was the heartbeat of everything above.

Maren pressed her palm to the wall of the chandler's workshop and counted. One. Two. Three. Four. The pulse was quick today, a half-beat faster than yesterday, a quarter faster than the morning. She pulled her hand back, knuckles white.

"You're not the first person to feel it," said old Tessa from across the alley, leaning on her doorframe with her arms folded over a shawl the color of dried blood. "Your mother felt it. Her mother felt it before that. The turtle's been quickening since the last frost. Or maybe since the stars went out."

"Since the stars went out," Maren repeated.

It had been a week now since the sky had dimmed. Not dark—never dark—but the constellations that had hung over Veranthos for six thousand years had simply faded, one by one, like candles blown out in a draft. The Oracle had called it a *veiling*. The priests had called it a *testing*. The merchants in the lower bazaar simply called it bad for trade, and they were right, because when people were frightened, they bought less and argued more.

Maren pushed past Tessa and climbed the spiral stairs inside the workshop. The chandler, a broad man named Goss, was already there, his thick fingers wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold. He looked up at her and his face did the thing it did whenever she asked a question he didn't want to answer—the polite, careful emptying of the eyes.

"The tide pools," he said before she could speak. "What did you find in the tide pools?"

"Nothing. The same kelp. The same small crabs with the blue markings. But the water—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The water was warm, Goss. Not warm like after the midday sun. Warm like a living thing. And it was moving *toward* the ridge. Not away from it. Toward the center of the shell."

Goss set the tea down. He poured a fresh cup and pushed it across the counter to her. His hands were steady. His voice was not.

"The center of the shell is the Spine," he said. "No one goes to the Spine. The Council sealed it three hundred years ago after the last time the turtle dreamed."

"Which time it nearly woke," Tessa said from below, where she had followed them up, her shawl trailing behind her like a dark banner.

Maren looked between them. The workshop was small, lit by three oil lamps and the grey winter light slanting through the single window. Outside, the bells of the upper terraces had begun their morning toll, each strike rolling down the curved stone of the shell and echoing off the inner walls until the whole city rang like a struck bowl.

She was twenty-two. She was a chandler's apprentice, not a scholar, not a priest, not an Oracle. She had never climbed higher than the third terrace because her knees ached when the wind picked up, and the higher terraces were where the wind lived. She was, in every way that mattered, an ordinary woman in an extraordinary city, and she had spent her life believing that ordinary was enough.

But ordinary women did not feel the pulse quicken. Ordinary women did not notice that the water in the tide pools—pools that the turtle had formed in its own skin, warm and brackish and ancient—was flowing toward the center. Not away, as it had for six thousand years, always draining toward the edges where the tide pools emptied into the sea far below the shell.

*Always toward the edges.*

"Who sealed the Spine?" she asked.

Goss and Tessa exchanged a glance. It was the kind of glance that could take a long time to form. Goss broke it first.

"No one alive remembers," he said. "The records are in the Archive. But the Archive is on the third terrace."

"Third terrace," Maren said, and she did not know why her voice came out so flat. The wind. The wind up there was something you felt in your teeth, in your bones, a constant thin howl that the old women called the Turtle's Hum. She had always avoided it.

Tessa uncrossed her arms. "You want to go up there, girl, and I can see it in the way you're holding your jaw. But let me be straight with you. The Council will not let you through the lower gates after dark, and they have not let *anyone* through after dark for eleven years. Not since the Veiling."

Maren looked out the window. The city spread below her in concentric rings of stone and copper and white plaster, all of it curving gently with the enormous architecture of the shell, every street, every alley, every chimney bending in that slow, ancient curve that made Veranthos look less like a city and more like a living thing itself, a creature made of ten thousand smaller creatures.

Below the lowest terrace, the underside of the shell dropped away into mist. The sea was not visible from here. It had never been visible from here. No one in Veranthos had seen the water in two hundred years, and no one had ever gone below to check. The city floated on the turtle's back, and the turtle swam in an ocean no living person had ever mapped, and everyone in the city had agreed, over two centuries of quiet, not to think about it too hard.

Maren picked up the tea Goss had poured. It was still warm.

"Show me the gates," she said.

Outside, the bells were still ringing. And somewhere deep below the stone, in the warm dark country of a creature older than any name, something shifted in its sleep. Just slightly. Just enough.

The pulse came again.

And this time, Maren did not count.
