# Chapter One: The Shell of the World

The city of Veranthos breathed with the turtle.

That was the first thing every child learned, before reading, before counting, before the names of the four great rivers that carved the plateau's spine from one edge to the other. You pressed your palm flat against the warm stone of the lower terraces and felt it. Not an engine. Not a furnace. A slow, tidal pulse, rising every forty minutes like the earth itself had a heartbeat. *Thump.* Silence. *Thump.* Silence. The whole city swayed three inches on its spine, and the bells in the Temple of the Old Back rang themselves clear.

Maren Kess felt it now, the way she had felt it every morning of her nineteen years, though this morning the pulse seemed heavier than it should have been. Heavier, and slower, and somewhere beneath the stone floor of her family's tallow-shop on the Third Terraced Way, something deep and ancient shifted in its sleep.

She set down the ladle she'd been using to stir a pot of rendered fat. The flame guttered. Outside the shuttered window, the sky had turned the color of a bruise—violet at the horizon bleeding into a sickly pewter overhead—and the wind off the sea carried the smell of salt and something else, something sharper. Copper, maybe. Or rust.

"Mother," she said, not turning. "The rhythm's off."

Her mother's hands didn't stop kneading. They never stopped. "The turtle's dreaming, Maren. You know that."

"I know what I know." Maren wiped her hands on her apron and stepped out into the narrow alley between the tallow-shop and the bone-carver's stall. The air was thick with the smoke of a hundred small fires and the low, constant murmur of the city's ten thousand souls going about their morning. Merchants unrolled their awnings along the curved walls of the shell. Fishwives from the lower docks hauled their catch up the spiraling stairways. Children chased a fat grey rat through the gutter that ran the length of every block, laughing, shrieking.

Above them all, the sky pressed close.

No one in Veranthos could see the horizon. The city sprawled across the broad, domed back of the turtle that scholars called *Vael'kess*, the World-Tortoise, and the shell's rim—its ancient, barnacled edge—rose twenty thousand feet above the plateaus and valleys they called the Underworld. The sun climbed only to the midline of the shell before its rays were swallowed. There was no true day in Veranthos, no true night. Only the long, amber dusk and the long, deep indigo dark, and the occasional shimmer of bioluminescent blooms along the shell's outer skin when the creature passed over a particularly rich stretch of the sea.

Maren walked north, toward the High Spire, past the tannery that reeked of alum and hide, past the water-cisterns where the city's engineers drew their supply from the thermal vents that bled through the shell's porous stone. Her boots clacked against the curved cobblestones, a sound every resident of Veranthos had known since birth.

She reached the base of the Spire and stopped.

The Spire was the oldest structure in the city, a tower of pale stone that rose from the center of the plateau and was, or had once been, the seat of the Shellkeepers—the council of elders who read the turtle's dreams, who negotiated with the sea, who decided when the city would shift its course to the left or the right along the creature's endless, uncharted path. For three generations now, no Shellkeeper had sat the high chair. The last one, an old woman named Daelith, had simply walked into the sea twenty years ago and never returned. The council had dissolved. The Spire had gone dark.

And yet Maren climbed it. Not because there was a council to see, but because she was a cartographer's apprentice, and the Spire housed the city's single working telescope—a brass instrument so old the lens had been ground by hand in a workshop that no longer existed—and she had been told, in a letter she'd found three nights ago in her mother's dead father's old desk, that something was coming.

The letter had been written in a hand she did not recognize.

*The pulse is changing. The deep one is waking. Come to the Spire before the next turn or the city falls with it. —A. Voss*

Below the Spire, the pulse came. *Thump.* The ground shuddered. Not the usual three-inch sway. A full, lurching heave. The market stalls in the lower quarter crashed into each other. Shouts rose like birds startled from a wire. And deep beneath Maren's boots, beneath the stone and the terraces and the ten thousand lives built atop the ancient creature's back, something vast and wet and impossibly old turned its head in the dark.

Maren pressed her palm flat against the base of the Spire. She felt it. The shift. The new angle.

Vael'kess was changing course.

She climbed.
