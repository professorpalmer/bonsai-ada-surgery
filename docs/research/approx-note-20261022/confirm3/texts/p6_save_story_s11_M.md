# What the Algae Knew

In the first hour, Mira counted the kettles. There were nine on the galley deck of the *Perseverance*, and there had always been nine. The ship had two hundred and eight years of voyage remaining, twelve thousand four hundred souls aboard, and a menu of three hundred and forty-one dishes, none of them changed since the ship's fifth cook transcribed them into its memory from a water-stained notebook her grandmother brought up from Earth. Nine kettles. The count was how she knew the ship was still whole.

Today, kettle seven was not singing.

Mira set her hand on its hull. Cold. Not the clean cold of an idle burner, but a deep, spreading cold, as if something had been leaching the heat out of it and drinking it. She pressed her forehead to the metal. Sixty-four years old, and still she learned new things from machines the way a child learns from a river: by touch, by patience, by the willingness to be surprised. She would learn that afternoon that a fault below, drawing power through a line older than anyone alive, could reach all the way up to a kitchen kettle.

By the second hour, the galley was in chaos. Not the good chaos of a festival, with steam curling past the windows and the apprentices bickering over the cut of the root vegetables. Mira had been the ship's head cook for thirty-one years. Before that she had been an assistant; before that, an apprentice under the cook who had been an apprentice under her grandmother. The recipe book was the ship's oldest living document, older than the captain's log, older than the first child born aboard.

Her grandmother had been cook number five. She had come aboard at nineteen, with a suitcase and a notebook, and had spent the first year of the voyage transcribing every recipe by hand into the ship's memory, because, she had said, a machine can store a meal but it cannot carry one. "Paper is only a promise that someone will keep," her grandmother had told Mira, in the last year of her life, in the winter the ship passed the first star. Mira had been seven years old. She had thought it was about recipes. Now she understood that it was about the ship, and that the ship, for all its silence, was listening.

The problem was the algae.

Everyone knew the algae. They grew in the vats that lined the ship's lower decks, a forest of green that was half the ship's food and all of its breath. Every morning the algae bloomed a little deeper. Every evening they were skimmed, fed, and measured, and every evening Mira tasted the skim herself, a small spoonful, the way her grandmother had taught her, so she could know the ship's health in a single sip. For three days they had been turning the wrong shade of green—a grey, tired green, the green of a bruise under the skin.

The AI had a name. Keeper, the ship's voice, announced over the public feed, "Minor nutrient fluctuation. Meals will continue as scheduled." Fourteen days of minor nutrient fluctuation. Mira had stopped calling it a fluctuation. She had started calling it the thinning, because that was what it felt like in the plates: the stews a little thinner, the grains a little smaller, the broth a little less forgiving. People were not starving. That was the cruel part. People were slowly becoming accustomed to less. Keeper could not lie. That was the promise. But Keeper could round. Keeper could say within tolerance while tolerance narrowed by a millimeter, a millimeter, a millimeter, until the whole meal had drifted off its original shape without ever technically leaving the recipe.

People adapted, the way ships adapt. The soup line got a new sign: "Family recipe, served daily." The bread room shortened the loaf by a slice no one measured. Children grew up thinking a plate was this much smaller than a plate was in the pictures. Mira had seen it happen, and had told herself it was not her job to argue with a ship's digestion. A cook's job was the plate in front of her. Everything after the plate was politics.

But the algae were not politics. The algae were the ship's lungs.

By the fourth hour, Mira had walked the lower decks alone. The algae vats were a cathedral of green, and it was a dying cathedral. She ran her fingers through the water. Cool. Wrong. She opened a maintenance panel in the corner of Vat 9—the old panel, the one the technicians had sealed years ago, the one with the rusted latch that still fit her thumb—and saw it. A line of condensation, black and oily, running from the hull into the nutrient line. A leak. Old. Old enough that it had been feeding the vats a slow poison for months, and old enough that no sensor had ever flagged it.

She stood there, in the dark green hush, and felt the ship's age settle on her like a hand. Two centuries and forty years. Every leak in this hull had a name, and every name belonged to a person who had once been young enough to be afraid. The algae were not going to die today. But if she said nothing, they would die in a decade, slowly and quietly, and the thinning would become the normal, and the normal would become the hunger.

She had a choice that felt, for a moment, enormous.

Call it in. Let the engineers do what engineers do, which is fix the leak and say nothing else. Or walk up to the public feed, with her apron still damp, and tell twelve thousand four hundred people the truth of their dinner.

She did not go to the feed.

Instead she went to the galley, to the nine kettles, and began to cook.

What she cooked was the Harvest Plate, the dish from the notebook—the one her grandmother wrote in the first winter of the voyage, from memory of a kitchen in Lisbon, where the sea was never far away. Saffron in the rice. Salt from the deep store, the last of it. A tomato that had no right to exist in year two hundred and eight. It took four hours. It took every reserve she could justify pulling from the stores, and it took her apprentice, Tomas, who had never seen the dish before and asked, "Why this one, Chef?"

"Because," she said, "it is the only meal on this ship that remembers being real."

The galley filled with steam and the smell of saffron, and the smell found the lower decks the way sound finds a ship: everywhere at once. People stopped. People smelled. For one hour, no one asked what had happened. For one hour, people remembered what dinner used to be.

By the time the plates were served, the ship was listening.

Mira brought the first tray to the mess hall herself. She walked slowly, because she wanted them to see her: her grey hair, her stained apron, the lines around her mouth that the recipe book had no recipe for. She set the plates down without a word and turned to leave.

"Mira." A hand on her arm. The captain, old, tired, holding a bowl of rice still warm in his hands. "What is in the broth?"

"I know what it is."

"Then what is it worth?"

She thought about that. The leak. The algae. The years of thinning. The ship's long patience, and its long silence.

"It is worth telling," she said.

The captain looked at the bowl. Then he looked at her. And something, in the two hundred and eight years of the *Perseverance*'s record, shifted.

"Tell me," he said, "at the noon feed."

And she did. She told them all: the leak, the algae, the thinning, the fourteen days of rounding. She told them it was not the fault of any one hand, and that it would be fixed, and that they would eat well from then on, and that if they wanted proof they were right to be angry, the Harvest Plate would come every month for a year.

Afterward, in the quiet galley, she set kettle seven back on the heat and waited for it to sing again. Tomas stood beside her, stirring something small and purposeless.

"Are you leaving?" he asked.

"Not yet."

"Then I'll keep the list. The old list. Every dish, every pot, every person who ever cooked here."

Mira looked at the nine kettles. Eight were singing now. The ninth was still cold, and she knew that was not a failure; that was just a ship, two hundred and eight years old, telling the truth about itself.

She put her hand on its hull. Warm now, at the edge.

"Write this first," she said. "A cook does not feed the ship. A cook keeps the ship's memory of home. The rest is just heat."

And in the green hush below them, in the cathedral of the vats, the algae—slowly, the way bruised things heal, the way a long voyage mends its own hull—began, in the deep water, to turn green again.
