CHAPTER ONE
THE LAST CHIME

The first thing a retired conductor notices in a body is not the face, but the footwear.

Ray Carver learned it the old way, on the Blue Ridge line, where the rails cut through fog like silver teeth and the passengers were farmers, teachers, and men with secrets too heavy for their suitcases. For forty years he had stood on the running boards, watching the towns below slide past, and in that work he had collected a hundred small observations that other people called trivia. He knew which shoes left no print in the ballast, which hats caught the wind, which pockets held a conductor’s cap only because the owner had never had the sense to throw it away.

At sixty-eight, he no longer wore the cap. It sat on a shelf above his kitchen stove, brass badge tarnished to the color of dried tea, its ribbon still blue as the old line. He wore it on Sundays when he went to the cemetery and on the days when the town needed him to remember.

Blackpine was waking. Fog lay in the hollow like a wet blanket, and the mountain behind the town rose out of it with no edge, only suggestion. The church bell rang six, then seven, and the sound was swallowed by pine. Ray stood at the window with his coffee gone cold, watching the street below: the empty sidewalk by the general store, the old depot with its paint flaking like sunburned skin, the two black trucks parked at the corner of Station and Main. They had not been there before.

He put on his coat and locked the back door.

By the time he reached the depot, the fog had thinned enough to show the platform in gray. The old station had been a post office, then a bank, then a gift shop that sold pine-scented candles and mugs printed with the town’s name. The real depot, the one with the brass railings and the clock that never worked, stood behind the gift shop like a ghost with a good memory. A small crowd had gathered there. Ray could see Sheriff Lila Brennan’s red coat against the dark wood, and beside her the two deputies from the county, men who smelled of coffee and bad weather.

Ray stopped under the eave and lit a cigarette, then stopped again and let it die between his fingers. He had not smoked in thirty years. The old habit of the rail: a cigarette in the hand when you needed to keep your hands busy while waiting for a train. He pulled the cigarette from his mouth and put it back in the pocket.

Sheriff Brennan saw him and hurried over.

“Mr. Carver.”

“Lila.”

“You’re up early.”

“The fog comes off the ridge at six. I was watching it.” He glanced toward the crowd. “And I see you’ve brought the whole county.”

She looked uncomfortable. “There’s a body on the old spur.”

Ray’s stomach tightened, but his face did not move. “How long ago?”

“We found it around four. The paper boy, Tommy Halloway, says he saw someone near the switch at three-thirty, but he was only at the far end.”

“Who’s the victim?”

Brennan hesitated. “A woman. In her thirties. We haven’t got a name yet. She was wearing a gray coat and city shoes. Someone thinks she’s a visitor.”

“Think?”

“Her car’s at the Mountain Inn. There’s a notebook in the trunk. It says, Eleanor Voss, University of Asheville, research files.”

Ray closed his eyes for a second. Eleanor Voss. He had seen her in town a few days earlier, walking from the inn to the old rail yard with a canvas bag over her shoulder, asking the shopkeeper about the 1962 derailment. She had been polite, too polite, the way people are when they are afraid to ask the wrong question.

“I’ll need to see her.”

Brennan hesitated again. “It’s not pretty.”

“Nothing on the line is pretty. Show me.”

The old spur ran behind the depot, a dead end of rusted rail that had once led to the logging camp before the camp burned and the men moved on. The tracks were buried under leaves and pine needles, but the steel still caught the weak morning light. Two deputies had laid a yellow line across the gravel, and a third stood apart with a camera. Sheriff Brennan blocked Ray’s path.

“You don’t have to go out there.”

“I do.”

“You were a conductor, Mr. Carver. Not a detective.”

He looked at her then, and for a moment she saw what he had always been: not a man in a cardigan with a quiet voice, but the man who had once stopped a runaway freight with nothing but a hand signal and a voice that could cut through a hurricane.

“In this town,” he said, “there’s no difference.”

She stepped aside.

The body lay on the track, exactly as Ray had feared. It was a woman, sitting upright in the way dead people sometimes sit when they have been set down carefully, one hand resting on the rail, the other open on her knee. Her head was turned toward the depot, so that if she had been alive she would have been looking for a door. Her eyes were closed. A thin line of frost had gathered along the collar of her coat.

Ray did not cross the yellow line. He stood at the edge and looked.

“First,” he said, “where is the weapon?”

Brennan pointed. “The switch lever. They think it’s what hit her, but it’s bent, not broken. The doctor says the wound was deeper than that could cause.”

Ray stepped inside the line only after the deputies nodded, and only because the place had been his workplace long enough that he knew every crack in the gravel. He crouched by the body, then rose and moved along the rail. The leaves were damp. The ballast was small stone, the kind that crunched under a boot and left no mark. He looked at the victim’s shoes.

“City shoes,” he said. “Leather, no lugs. She hasn’t been walking in the mud.”

“Then how did she get out here?”

“She came from the depot.” He pointed to a narrow path of disturbed leaves that ran from the platform to the rail. “See that? The grass is pressed, not torn. Someone walked it often. But these prints are fresh. She walked this path in the dark.”

Ray’s eyes narrowed. “Her shoes are clean.”

The deputy frowned. “What?”

“If she had been out in the open, her soles would be wet. These are dry. She put them on at the station, or she had just come from inside.”

Sheriff Brennan moved closer. “That doesn’t prove anything.”

“No,” he agreed. “It proves someone dressed her.”

A few words, but the crowd went quiet. Ray did not look at them. He was examining the rail.

The old track had been out of service for years, but the steel was still straight. He ran his fingers along the top, feeling for oil, rust, and the faint ridges left by decades of wheels. At the place where the victim’s hand rested, the metal was shiny.

“Someone cleaned this rail,” he said.

“Cleaned it?”

“Yes. Look. The rust has a pattern. Where the hand has been, the dust is gone. If this had happened naturally, the body would have been dragged or fallen. The hand would have made a mark, not a polished line.”

Brennan’s face tightened. “You’re saying someone placed her here.”

“I’m saying they laid her out.”

Ray stood. He looked at the body again. Her posture was deliberate. The left arm was bent, the hand open, fingers spread. The right arm lay across the rail, the palm turned up. In the palm, small as a seed, was a brass button.

He knelt and picked it up between his thumb and forefinger. It was a brass button, oval, stamped with the letters B&S in a worn script. Behind the stamp, almost invisible, was a small number: 17.

“B&S,” he said.

“Blackpine and Southern?” Brennan asked.

“Exactly. That company hasn’t existed for half a century.”

Ray turned the button over. On the reverse was a faint engraving, a date: 1962.

The sheriff’s voice changed, lower now. “The derailment.”

Ray did not answer. Everyone in Blackpine knew the story. The Blackpine and Southern Night Owl had gone over the creek bed in November of 1962, six passengers and the engineer killed, the conductor missing. The town had built a memorial on the hill above the depot: a simple cross of iron and stone. For forty years, the case had been closed, the missing man presumed dead, and the old line abandoned.

Ray had been a boy then. His father had been the train’s foreman. He had remembered the night the train failed, remembered his father’s hands shaking over a ledger of missing records, remembered the way the fog had pressed against the windows like something trying to get in.

He had also remembered the rumor: that the conductor had not died. That he had been taken by the mountain.

The button was not a body part. It was a token.

Ray put it in his pocket.

“Check the switch lever,” he said. “Not for blood. For oil.”

The deputy went and came back with a rag. He held it up. “There’s oil here. Fresh.”

Ray nodded. “And the lever was used recently. But not by the victim.”

Brennan looked from the deputy to Ray. “How do you know?”

“Because the lever is on the far side of the rail. To reach it, someone would have had to cross the track. The victim’s path is on this side. If she had tried to get to the lever, the leaves would show a second trail. They don’t.”

“Someone else came here.”

“Someone else came here and put her down.” Ray’s voice was quiet, but the fog seemed to listen. “Now I need to know what she was doing.”

The deputy who had found the body came forward with a small canvas bag. “We found this in her coat.”

Ray took the bag and opened it. Inside lay a notebook, a pen, and a folded paper. He unfolded the paper. It was a ticket, old and soft as cloth. On one side it read: ONE FARE, BLACKPINE TO THE OLD DEPOT, 7:15 P.M. The date was printed in faded ink: NOVEMBER 17, 1962.

Ray’s breath stopped.

Brennan leaned in. “That’s the night of the derailment.”

“I know.”

“There’s a name on the back.”

Ray turned the ticket over. In pencil, the name ELEANOR VOSS had been written. Beneath it, in smaller letters, was a single sentence:

For Ray, so the train will remember.

The words were so faint he had to hold the paper to the light. His hands did not shake. They never did. That was the thing about a conductor: you learned to keep your hands steady while the world fell apart.

Behind them, the old depot clock, broken for forty years, began to tick.

No one touched it. No one had touched it in decades.

Ray looked up. “Take her to the mortuary,” he said. “And find the woman who gave her this ticket.”

“Who is Ray?” Brennan asked.

He did not answer. In his pocket, the brass button lay against his hip, cold as a small, waiting mouth.

The fog had moved. For a moment, the mountain stood clear above Blackpine, black and sharp, as if it had been holding its breath.

Then the bell in the old depot began to chime.

Not the clock. The bell. The great iron bell that had hung above the station door, silent since the line closed. It swung in a wind Ray could not feel, once, twice, three times, and then was still.

Somewhere above the town, a crow cried.

Ray Carver closed his eyes and listened.

The train was coming.

He knelt and picked it up between his thumb and forefinger. It was a brass button, oval, stamped with the letters B&S in a worn script. Behind the stamp, almost invisible, was a small number: 17.

“B&S,” he said.

“Blackpine and Southern?” Brennan asked.

“Exactly. That company hasn’t existed for half a century.”

Ray turned the button over. On the reverse was a faint engraving, a date: 1962.

The sheriff’s voice changed, lower now. “The derailment.”

Ray did not answer. Everyone in Blackpine knew the story. The Blackpine and Southern Night Owl had gone over the creek bed in November of 1962, six passengers and the engineer killed, the conductor missing. The town had built a memorial on the hill above the depot: a simple cross of iron and stone. For forty years, the case had been closed, the missing man presumed dead, and the old line abandoned.

Ray had been a boy then. His father had been the train’s foreman. He had remembered the night the train failed, remembered his father’s hands shaking over a ledger of missing records, remembered the way the fog had pressed against the windows like something trying to get in.

He had also remembered the rumor: that the conductor had not died. That he had been taken by the mountain.

The button was not a body part. It was a token.

Ray put it in his pocket.

“Check the switch lever,” he said. “Not for blood. For oil.”

The deputy went and came back with a rag. He held it up. “There’s oil here. Fresh.”

Ray nodded. “And the lever was used recently. But not by the victim.”

Brennan looked from the deputy to Ray. “How do you know?”

“Because the lever is on the far side of the rail. To reach it, someone would have had to cross the track. The victim’s path is on this side. If she had tried to get to the lever, the leaves would show a second trail. They don’t.”

“Someone else came here.”

“Someone else came here and put her down.” Ray’s voice was quiet, but the fog seemed to listen. “Now I need to know what she was doing.”

The deputy who had found the body came forward with a small canvas bag. “We found this in her coat.”

Ray took the bag and opened it. Inside lay a notebook, a pen, and a folded paper. He unfolded the paper. It was a ticket, old and soft as cloth. On one side it read: ONE FARE, BLACKPINE TO THE OLD DEPOT, 7:15 P.M. The date was printed in faded ink: NOVEMBER 17, 1962.

Ray’s breath stopped.

Brennan leaned in. “That’s the night of the derailment.”

“I know.”

“There’s a name on the back.”

Ray turned the ticket over. In pencil, the name ELEANOR VOSS had been written. Beneath it, in smaller letters, was a single sentence:

For Ray, so the train will remember.

The words were so faint he had to hold the paper to the light. His hands did not shake. They never did. That was the thing about a conductor: you learned to keep your hands steady while the world fell apart.

Behind them, the old depot clock, broken for forty years, began to tick.

No one touched it. No one had touched it in decades.

Ray looked up. “Take her to the mortuary,” he said. “And find the woman who gave her this ticket.”

“Who is Ray?” Brennan asked.

He did not answer. In his pocket, the brass button lay against his hip, cold as a small, waiting mouth.

The fog had moved. For a moment, the mountain stood clear above Blackpine, black and sharp, as if it had been holding its breath.

Then the bell in the old depot began to chime.

Not the clock. The bell. The great iron bell that had hung above the station door, silent since the line closed. It swung in a wind Ray could not feel, once, twice, three times, and then was still.

Somewhere above the town, a crow cried.

Ray Carver closed his eyes and listened.

The train was coming.
