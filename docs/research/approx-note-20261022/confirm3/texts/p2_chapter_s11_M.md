Chapter One
The 4:17

Arthur "Art" Marlowe had spent thirty-four years of his life listening to a mountain tell the time.

When he was young, the mountain never needed clocks. The 4:17 from Crag Hollow arrived with the smell of coal and wet wool, with the sharp bark of the low whistle, with the way the iron rails gave a little sigh under the locomotive's weight. Even after the line was closed in 1971, even after the passenger cars were stripped of their seats and the station's water tower rusted into a brown tooth against the sky, Art still woke to the schedule in his bones.

That morning it was only fog.

He was sixty-one, broad-shouldered, with a face like a map of old roads: deep lines around the eyes, a jaw set under a gray beard, hands that had once been quick on a switch lock and now a little stiff from years of handling the same brass whistle. He wore a brown wool coat, boots with iron caps, and the conductor's cap he had carried from the last train he ever walked. On his belt hung the whistle, its red leather cord frayed at the end.

He walked the short route from his house behind the station to the old siding, past the boarded-up post office and the general store where the sign still read HARROW & SONS, though no Harrow had lived in Crag Hollow for twenty years. The mountain town sat in a narrow valley where three rivers met, and the only thing that still moved on time was the mail truck that came every other day, if the plow didn't fall through on the ridge.

Art stopped at the old station. The doors were locked, but the lock had long since given up. He did not enter. He never did. The building had a smell that got into you, a mix of dust, old pine, and something faintly human, like a breath held too long. Instead, he stood on the platform and watched the rails.

The mountain line had been closed for fifty years, but the rails were still there, black and clean where the snow had fallen. They ran straight from Crag Hollow into the teeth of the mountains, disappearing into the dark pines beyond the trestle. For a conductor, that was not a sad thing. A closed line was only a line that had stopped running. It still remembered the trains.

He whistled softly to himself, one long note.

The fog broke around the water tower, and then he saw the light.

It was not a headlight, not a lamp, not the yellow blink of the highway sign out near the mill. It was a small white point, steady and low, hanging in the darkness at the end of the siding. Art stood very still. His first thought was that someone had left a lantern out. His second thought was that no one ever left a lantern out in that place, because the siding was a private property of the old railway company, and the company was a name on a tombstone in the town cemetery.

The light moved.

It slipped behind the end of the coal car, then came back out, brighter now. Art felt his pulse change. Not because he was afraid—though fear was there, a cold wire in his chest—but because the light had a rhythm. Left, right, left. Slow. Patient.

He started to walk.

The snow on the ground was fresh, a thin white sheet over the black gravel. Art's boots made two prints with each step. The old siding sloped down a few feet, and the coal car sat on a short spur that curved away from the station like a black thumb. The car was long, narrow, with a roof like a low hump and a single door at each end. No one used it. No one had used it in years.

The light stopped.

Art reached the far end of the platform and looked down. The coal car's door was open.

Inside, the dark was complete except for a small pool of white on the floor. A body lay at the center of the car, on its back, arms spread out as if someone had tried to keep them from falling. The coat was dark, the face pale in the light, the mouth open a little.

Art did not run. He climbed the steps of the platform and stepped onto the car.

The wood groaned under his boots.

The man was not a conductor. Not a miner. Not a man from Crag Hollow. He was wearing a gray city coat, a silk tie, and shoes so polished they looked like glass. His hands were tucked at his sides, and in his right hand he held a small white ticket.

Art knelt.

The face was familiar.

He had seen it in the general store two days ago, when the man had bought a bottle of water and asked if the mountain road would be plowed by Thursday. He had smiled politely and left before the owner could answer. His name was Dr. Miles Vale, the town's new doctor. He had come from Portland a week before, with a black bag and a long case of instruments and a way of looking around the valley as if he were checking for faults.

The doctor's eyes were open.

Art did not look at the wound on the throat until he had to.

It was a single cut, clean and narrow, from ear to ear. The blood had been wiped away almost entirely, except for a dark line down the collar of the coat. No struggle. No blood on the floor. No boot marks in the snow outside the door. The man had been placed there, or had been taken there, and the place had been cleaned with the care of someone who knew exactly what would not be noticed.

Art's hand went to the ticket in the dead man's fingers.

It was not a paper ticket. It was a small card, cream-colored, with a number printed in ink. One way. Car 417. Time 4:17. Destination: Crag Hollow.

He turned it over. On the back, in a fine, slanted hand, someone had written:

You forgot the 4:17.

Art's breath stopped.

He knew that number. Not from the old company's records, because there were no records, only rumors and a few old men who claimed they had seen the 4:17 one last time in 1971, the night the line closed. He knew it from the schedule in his own head, the one that had never changed, even when the trains stopped. The 4:17 had been the last train down. The last one to leave Crag Hollow. It had always been a car with a number that matched the minute: 4:17, car 417.

He had walked it.

He had stood in that car, with his cap in his hand and his ticket in his pocket, and listened to the engine pull away into the mountain. He had felt the car lurch forward and then settle into the long, black silence of the tunnel beyond. He had carried that feeling for fifty years.

Now someone had written it on a card, in a hand he did not recognize, and pinned it to a dead man's hand like a message.

The sound of boots on snow came from the platform behind him.

"Art?"

The voice was Deputy Nell Kessler's, young and tight. She had been with the sheriff for two years, and she still wore her badge like something borrowed.

Art stood. He put the ticket back in the doctor's hand.

"Deputy Kessler," he said.

She appeared on the platform, her flashlight beam shaking slightly in the cold. She did not see the body until she had stepped onto the car. The beam moved from Art's face to the dead man and stopped.

"Oh God," she said.

"Stay back," Art said. It came out sharper than he intended. He softened his voice. "Don't touch anything."

Nell's flashlight trembled. "Sheriff? Should I call—"

"He's already on his way."

She looked at him as if she had never seen him before. "You're not a doctor, Art."

"No."

"You're not a cop."

"No."

"Then what are you doing here?"

Art looked at the body. The doctor's face had begun to take on the blue-gray cast of death. The mountain air was getting colder, and the light from the small lamp in the car was not enough to keep it out.

"I'm a conductor," he said.

Nell's brow moved. "You're not even on the job."

"Not anymore."

She looked down at the ticket. "What's that?"

Art did not answer at first. The question sat in the cold air between them. The lamp in the coal car made a small circle of white around the body, and beyond it the mountain was black. Somewhere far up the ridge, a branch cracked.

"That," Art said, "is a train ticket."

Nell frowned. "There's no train."

"No."

"Then what's it for?"

Art looked at the ticket again. The number 417 was printed in black, and beneath it, in smaller letters, the words: Crag Hollow, terminal. He had seen that typeface before, on the old cards that used to be stapled to the inside of the station door. He had picked them up with his thumb and pressed them into his pocket, a habit so small that no one had ever noticed.

"For a schedule," he said.

The sheriff's headlights appeared at the end of the siding, two yellow eyes in the fog. They stopped, and the sound of the engine dying was a low, satisfied exhale. Sheriff Dale Braddock stepped out, tall and careful, with his coat buttoned to the throat and his hat in his hands.

"Art," he said.

"Sheriff."

"Who told you it was a body?"

Art looked at the open door, the dark car, the white ticket. "The light did."

Braddock nodded slowly. "What kind of light?"

"A lantern."

He stepped onto the platform and followed Art inside. The three of them stood around the body, and the mountain outside went quiet. There was no wind. No birds. No train. Only the small tick of the lamp and the faint sound of Nell's breathing.

Braddock looked at the throat. "Who is he?"

Art had already told Nell. He said the name again. "Dr. Miles Vale. He's new here. He came a week ago."

Braddock's face tightened. "I've seen him at the hospital. He was talking to the mayor about the mine road."

Art waited.

Braddock looked at the ticket. "What's that?"

Art picked it up with two fingers. "It's a train ticket."

Braddock's eyes went narrow. "There's no train."

"No."

"Then where did this come from?"

Art held the ticket up. The fog pressed against the car's roof like a hand. "That's what I need to find out."

Nell's voice was small. "Should I get a bag?"

Braddock shook his head. "Not yet. Art, can you tell me anything else?"

Art looked at the doctor's body. The cut was clean, almost surgical. The blood had been wiped away, but not completely. There was a smear on the collar, a dark line that looked like it had been dragged, as if the doctor had been holding something when it happened. His hands were at his sides, but the left one was not relaxed. The fingers were curled slightly, as if he had been gripping something until the last second.

"Whoever did this knew how to do it quietly," Art said. "No struggle. No blood where it should be. The car was clean before he was put there."

Braddock looked around. "Clean?"

"The floor. The walls. The door. There's no sign of a fight, no broken thing, no dragged body. Just this."

Braddock knelt and looked at the ticket in Art's hand. "A train ticket. From a train that hasn't run in fifty years."

Art felt the weight of it in the lamp light. "Yes."

Nell looked at the doctor's face. "Why would someone kill him for a ticket?"

Art did not answer. He looked at the card again. The handwriting on the back was fine, slanted, almost elegant. The letters were sharp, the slant consistent, the pen pressure even. It was not the hand of a drunk, or a man in a hurry, or a person who had written it in anger. It was deliberate. Patient.

"Maybe not for a ticket," he said. "Maybe for a date."

The silence that followed was thick.

Braddock stood. He looked at the open door, then at the snow outside. "Any tracks?"

Art shook his head. "None. The snow is fresh, but there are no footprints in it."

Braddock's jaw tightened. "That's impossible. Someone had to bring him here."

"Or someone had to take him away," Nell said.

Braddock looked at her. "What?"

"The car. It's open. If the doctor came in here alive, he would have left prints when he went out. If he was put here dead, someone would have to go back in and clean up. Either way, there should be prints."

Art looked down.

The snow outside the open door was smooth and white. Not a single boot mark. Not a drag. Not a shadow.

But on the edge of the platform, where the wood met the gravel, there was a single, dark smear of mud. It was small, no larger than a coin, and it had been wiped almost flat, as if someone had tried to hide it. The mud was black, damp, and mixed with a faint smell of pine resin.

Art crouched.

Braddock bent with him.

"What is it?" Braddock asked.

Art reached out with two fingers and touched it. The mud was still wet.

"Someone stood on the platform," he said, "and wiped their boot."

Nell looked at the open door. "Inside?"

Art looked at the floor of the car. The wood was old and black, with a fine layer of dust in the corners. He did not see prints. He did not see mud. He did not see anything that would tell him who had been there.

"Inside the car, the floor is clean," he said. "But the door hinges are oiled. Very fresh oil. Someone opened and closed that door recently, and they did it carefully."

Braddock looked at the hinges. He saw the shine.

He straightened. "We need to get the doctor identified, get the ticket, and figure out who this was about."

Art nodded.

Braddock turned to Nell. "Call the medical examiner. Tell him to bring a body bag and a camera. Tell him not to touch anything else."

Nell was already pulling out her radio. "I'm on it."

As she walked back down the steps, Art remained inside the coal car. Braddock stood behind him, looking at the doctor's body with the careful, uncomfortable stillness of a man who had seen death before but was not used to it happening on his own ground.

"The mine road," Braddock said. "Vale was talking about it. The mayor wanted to reopen the old tunnel for the new hotel project."

Art did not answer.

"The tunnel was closed after the 1974 collapse," Braddock continued. "Three men died. The company never finished the repairs. No one's talked about it in years."

Art's mind moved to the number. 417. Car 417. The last train. The last train out of Crag Hollow.

He remembered the night. He had been twenty-seven, with a new cap and a full heart. The 4:17 had been a short run, a local, a small passenger car with a handful of people. He had stood in the aisle and watched the mountain pass in black shapes. When the car reached the trestle, the rails had sung, and the engine had given a low whistle that he had carried for the rest of his life.

He had been the last conductor on that line.

When Braddock turned to him, Art was already at the open door.

"Art?"

Art looked out at the snow.

The lamp in the coal car was still on, but the small white light no longer seemed to be from a lantern. It looked like a headlight. A headlight from the end of the siding, low and steady, hanging in the darkness.

Braddock stepped beside him.

He looked down the siding.

The fog had moved.

In the distance, at the end of the tracks, there was a light.

It was not a lantern.

It was not a lamp.

It was a headlight.

Art's hand went to the whistle at his belt.

"Nobody," Art said, "has run a train on this line for fifty years."

The light blinked once.

Then it was gone.

**Chapter One**  
**The 4:17**

Arthur “Art” Marlowe had spent thirty-four years of his life listening to a mountain tell the time.

When he was young, the mountain never needed clocks. The 4:17 from Crag Hollow arrived with the smell of coal and wet wool, with the sharp bark of the low whistle, with the way the iron rails gave a little sigh under the locomotive’s weight. Even after the line was closed in 1971, even after the passenger cars were stripped of their seats and the station’s water tower rusted into a brown tooth against the sky, Art still woke to the schedule in his bones.

That morning it was only fog.

He was sixty-one, broad-shouldered, with a face like a map of old roads: deep lines around the eyes, a jaw set under a gray beard, hands that had once been quick on a switch lock and now a little stiff from years of handling the same brass whistle. He wore a brown wool coat, boots with iron caps, and the conductor’s cap he had carried from the last train he ever walked. On his belt hung the whistle, its red leather cord frayed at the end.

He walked the short route from his house behind the station to the old siding, past the boarded-up post office and the general store where the sign still read HARROW & SONS, though no Harrow had lived in Crag Hollow for twenty years. The mountain town sat in a narrow valley where three rivers met, and the only thing that still moved on time was the mail truck that came every other day, if the plow didn’t fall through on the ridge.

Art stopped at the old station. The doors were locked, but the lock had long since given up. He did not enter. He never did. The building had a smell that got into you, a mix of dust, old pine, and something faintly human, like a breath held too long. Instead, he stood on the platform and watched the rails.

The mountain line had been closed for fifty years, but the rails were still there, black and clean where the snow had fallen. They ran straight from Crag Hollow into the teeth of the mountains, disappearing into the dark pines beyond the trestle. For a conductor, that was not a sad thing. A closed line was only a line that had stopped running. It still remembered the trains.

He whistled softly to himself, one long note.

The fog broke around the water tower, and then he saw the light.

It was not a headlight, not a lamp, not the yellow blink of the highway sign out near the mill. It was a small white point, steady and low, hanging in the darkness at the end of the siding. Art stood very still. His first thought was that someone had left a lantern out. His second thought was that no one ever left a lantern out in that place, because the siding was private property of the old railway company, and the company was a name on a tombstone in the town cemetery.

The light moved.

It slipped behind the end of the coal car, then came back out, brighter now. Art felt his pulse change. Not because he was afraid—though fear was there, a cold wire in his chest—but because the light had a rhythm. Left, right, left. Slow. Patient.

He started to walk.

The snow on the ground was fresh, a thin white sheet over the black gravel. Art’s boots made two prints with each step. The old siding sloped down a few feet, and the coal car sat on a short spur that curved away from the station like a black thumb. The car was long, narrow, with a roof like a low hump and a single door at each end. No one used it. No one had used it in years.

The light stopped.

Art reached the far end of the platform and looked down. The coal car’s door was open.

Inside, the dark was complete except for a small pool of white on the floor. A body lay at the center of the car, on its back, arms spread out as if someone had tried to keep them from falling. The coat was dark, the face pale in the light, the mouth open a little.

Art did not run. He climbed the steps of the platform and stepped onto the car.

The wood groaned under his boots.

The man was not a conductor. Not a miner. Not a man from Crag Hollow. He was wearing a gray city coat, a silk tie, and shoes so polished they looked like glass. His hands were tucked at his sides, and in his right hand he held a small white ticket.

Art knelt.

The face was familiar.

He had seen it in the general store two days ago, when the man had bought a bottle of water and asked if the mountain road would be plowed by Thursday. He had smiled politely and left before the owner could answer. His name was Dr. Miles Vale, the town’s new doctor. He had come from Portland a week before, with a black bag and a long case of instruments and a way of looking around the valley as if he were checking for faults.

The doctor’s eyes were open.

Art did not look at the wound on the throat until he had to.

It was a single cut, clean and narrow, from ear to ear. The blood had been wiped away almost entirely, except for a dark line down the collar of the coat. No struggle. No blood on the floor. No boot marks in the snow outside the door. The man had been placed there, or had been taken there, and the place had been cleaned with the care of someone who knew exactly what would not be noticed.

Art’s hand went to the ticket in the dead man’s fingers.

It was not a paper ticket. It was a small card, cream-colored, with a number printed in ink. One way. Car 417. Time 4:17. Destination: Crag Hollow.

He turned it over. On the back, in a fine, slanted hand, someone had written:

You forgot the 4:17.

Art’s breath stopped.

He knew that number. Not from the old company’s records, because there were no records, only rumors and a few old men who claimed they had seen the 4:17 one last time in 1971, the night the line closed. He knew it from the schedule in his own head, the one that had never changed, even when the trains stopped. The 4:17 had been the last train down. The last one to leave Crag Hollow. It had always been a car with a number that matched the minute: 4:17, car 417.

He had walked it.

He had stood in that car, with his cap in his hand and his ticket in his pocket, and listened to the engine pull away into the mountain. He had felt the car lurch forward and then settle into the long, black silence of the tunnel beyond. He had carried that feeling for fifty years.

Now someone had written it on a card, in a hand he did not recognize, and pinned it to a dead man’s hand like a message.

The sound of boots on snow came from the platform behind him.

“Art?”

The voice was Deputy Nell Kessler’s, young and tight. She had been with the sheriff for two years, and she still wore her badge like something borrowed.

Art stood. He put the ticket back in the doctor’s hand.

“Deputy Kessler,” he said.

She appeared on the platform, her flashlight beam shaking slightly in the cold. She did not see the body until she had stepped onto the car. The beam moved from Art’s face to the dead man and stopped.

“Oh God,” she said.

“Stay back,” Art said. It came out sharper than he intended. He softened his voice. “Don’t touch anything.”

Nell’s flashlight trembled. “Sheriff? Should I call—”

“He’s already on his way.”

She looked at him as if she had never seen him before. “You’re not a doctor, Art.”

“No.”

“You’re not a cop.”

“No.”

“Then what are you doing here?”

Art looked at the body. The doctor’s face had begun to take on the blue-gray cast of death. The mountain air was getting colder, and the light from the small lamp in the car was not enough to keep it out.

“I’m a conductor,” he said.

Nell’s brow moved. “You’re not even on the job.”

“Not anymore.”

She looked down at the ticket. “What’s that?”

Art did not answer at first. The question sat in the cold air between them. The lamp in the coal car made a small circle of white around the body, and beyond it the mountain was black. Somewhere far up the ridge, a branch cracked.

“That,” Art said, “is a train ticket.”

Nell frowned. “There’s no train.”

“No.”

“Then what’s it for?”

Art looked at the ticket again. The number 417 was printed in black, and beneath it, in smaller letters, the words: Crag Hollow, terminal. He had seen that typeface before, on the old cards that used to be stapled to the inside of the station door. He had picked them up with his thumb and pressed them into his pocket, a habit so small that no one had ever noticed.

“For a schedule,” he said.

The sheriff’s headlights appeared at the end of the siding, two yellow eyes in the fog. They stopped, and the sound of the engine dying was a low, satisfied exhale. Sheriff Dale Braddock stepped out, tall and careful, with his coat buttoned to the throat and his hat in his hands.

“Art,” he said.

“Sheriff.”

“Who told you it was a body?”

Art looked at the open door, the dark car, the white ticket. “The light did.”

Braddock nodded slowly. “What kind of light?”

“A lantern.”

He stepped onto the platform and followed Art inside. The three of them stood around the body, and the mountain outside went quiet. There was no wind. No birds. No train. Only the small tick of the lamp and the faint sound of Nell’s breathing.

Braddock looked at the throat. “Who is he?”

Art had already told Nell. He said the name again. “Dr. Miles Vale. He’s new here. He came a week ago.”

Braddock’s face tightened. “I’ve seen him at the hospital. He was talking to the mayor about the mine road.”

Art waited.

Braddock looked at the ticket. “What’s that?”

Art picked it up with two fingers. “It’s a train ticket.”

Braddock’s eyes went narrow. “There’s no train.”

“No.”

“Then where did this come from?”

Art held the ticket up. The fog pressed against the car’s roof like a hand.

“That’s what I need to find out.”

Nell’s voice was small. “Should I get a bag?”

Braddock shook his head. “Not yet. Art, can you tell me anything else?”

Art looked at the doctor’s body. The cut was clean, almost surgical. The blood had been wiped away, but not completely. There was a smear on the collar, a dark line that looked like it had been dragged, as if the doctor had been holding something when it happened. His hands were at his sides, but the left one was not relaxed. The fingers were curled slightly, as if he had been gripping something until the last second.

“Whoever did this knew how to do it quietly,” Art said. “No struggle. No blood where it should be. The car was clean before he was put there.”

Braddock looked around. “Clean?”

“The floor. The walls. The door. There’s no sign of a fight, no broken thing, no dragged body. Just this.”

Braddock knelt and looked at the ticket in Art’s hand. “A train ticket. From a train that hasn’t run in fifty years.”

Art felt the weight of it in the lamp light. “Yes.”

Nell looked at the doctor’s face. “Why would someone kill him for a ticket?”

Art did not answer. He looked at the card again. The handwriting on the back was fine, slanted, almost elegant. The letters were sharp, the slant consistent, the pen pressure even. It was not the hand of a drunk, or a man in a hurry, or a person who had written it in anger. It was deliberate. Patient.

“Maybe not for a ticket,” he said. “Maybe for a date.”

The silence that followed was thick.

Braddock stood. He looked at the open door, then at the snow outside. “Any tracks?”

Art shook his head. “None. The snow is fresh, but there are no footprints in it.”

Braddock’s jaw tightened. “That’s impossible. Someone had to bring him here.”

“Or someone had to take him away,” Nell said.

Braddock looked at her. “What?”

“The car. It’s open. If the doctor came in here alive, he would have left prints when he went out. If he was put here dead, someone would have to go back in and clean up. Either way, there should be prints.”

Art looked down.

The snow outside the open door was smooth and white. Not a single boot mark. Not a drag. Not a shadow.

But on the edge of the platform, where the wood met the gravel, there was a single, dark smear of mud. It was small, no larger than a coin, and it had been wiped almost flat, as if someone had tried to hide it. The mud was black, damp, and mixed with a faint smell of pine resin.

Art crouched.

Braddock bent with him.

“What is it?” Braddock asked.

Art reached out with two fingers and touched it. The mud was still wet.

“Someone stood on the platform,” he said, “and wiped their boot.”

Nell looked at the open door. “Inside?”

Art looked at the floor of the car. The wood was old and black, with a fine layer of dust in the corners. He did not see prints. He did not see mud. He did not see anything that would tell him who had been there.

“Inside the car, the floor is clean,” he said. “But the door hinges are oiled. Very fresh oil. Someone opened and closed that door recently, and they did it carefully.”

Braddock looked at the hinges. He saw the shine.

He straightened. “We need to get the doctor identified, get the ticket, and figure out who this was about.”

Art nodded.

Braddock turned to Nell. “Call the medical examiner. Tell him to bring a body bag and a camera. Tell him not to touch anything else.”

Nell was already pulling out her radio. “I’m on it.”

As she walked back down the steps, Art remained inside the coal car. Braddock stood behind him, looking at the doctor’s body with the careful, uncomfortable stillness of a man who had seen death before but was not used to it happening on his own ground.

“The mine road,” Braddock said. “Vale was talking about it. The mayor wanted to reopen the old tunnel for the new hotel project.”

Art did not answer.

“The tunnel was closed after the 1974 collapse,” Braddock continued. “Three men died. The company never finished the repairs. No one’s talked about it in years.”

Art’s mind moved to the number. 417. Car 417. The last train. The last train out of Crag Hollow.

He remembered the night. He had been twenty-seven, with a new cap and a full heart. The 4:17 had been a short run, a local, a small passenger car with a handful of people. He had stood in the aisle and watched the mountain pass in black shapes. When the car reached the trestle, the rails had sung, and the engine had given a low whistle that he had carried for the rest of his life.

He had been the last conductor on that line.

When Braddock turned to him, Art was already at the open door.

“Art?”

Art looked out at the snow.

The lamp in the coal car was still on, but the small white light no longer seemed to be from a lantern. It looked like a headlight. A headlight from the end of the siding, low and steady, hanging in the darkness.

Braddock stepped beside him.

He looked down the siding.

The fog had moved.

In the distance, at the end of the tracks, there was a light.

It was not a lantern.

It was not a lamp.

It was a headlight.

Art’s hand went to the whistle at his belt.

“Nobody,” Art said, “has run a train on this line for fifty years.”

The light blinked once.

Then it was gone.
