# Chapter One: The Low Tide of the World

The first rule of Ostra was learned before a child could speak: when the old world groaned, you run for the high places.

Miren learned it the hard way, at six years old, when a tremor rolled through the shell-floor of the Undercity and three hundred people were swept into the gap between the scales. She never forgot the sound the city made — a long, wooden groan, like a ship being dragged across sand. Now, at nineteen, she still woke at the first shiver in the stone.

Ostra had no bottom. Not really. The city rose in terraces from the great dome of a turtle's back, climbing the ridges between the shell-plates like terraced rice fields on a mountain. There were no foundations here, no bedrock to pin a house to the earth; every beam and bolt was lashed to living plate, to a creature so vast that its breath was a weather. From the highest market on the Crown Spire, Miren had looked down once, at the edge of the shell, and seen the sea — a silver sheet, a thousand feet below, rolling under the sky. The world did not turn. Ostra did.

Below the shell, in the hollowed belly of the Undercity, the old people told stories that made them look up at the sky. They said the turtle was older than memory, older than the mountains it had once crossed, and that its shell had worn a city the way a stone wears moss. They said the turtle swam in a sea no map had ever drawn, and that the world above it — the clouds, the sun, the birds — was only its weather. Miren had never seen the underside of the shell. No one had. To walk beneath the city was a death sentence, for the underbelly hung in the black water, and the water had teeth.

"Shell-sweeper's shift in the morning, Miren. And take the east ridge today. The tide's pulling salt onto the walkway."

The voice belonged to Brant, the foreman, a man whose hands were cracked and yellowed from years of scraping brine off the shell. He stood at the gate of the Terraced Ward, his lantern swinging from a rope that went up and out of sight, into the roof of the world. Behind him, the ward slept in stone houses the color of the shell, their doors shut against the dawn.

Miren shouldered her bag. Inside were a bucket, a scrubbing brush, and a coil of rope the size of her wrist. She paused at the gate and looked up.

The dawn was coming up over the edge of the shell, and it was beautiful. That was the thing about Ostra — you could hate it and it would still take you. The sky, close enough to touch, went from copper to rose, and the first light struck the great central ridge of the dome and set the whole city on fire with gold. The birds — the shell-owls and the gulls that nested in the cracks between plates — lifted off in a single great wave, and for a moment the city felt like it was breathing.

"Beautiful, isn't it?" she said to no one, because there was no one to say it to.

Brant nodded, already turning the corner of the street. "Keep your head down and your footing true. The ridge's slick."

The east ridge climbed steeply from the middle of the city toward the dome's summit. It was a walkway of lashed planks set between the ridges of the shell-plates, and the whole ridge swayed — just a little, just enough — when Miren walked it. The shell moved. Below her, the city swayed with it. Above, the sky swayed with it. She had stopped counting the number of times she had almost fallen.

At the halfway point, she stopped.

Something was wrong.

She stood still, and the swaying under her feet grew slower, deeper. Not a tremor. A settling. As if something very large, very heavy, had shifted its weight and was now deciding whether to rest. The planks creaked. A long sound rolled through the plates beneath her boots, low and slow, and it was not the sound Miren recognized.

Not the old world groaning.

The old world groaned fast. This groaned slow.

Her hands were in the rope of her bag, and she was walking faster, and the city was tilting — not much, not enough to tip a cup over, but enough to make her stomach lurch and the birds overhead turn and lift, one by one, toward the Crown Spire.

The gulls went first. Then the shell-owls. Then the windows opened, one by one, along the ridge, and a hundred voices rose from the city below, a single word repeated in a hundred languages, in a hundred pitches, in a hundred different kinds of fear.

Miren ran.

Behind her, the edge of the shell caught the last light of dawn, and the silver sea below it — the sea no map had drawn, the sea that had a thousand feet of nothing between it and the city — was rising, and was no longer silver.

It was black.

And far, far below, at the bottom of that black, something was rising to meet it.
