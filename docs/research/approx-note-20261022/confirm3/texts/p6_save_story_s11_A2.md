# The Last Seasoning

The galley on the Meridian smelled of something that no one on the ship could name and that everyone recognized. It smelled of Sunday.

Mara set the cast-iron pan on the induction ring and listened to it take heat. She was sixty-three years old, and she was the last cook on the Meridian who remembered the old recipes not from the database but from her hands. The database held everything, of course — every gram of thyme, every degree of heat, every calibrated pulse of salt — but a recipe was not data. A recipe was a person remembering another person. Her grandmother had cooked this dish on the original Earth, and her mother had cooked it in the greenhouse at the end of the ship, and Mara had cooked it in the galley where the air recyclers hummed at forty-two hertz and the windows showed nothing but the black, patient dark.

Today she would cook it again. Not for the database. Not for the nutrient rationers who audited her pantry every third week. For Lena.

Lena was seventeen, three weeks away from being assigned to the galley for the term that would make her a cook. Every generation of the Meridian's crew came from the same families, the same handful of surnames that had been on the manifest at the launch. After one hundred and eighty years the ship's population had settled into something between a town and a species. The children knew the ship the way a child knows a body: the groan of the forward bulkhead when the sun was a memory, the creak of deck seven when the hydroponics fans kicked up. They had never known a wind. They had never known rain on a window that was not a viewport.

And they had never tasted anything that was not grown inside the ship.

Mara chopped the onion. The database called it *Allium cepa*, and the label had been printed on the seed packet she'd found in the hydroponics bay three years ago, misfiled behind a rack of soy. A small, dry seed. It had not been part of the official crops. Someone had hidden it, the way the old families had hidden everything they loved — in the gaps, in the seams.

She did not know whose grandmother had planted it. She only knew that when it sprouted, it grew a green so deep it looked like it was holding a secret. And when she dried it in the drying bay, crumbled it between her fingers, and put a pinch into her palm and held it over the pan, the smell that rose was not in any database. The smell had a past.

The database would have said: *aromatic compound. acceptable. optional.*

Mara had said: *seasoning.*

---

When Lena came to watch, she brought a tray of the week's standard proteins and set it down at the prep table with the careful neutrality of someone who had learned early that the galley was a sacred place.

"It smells like the stories," Lena said.

"That's the trick," Mara said. "Stories are just smells that got old."

Lena leaned closer to the pan. "Grandfather says you're the last one. He says when you leave the galley, the recipes are going to go in the database and it'll be all numbers."

"The numbers won't die," Mara said. "People die. Recipes get cooked a thousand times and still taste the same, because a recipe is a number. I'm not teaching you numbers, Lena."

"Then what?"

Mara turned off the heat under the pan and let it breathe. "I'm teaching you that a meal is an apology. We left Earth without asking anyone. We left it to eat. That dish is the apology. You cook it and you remember, and for one hour the ship is not a machine, it is a place where someone forgave us."

Lena was quiet for a long time. "The hydroponics bay had a problem last week. A whole rack. The leafy greens. They just... stopped."

Mara's knife stilled above the cutting board.

"How many people knew?"

"Me. And the bay tech. We fixed it. It's growing again now."

Mara nodded, but she was thinking about the seed packet. About the small dry seed that had been misfiled behind the rack of soy. About how some things grow in the gaps. She looked at the onion, half-chopped, and she thought: *you do not fix a ship by saving one rack.*

She chopped the garlic.

---

That night, the whole ship came to the observation deck for the viewing. It was the anniversary of the first sighting of the new system, and every generation had its traditions: the crew would line up along the deck, and someone would read the coordinates into the dark, and the ship would hum its long, low hum, and everyone would pretend that the stars were looking back.

Mara stood in the line with the tray of food behind her. She had made enough for thirty. The database had logged the ingredients as *unauthorized*, and the rationers had given her a warning that was almost polite. She had not cared. The warning said: *nutritional profile incomplete.* It did not say that the dish was a lie, or that it was nothing, or that it was no different from anything else. It did not know what it was.

Lena stood beside her, carrying the second tray. Her hands were steady.

"Are you afraid?" Mara asked.

"A little."

"Good. Fear means you're paying attention. The food is not dangerous. The memory is. That's why it tastes like home to the people who don't have one."

The coordinates were read into the dark. Someone's voice, older than Mara's, went out over the speakers and the numbers went into the black like a key into a lock that would never be turned. The ship hummed. The stars held their breath.

And then Mara lifted the lid.

The smell went out first. It moved through the line, through the deck, through the observation glass and out into the dark, and for a moment — one honest, unrecorded moment — the Meridian was not a ship. It was a kitchen. It was a table set in a house that had a door that could be closed. It was a place where the air had weight and the windows had weather and the people inside had names that mattered because they were going to be used, tomorrow, for something that would not be repeated.

Lena watched a girl at the other end of the line stop walking. Watch her put her hand to her mouth. Watch her say, very softly, a word that the database would never log.

A word that meant *home.*

Mara set the tray on the deck plate and stepped back.

The database had everything. It held the ship, the crops, the children, the years, the distance to a star that might or might not have a name. It did not hold the smell of a grandmother's Sunday. It did not hold the way a pinch of thyme could reach across a century and say: *I remember you. You are allowed to be hungry.*

She would teach Lena the recipes. She would teach her the cuts, the heat, the patience. She would teach her that the galley was not a place that made food. It was a place that made people into something that could be carried across the dark.

And when the tray was empty, when the last spoonful of broth had gone into the last mouth, and the deck was quiet again, and the stars were just stars, Mara would say the same thing her grandmother had said, and her mother, and the first cook on the Meridian:

*The meal is finished. The memory is not.*

Lena heard it. Lena would carry it.

And the ship, humming its long hum, went on.

---

The seed packet in the hydroponics bay was found, three months later, in a maintenance closet behind the water reclamation unit. It had been growing a second plant. No one knew whose grandmother had planted it. No one knew why.

But the cook, the next cook, the cook after that, would find it. And they would plant it. And the smell would come up out of the drying bay, and the ship would be, for an hour, a place where someone had forgiven them.

That was the recipe.

That had always been the recipe.
