**The Long Table**

0540. Before the first bell, the galley on deck 4 already smelled of yeast and warm flour. Cass Venn stood at her station with a spoon in one hand and a ledger in the other, listening to the ship.

The *Meridian* was a ship in the way that a forest is a river: it had no front, no edge, no place to turn around. It had only length—forty kilometers of living, breathing hull, and in its belly, twelve thousand people were waking up to follow the slow drift of the stars toward a planet called, in the old files, *Terminus*.

Cass's grandmother had been the cook. So was her mother. Cass was the fourth, and in thirty-two years on the ship she had made a total of eleven million, three hundred and twenty-four meals, by her tally. The official count, kept in the ship's records, was about three times that, because the records counted the algae vats, the nutrient bars, the emergency ration packets. The ledger was for the real food. The good food.

She had no intention of stopping.

The morning menu went out at 0550: porridge, bread, kelp soup. Porridge made from the grain grown in hydroponic bay 7, which had been her mother's bay, which had been her grandmother's. Bread from the same flour, proofed overnight in the warm dark of the oven that had been built by the original engineers' families. Kelp soup, because the kelp grew fast, and because, every year, without fail, someone in the crew asked why the soup didn't have potatoes in it.

It never did. There were no potatoes.

There were no potatoes, no tomatoes, no chocolate, no coffee. There was no "real" anything anymore, not since the first harvest failure had emptied the seed vault and the second had emptied the trust. What had remained was what the engineers had grown, what the chemists could synthesize, what Cass's ancestors could remember.

This last item was what made her job difficult, and what made it sacred.

Every dish on the *Meridian*'s menu was a translation. The recipes had drifted with the people, generation by generation, like a language spoken across an ocean. The "kettle soup" of the first cook's notes was, by now, a pale, briny broth that tasted faintly of rain and metal. "Kettle" was a word most children had no reference for. When they asked, Cass explained: "It was a vessel of metal, with a lid, over fire, on land." They nodded, satisfied, and went back to school.

The children were the most dangerous part of the job. They didn't know that food was a kind of memory, that every recipe on the menu was a fossil. They only knew that Cass's porridge tasted like home, which was a strange thing, because none of them had ever been home.

On the morning in question, the danger came in the form of a girl of about ten, with paint on her fingers and questions in her eyes.

"Mrs. Venn?" She had a way of saying it that was older than she was. "My mother says the old cook's book has a recipe. A recipe that was never used."

Cass's spoon stopped.

There was a book. It lived in the archive, in a locked cabinet with a rust-colored tag, stamped with the word *UNTRIED*. Cass had been told about it the day after her mother died, by a man who was either a historian or a man who had been paid to say things. "Your grandmother kept it," he had said. "She never made it. Said it wasn't ready yet."

She had never opened it. Not because she was afraid, but because she was curious. There's a difference.

Now the girl was looking at her with an expression that Cass recognized. It was the look of someone standing at the edge of a pool and wondering if the water would hold.

"What recipe?" Cass asked.

The girl shrugged. "I don't know. The name was… 'Sunday.' Nothing more."

That night, after the last tray was collected and the galley was wiped clean and the yeast was checked and the temperature logs were signed, Cass took the key to the archive from its hook.

The book was small. Leather cover, or what passed for leather in a place where nothing grew. The pages were soft with age, the ink faded to the color of tea.

Sunday, it said. A heading, in a careful hand. Below it:

*For the end of the week, when the hands are tired and the sky is not there.*
*Flour. Fat. Yeast. Salt. Water.*
*And something sweet that is not sugar.*
*Make it as you can. The rest is faith.*

Cass sat down on the floor of the archive, with her back against the cabinet, and read the rest of the page.

There was a list. Flour, yes, and she had that. Fat—rendered, or otherwise, she could manage. Yeast and salt, she had. Water.

But "something sweet that is not sugar"—Cass knew that. She had it in the small jar behind her station, wrapped in three layers of cloth. A single seed, no bigger than her thumbnail, from a plant that had once grown in a garden on a world called Earth. A cacao pod, dried and split and saved, because a chemist in the second generation had looked at it and said, "This is a library. Don't throw it away."

No one had used the seed. To grow a tree, you needed a season. The *Meridian* had no seasons, only schedules.

Cass closed her eyes. In the dark behind her lids, she saw her grandmother's hands. She remembered the way she kneaded. She remembered the words that went with it, half in one language, half in another, a kind of prayer that wasn't a prayer: *let it rise, let it rest, let it be enough.*

She did not know what "Sunday" was. She didn't think she ever would. The word was a fossil, like "kettle."

But she knew what Sunday felt like.

She knew it from her mother's voice, in the late shift, when the ship hummed a little lower and the lights were set to warm. She knew it from the way the crew slowed, from the way the children were let out early, from the way, even in the long dark, someone always made something extra. A little extra. A small, pointless, unnecessary extra.

That was what the recipe was not. That was what it was.

The next three days, Cass did not cook the porridge. She cooked bread. A great loaf, bigger than she had ever made, in a mold she built from spare parts and the patience of a woman who had had nothing else to do but make it. She baked the "something sweet"—a paste from the seed, ground, mixed with the last of the fat and a pinch of salt—and it was not chocolate, not exactly, but it was close enough to make her eyes water.

On the fourth day, she made the soup. On the fifth, she wrote the menu.

She wrote: *Sundays.*

It went out at 0600, and by 0900, the galley door was open and the smell was in the corridor, and people were stopping in the aisles, sniffing, and the whole ship was holding its breath.

The girl came. Her mother came. Others came, people Cass had known for decades, and people she had only seen in the crowd. They stood at the door, and they did not speak, and Cass, who had made eleven million meals, felt for the first time in her life that her hands were not steady.

She cut the bread.

The room was silent in a way she had not heard since the first day she came to the galley, thirty years before the ship had hummed. And then a woman in the front row, older than the girl, older than most of the people standing there, said, "It smells like my grandmother's house."

It smelled like nothing that had ever been. The ship was forty kilometers of metal and light and silence, and no one had ever stood in a house, and no grandmother had ever lived there, and no one had ever cut bread at a table on a morning in a world with weather.

But the woman was right.

It smelled like something that had been, and something that might be, and something in between that had no name except the one they were all standing in it together.

Cass served.

The girl ate first, and then she looked up, and her eyes were wet, and she said, "I don't know what it is. But I want to eat it again. When is the next time?"

Cass smiled. It was the first time she had smiled at a meal without counting it.

"Sunday," she said. "Every Sunday."

Outside the galley door, the *Meridian* drifted on through the dark, and somewhere forward, in a sky no one would ever see with their own eyes, a planet was getting closer.

And somewhere in the galley, a woman with flour on her hands was writing in her ledger, in small, careful letters, a new entry that would be there for generations, and for the one after that, and for the one after that, and for the one after:

*Sunday. Made. It was enough.*

0540. Before the first bell, the galley on deck 4 already smelled of yeast and warm flour. Cass Venn stood at her station with a spoon in one hand and a ledger in the other, listening to the ship.

The *Meridian* was a ship in the way that a forest is a river: it had no front, no edge, no place to turn around. It had only length: forty kilometers of living hull, and in its belly, twelve thousand people waking to follow the slow drift of stars toward a planet that, in the old files, had a name—Terminus.

Cass's grandmother had been the cook. So was her mother. Cass was the fourth, and in thirty-two years on the ship she had made a total of eleven million, three hundred and twenty-four meals, by her tally. The official count, kept in the ship's records, was about three times that, because the records counted the algae vats, the nutrient bars, the emergency ration packets. The ledger was for the real food. The good food.

She had no intention of stopping.

Bread from the same flour, proofed overnight in the warm dark of the oven that the original families had built. Kelp soup, because the kelp grew fast, and because every year, without fail, someone in the crew asked why the soup didn't have potatoes in it.

There were no potatoes, no tomatoes, no chocolate, no coffee. There was no "real" anything anymore, not since the first harvest failure had emptied the seed vault and the second had emptied the trust. What had remained was what the engineers could grow, what the chemists could synthesize, what Cass's ancestors could remember.

That last item was what made her job difficult, and what made it sacred.

Every dish on the *Meridian*'s menu was a translation. The recipes had drifted with the people, generation by generation, like a language spoken across an ocean. The "kettle soup" of the first cook's notes was, by now, a pale, briny broth that tasted faintly of rain and metal. "Kettle" was a word most children had no reference for. Said it wasn't ready yet."

*Flour. Fat. Yeast. Salt. Water.*
*And something sweet that is not sugar.*
*Make it as you can. The rest is faith.*

A cacao pod, no bigger than her thumbnail, from a tree that had once grown in a garden on a world called Earth. Dried and split and saved, because a chemist in the second generation had looked at it and said, "This is a library. Don't throw it away."

No one had used the pod. A tree needed a season to grow. The *Meridian* had no seasons, only schedules.

She knew it from the way the crew slowed, from the way the children were let out early, from the way, even in the long dark, someone always made something extra. A little extra. But the woman was right.

It smelled like something that had been, and something that might be, and something in between that had no name except the one they were all standing in it together.

Cass served.

Cass smiled. It was the first time she had smiled at a meal without counting it.

"Sunday," she said. "Every Sunday."

Outside the galley door, the *Meridian* drifted on through the dark, and somewhere forward, in a sky no one would ever see with their own eyes, a planet was getting closer.

And somewhere in the galley, a woman with flour on her hands was writing in her ledger, in small, careful letters, a new entry that would be there for generations, and for the one after that, and for the one after:

*Sunday. Made. It was enough.*
