# The Salt of Distance

The first thing the cook does every morning on the Meridian is open the window.

Not a real window. There isn't one, not on this hull, not on any hull. There are only the porthole panels over the galley, the broad curved panes that look out into the endless black and the slow wheel of stars. Mira opens the panel anyway, on a long routine that belongs to a ship three hundred and twelve years from Earth, and she lets the cold of the vacuum-locked corridor seep into the galley, where the steam from the morning porridge rises into it and disappears.

"Good morning, you old thing," she says to the window.

The Meridian is a city in a cylinder, thirty kilometers long, spinning to keep four thousand people standing up straight in the dark between stars. For the first two centuries, most of them had never seen a daybreak that wasn't printed on a screen. Mira, the galley's chief cook and the twelfth in a line that stretches back to the ship's departure, has never seen one at all. She knows sunrise the way a monk knows scripture: from other people's accounts, in the color of the records.

Which is why, on a morning in the ship's year three hundred and twelve, she is standing in her apron with flour on her wrists and a single red tomato in her palm, feeling foolish.

She found the seed in the archive.

Not in the hydroponics levels, where the grain and leafy greens and pale tubers of the Meridian's agriculture cycle were grown under white lamps in their endless rotations. She found it in a sealed drawer on the third level of the personal-effects vault, a drawer that had not been opened since the death of the first cook, a woman named Elias Thorne, who had died in the year one hundred and four with her hands still stained with paprika.

The drawer contained a tin of salt, a folded recipe card, and a small glass vial holding a single tomato seed. The card was written in an ink that had gone the color of rust:

*Sauce for the ship's feast. One tomato, one shallot, two cloves garlic, a pinch of sugar, the good oil, the salt from the tin. It takes time. Do not hurry it.*

Elias Thorne's recipe. For the ship's feast, which had been held every year since the first year, in which the sauce had been made from frozen fruit and hydroponic peppers because tomatoes had never, in three hundred years, been more than a word.

The hydroponics team had been skeptical, naturally. "It's a one-generation window, Mira," said the grower-in-charge, a careful man named Doss, when she'd brought him the vial with its request to plant it. "We can grow one tomato. If we can get a vine to set in the climate bay. If the pollen takes. If the fruit ripens before the cold cycle. That's a long if-chain."

So it was a long if-chain. The seed went into a tray of soil that had been composted over decades from the ship's own waste. The vine grew in the climate bay, slow and pale-green at first, then greedy, its tendrils curling around the supports with an almost human determination. Mira checked it every morning after the porridge. She talked to it, which she would not have admitted in a crew meeting. She sang to it once, off-key, a fragment of a song from the archive's music library, a song whose title was simply *Home*, and she did not know who had written it.

The fruit came in the ship's year three hundred and thirteen, small and green and the size of a walnut. She let it ripen. The vine died afterward, exhausted, as if it had given everything it had to give. One tomato, from one vine, in a ship that carried the descendants of every person on its launch list.

Now it sat in her palm in the galley, round and heavy, a red thing that looked, she thought, like a small wound in the light.

She could have preserved it. She could have frozen it for the feast and served it on a platter like a relic, under a glass dome, with a placard explaining its provenance. She could have done what the Meridian did with everything precious: put it behind glass.

Instead she made the sauce.

She found the tin of salt in the drawer beside the seed, a salt that had been carried aboard the ship three centuries ago, its grains worn smooth by centuries of being lifted by human hands. She used a fraction of it. The shallot came from the vegetable level, a small sharp thing with a papery skin. The garlic, two cloves, from the same bed. The oil, from the press on deck nine, cold-pressed from the seed cakes of the oilseed crop, golden and nutty.

She chopped the tomato by hand. It burst under her knife, and the juice ran over the board, and she wiped the board and began again, because the recipe had said *do not hurry it*, and because the juice tasted, even raw, even cold, like something that had never existed before in the history of the ship.

The sauce simmered for four hours. Mira stirred it once every fifteen minutes, which she learned was exactly the right amount of attention, and once every quarter hour she opened the galley window a crack, because the steam carried the smell of the tomato out into the black, and she wanted the ship to know.

Word spread, of course, the way it always does on a city that has never had a front door. By noon the whole upper level was leaning in the corridor windows with their faces pressed to the glass, and the children of the hydroponics families were at the rail in their jumpsuits, and Doss stood with his arms crossed, his skeptical expression doing its best to hold.

At six, in the time the ship called evening, the feast was underway in the central hall, the long cylindrical room with its hanging lights that mimicked a sky. Four thousand people at tables, and at the center of the long table, under no dome, in no case, a shallow dish of red sauce with a small bowl of bread from the bakery level beside it.

Mira carried the dish herself.

She set it down, and she stepped back, and for a moment no one ate, because everyone was watching her, and she could feel the weight of three hundred and twelve years in the silence of the hall — the first cook who'd planted the seed, the grower who'd kept it alive, every cook since then who'd made the feast from what the ship could offer, and now the one who'd cooked the sauce.

Doss ate first. He'd been watching the vine for two years, and he understood better than anyone what the tomato had cost. He took a piece of bread, tore it, dipped it, and he was quiet for a long time.

Then he said, "It tastes like the records. It tastes like the records, and it's real."

And the hall, which had been holding its breath, let go, and the eating began, and the sauce ran out in the end, and people asked if there was more, and Mira, standing at the head of the long table with flour still on her wrists and salt on her hands, said yes, she would make more, and she would write the recipe down, and she would put the new card in the drawer, so that the next cook, two hundred years from now, would open the window in the morning and feel, in the steam, that they were not so far away from home.

That night, after the last plate was scraped clean and the hall lights dimmed to their low hum, Mira wrote the recipe on a card. She wrote it in the same ink as the old one, which she had found in the tin's drawer, a small dark thing that had outlived its first user. She wrote:

*Sauce for the ship's feast. One tomato, one shallot, two cloves garlic, a pinch of sugar, the good oil, the salt from the tin. It takes time. Do not hurry it. And if the fruit ripens, plant the seed. If it doesn't, plant the hope. It takes the same kind of patience.*

She folded the card, and put it in the drawer, and closed the drawer, and opened the window, and let the cold of the corridor seep into the galley, and she watched the stars wheel, and she thought that the Meridian was still moving, still crossing the black, still carrying its four thousand people toward a sun they would never see but would know, in the end, by the taste of a tomato.

And it was enough. It was, she thought, more than enough. It was the beginning.
