Something vast. # Chapter One

The turtle breathed at dawn, and the city of Vaelthir exhaled with it.

Mara felt the breath before she heard it — a slow, shuddering outflow of warm air that rolled through the gutters and under the doorframes of the Undercity, lifting the thatch of the shell-tents and sending the lantern-birds scattering from their roosts in the cracks between the shell's plates.

The elders said the turtle was named Orem, that it had been alive since before the first stone was laid on its back, that its heart was the deep mountain that hummed under the city's feet. They did not say it had ever stopped. They were too busy building.

Mara climbed. The Shellwalk was carved into the upper edge of the largest plate — a spiral path of stone that wrapped the turtle's back three times before reaching the Crown, the highest point, where the lighthouse burned all day. She was sixteen, and she had been climbing for six hours since before dawn, hauling her satchel of rags, salve, and the small iron needle her mother had left her the day before she died.

The Shellwalk groaned behind her. That was the sound the turtle made when it turned, and it was the sound Mara had not wanted to hear. But the old ones said the beast turned slowly, one degree per moon, and that the city was built to follow, and that everyone who had ever lived in Vaelthir had walked this walk, and that no one had ever looked down at the sea far below and seen it.

She looked down anyway.

Below the shell, the world was water, endless and pale in the early light. No one in Vaelthir knew what lay beneath it. The Turtle Keepers had sealed the Undercity's lower vaults with iron and prayer, and the books were old and few. The Keepers said there was nothing below but the deep and the dark, and that the turtle carried the world on its back the way a shell carries the sea inside it.

Mara had a different question. She pressed her hand to the plate and felt it vibrate. Not a turn. Not a breath. A beat. One. Two. The needle in her satchel rang against the glass of its case. "Three," she said.

The needle was not meant to be worn by anyone living in Vaelthir. It had been made in the Undercity, in the old forge where the Keepers kept their first and only fire, and it was said to sing only when the turtle was speaking. Mara's mother had been a Keeper before the sickness took her, and she had pressed the needle into Mara's palm on her last night: "If it sings, listen. Do not be afraid of what you hear."

The beat came again, and with it a sound that was not in the books — not a turn, not a breath, not a word. A second heartbeat. Below her, in the Undercity, the great bell of the shell-tower began to toll. Not for dawn. The dawn bells rang once, in a single low note, and then the day began. These were fast. Three at a time, close together, like a word spoken quickly.

Mara ran. She ran down the Shellwalk and through the gate at the base of the spiral, into the Undercity, where the streets were narrow and the buildings leaned in from the sides because the walls were the inside of the shell and the ceiling was the underside of the plate and there was no up that was not also in, and no in that was not also up. She ran past the tanners who smelled of salt and old rope, past the bread-women with their arms wrapped in linen, past the fish-boys hauling their nets into the salt ponds that the turtle's own sweat fed. She ran past everything and into the bell-house, up the iron stairs, through the door that the Keepers had bolted with a lock that had no keyhole, and up, up, up.

The bell-house was small. A circle of stone. A single window in the center of the far wall, looking out over the Undercity and the Shellwalk and the sea beyond, and in the window, a reflection that was not a reflection.

Mara stood before it and did not blink. In the window, she saw herself. And behind her reflection, she saw a second reflection. A shadow of a woman, with Mara's face, in the same coat, in the same satchel. But the shadow was not moving. And when Mara lifted her hand, the shadow's hand stayed still, and the shadow's face turned, very slowly, and it was looking at her with something that was not quite surprise, and not quite sorrow, and was, Mara thought, looking at her the way you look at a thing you have not seen for a long time and are not sure you recognize.

The needle in her satchel rang again. And below, in the Undercity, the bell tolled a fourth time.

The window cracked. Not from the outside. Not from the wind. From the inside, a fine line of light, thin as a thread, running from the top of the glass to the bottom, and the shadow in the window raised its hand and pressed it to the glass, and the line of light grew, and grew, and grew, and the glass was not cracking but splitting, and through the split, Mara did not see the Undercity behind her, she did not see the sea, she did not see the shell, she did not see anything at all, and she saw, in the split, in the dark, in the place where there had been no crack but was now a window, something moving. Something vast. Something with eyes the size of the bell-tower, and something that was not a reflection and was not a shadow but was, Mara thought, with a certainty that was not her own, a thing that had been waiting.

And then it was quiet. And the turtle, beneath her feet, beneath the city, beneath the world, breathed in.
