The first letter found Amos Thorne in the oil room, where he was cleaning the lamp's lens with a cloth that had once belonged to his father. It was folded into a square the size of a playing card and slipped under the door while the gale kept the sea from the shore. Amos had been keeper at Gull's Reach for thirty-one years, long enough to know every trick the weather played and most of the people who came to the headland for their reasons. He had not expected a visitor from the sea.

The envelope was blue, unmarked, with no stamp and no return address. The writing inside was small and careful, as if the pen had been held by a hand that was still learning its own strength.

Dear Keeper,
I am sorry if this frightens you. I am not born yet. I know that is a strange way to begin, but it is the truest. My name is Wren, and I am writing from the future, which is only a long way off. If you will read this, I beg you to keep the light burning on the night the Meridian meets the gale.

Amos set the note on the table and looked at the window. The sea was a gray sheet hammered by wind. He thought for a moment that it was a joke, perhaps from one of the sailors at the mainland station who had been drinking too much. Then he noticed that the paper smelled of brine and lamp oil, and that in the corner was drawn a tiny sketch of the lamp room: the table, the window, the clock, and the black cat that did not live in the room but whose picture hung there from a kindness long gone.

He laughed, though the sound made him tired.

The second letter came the next morning. By then the mail boat had not arrived, and the post had not come from the village, so there was no way to say who had placed the note in his coat pocket. It was warm to the touch.

You have the habit of pressing your thumb to the glass before you sleep, I have seen it. You keep the cold tea because you believe it reminds you not to be too comfortable. You count the waves only when you are afraid. Please do not throw this away. The night is coming.

Amos read it three times. Then he sat down and did something he had not done in many years: he wrote a reply.

Who are you? How do you know these things? And what ship do you mean?

He left the paper on the table.

By evening it was gone, and in its place lay another blue note.

I am your question, Keeper. I am the answer you have not yet made. I do not know how to explain time. I only know that if the light fails, my mother dies, and I do not come into the world. I am not asking for a miracle. I am asking for duty.

The words stayed with him.

For a week, no more letters came. Amos told himself he had imagined them, or that the storm had loosened something in his head. He polished the lens, wound the clockwork, filled the logbook in his old copperplate, and tried to be a man who did not read messages from the sea. But every morning he looked for the blue square, and every morning there was one.

They were not all words. Sometimes there were small drawings: a girl with a gap in her teeth, a boat in a storm, a lighthouse made of a crayon line, a hand reaching through fog. Sometimes there were questions. What did the gulls sound like when the wind was high? Did the stone steps ever creak? Did you ever want to leave the light?

He wrote back. He wrote that the gulls sounded like knives, that the steps creaked like old knees, and that he had wanted to leave only once, the year his daughter died, and then he had stayed because the sea had no patience for grief.

The next letter was only a line.

Gone is not the same as absent.

After that, he began to understand.

Wren told him, in a manner of speaking, what she needed. Her mother was a passenger on the Meridian, a northbound freighter with a name that did not appear in any local schedule. The light would be automated in a few weeks, and the mainland had sent a notice that the tower would be closed by spring. If the lamp was not kept by a man, if the oil failed, if the storm blinded the lens, the Meridian would strike the island. Her mother would die in the water, or on the rocks, or in the ship's hold after the water came in. And Wren would not be born.

He began to prepare.

They will not, he received back. Or rather: They will try. The light will remember its keeper. Keep the old oil in the cellar. Keep the wicks cut. Keep your name on the log. The future is not a door; it is a tide. You can stand in it.

You will have me, he answered, even if I am only a letter.

The oil grew old, and the lens needed polishing, and the steps needed oiling, and every night his name went into the log. The blue letters stopped after the storm. He wrote to Wren often, but he received no answers. He left his replies in the oil room, under the table, beside the cold tea.

He told himself it was enough.

Then, on a morning in September, when the gulls were loud and the light was a thin gold thread against the water, a woman came to the lighthouse door. She was not old, but her hands had the look of work, and she carried a girl on her hip. The girl had a gap in her teeth and a small scar on her palm where a shell had cut her once.

The woman looked at Amos, and her eyes filled.

My name is Mara, she said. I was on the Meridian. I have come to thank the keeper.

Amos opened his mouth, but the girl on his hip—no, on the woman's hip—looked at him with steady brown eyes.

I'm Wren, she said.

The woman smiled. My mother says you wrote to her before I was born. I didn't believe it. But I found the letters in the oil room when I was eight. They were in a tin can under the table.

Amos looked past the woman to the sea. The light burned above them, patient and bright.

I was trying to make you possible, he said.

Wren took his hand.

You already did, she said.

Then the gulls cried, and the lens turned, and the sea went on without end.

## final text

**The Letters from the Unborn**

The first letter found Amos Thorne in the oil room, where he was cleaning the lens with a cloth that had once belonged to his father. It was folded into a square the size of a playing card and slipped under the door while the gale kept the sea from the shore. Amos had been keeper at Gull’s Reach for thirty-one years, long enough to know every trick the weather played and most of the people who came to the headland for their reasons. He had not expected a visitor from the sea.

The envelope was blue, unmarked, with no stamp and no return address. The writing inside was small and careful, as if the pen had been held by a hand that was still learning its own strength.

*Dear Keeper,*  
*I am sorry if this frightens you. I am not born yet. I know that is a strange way to begin, but it is the truest. My name is Wren, and I am writing from the future, which is only a long way off. If you will read this, I beg you to keep the light burning on the night the Meridian meets the gale.*

Amos set the note on the table and looked out the window. The sea was a gray sheet hammered by wind. He thought for a moment that it was a joke, perhaps from one of the sailors at the mainland station who had been drinking too much. Then he noticed that the paper smelled of brine and lamp oil, and that in the corner was drawn a tiny sketch of the lamp room: the table, the window, the clock, and the black cat whose picture hung there from a kindness long gone.

He laughed, though the sound made him tired.

The second letter came the next morning. By then the mail boat had not arrived, and the post had not come from the village, so there was no way to say who had placed the note in his coat pocket. It was warm to the touch.

*You have the habit of pressing your thumb to the glass before you sleep. You keep the cold tea because you believe it reminds you not to be too comfortable. You count the waves only when you are afraid. Please do not throw this away. The night is coming.*

Amos read it three times. Then he sat down and did something he had not done in many years: he wrote a reply.

*Who are you? How do you know these things? And what ship do you mean?*

He left the paper on the table.

By evening it was gone, and in its place lay another blue note.

*I am your question, Keeper. I am the answer you have not yet made. I do not know how to explain time. I only know that if the light fails, my mother dies, and I do not come into the world. I am not asking for a miracle. I am asking for duty.*

The words stayed with him.

For a week, no more letters came. Amos told himself he had imagined them, or that the storm had loosened something in his head. He polished the lens, wound the clockwork, filled the logbook in his old copperplate, and tried to be a man who did not read messages from the sea. But every morning he looked for the blue square, and every morning there was one.

They were not all words. Sometimes there were small drawings: a girl with a gap in her teeth, a boat in a storm, a lighthouse made of a crayon line, a hand reaching through fog. Sometimes there were questions. What did the gulls sound like when the wind was high? Did the stone steps ever creak? Did you ever want to leave the light?

He wrote back. He wrote that the gulls sounded like knives, that the steps creaked like old knees, and that he had wanted to leave only once, the year his daughter died, and then he had stayed because the sea had no patience for grief.

The next letter was only a line.

*Gone is not the same as absent.*

After that, he began to understand.

Wren told him, in a manner of speaking, what she needed. Her mother was a passenger on the Meridian, a northbound freighter with a name that did not appear in any local schedule. She was to cross the headland in March, when the current turned and the rocks under Gull’s Reach became teeth. The Meridian would be late. The light would be automated in a few weeks, and the mainland had sent a notice that the tower would be closed by spring. If the lamp was not kept by a man, if the oil failed, if the storm blinded the lens, the Meridian would strike the island. Her mother would die in the water, or on the rocks, or in the ship’s hold after the water came in. And Wren would not be born.

Amos asked, in his letter, if she was certain of all this.

*I am certain the way you are certain of the light when it is burning. I cannot feel my own pulse yet. I know only what must happen.*

He began to prepare.

He wrote to the harbor authority and asked for the Meridian’s expected arrival. The reply came that the ship was under the flag of a northern registry, that her passenger list was sealed, and that the keeper should attend to the light and not to the weather. He wrote again. He received no answer.

He wrote to Wren.

*What if they take the lamp from me?*

*They will not, he received back. Or rather: They will try. The light will remember its keeper. Keep the old oil in the cellar. Keep the wicks cut. Keep your name on the log. The future is not a door; it is a tide. You can stand in it.*

Amos kept the old oil, though it smelled of the last century. He kept the wicks in a tin can by the clock. He wrote his name each night in the log, and then, below it, a smaller name: Wren.

The letters continued until the sky before the storm turned the color of a bruise.

Wren asked him what he saw when he looked out from the gallery.

*The whole world, he wrote. The sea going in every direction at once.*

She asked if it was lonely.

*At night, yes, he wrote. In the day, no. The light makes company.*

*Will I have company?* she wrote.

*You will have me, he answered, even if I am only a letter.*

She wrote back:

*That is enough to be born.*

The storm came on the night they said it would.

The sea was not gray or black. It was white, and the wind had a voice, as if every wave were a man shouting a name. Amos climbed the tower before midnight, his boots ringing on the stone, the old lamp beside him. The automated light had not yet been installed, but the notice from the mainland lay in his pocket, folded like a small flag of surrender. He did not read it.

He lit the lamp.

The lens turned above him, a great wheel of fire, and for a while the room was full of rainbows on the walls. He put his hand to the glass and felt the heat of it. Then he went to the gallery and looked out.

The fog was so thick that the sea had no edge. The Meridian was nowhere to be seen. Amos stood in the wind and held the lantern in his hand, and he thought of all the people who had ever died because a light had failed: a wick too low, a lens cracked, a keeper asleep, a door left open. He thought of his daughter, who had gone out to the sea and not come back. He thought of Wren, who had not yet learned to walk, if the light held.

The gale rose.

The lamp swung. The lens caught the storm and broke it into a thousand points.

Somewhere in the fog, a ship’s horn sounded, low and desperate. Then another. Amos ran the lamp by hand when the mechanism caught, though his arms shook. He called out to the dark, not because he expected an answer, but because a keeper’s voice is part of the light.

The Meridian came into the fog like a white ghost.

She was listing hard. Her deck was empty, but a figure stood at the rail, small and dark against the spray. Amos could not tell if it was a man or a woman. He did not care. He drove the beam across her hull, again and again, a hand pushing the ship toward the water and away from the teeth.

The ship turned.

The rocks rose below them, black and waiting, and the Meridian passed over them by a distance that felt no wider than a breath. The horn sounded once more, stronger. Then the fog opened, and the sea went out of the light, and the ship was gone toward the south harbor.

Amos stood on the gallery until dawn.

His hands would not stop trembling. The lamp burned. The logbook lay open on the table below, and his name was there, followed by the smaller one.

In the south harbor, they would find the Meridian with a hull scarred and her passenger alive. Her name was Mara Vane, a marine biologist, and she would tell the rescue men that she had seen a lighthouse in the fog and that its keeper had been waving a lantern though the tower’s light was out. She would say she had dreamed of a blue envelope. She would say that when the storm broke, she felt, for one second, that someone on the shore had named her.

She did not know Amos.

Years passed.

Amos Thorne kept the light after the mainland sent its notices and after the tower had been declared unnecessary. The oil grew old, and the lens needed polishing, and the steps needed oiling, and every night his name went into the log. The blue letters stopped after the storm. He wrote to Wren often, but he received no answers. He left his replies in the oil room, under the table, beside the cold tea.

He told himself it was enough.

Then, on a morning in September, when the gulls were loud and the light was a thin gold thread against the water, a woman came to the lighthouse door. She was not old, but her hands had the look of work, and beside her walked a girl of fourteen with a gap in her teeth and a small scar on her palm where a shell had cut her once.

The woman looked at Amos, and her eyes filled.

“My name is Mara Vane,” she said. “I was on the Meridian. I have come to thank the keeper.”

The girl took a step forward. “I’m Wren.”

Mara smiled. “My mother? No—my name is your mother’s name. I’m her daughter. I’ve been coming here every year since I was eight. I found your letters in the tin can, and I have kept them.”

Wren reached into her coat and brought out a small blue envelope, soft with handling. She held it out to him.

“I didn’t believe it at first,” she said. “That a person not yet born could write. But I found your replies too. They were dated after the storm, but they were in the same tin can. How could you keep writing to me if I wasn’t born yet?”

Amos looked at the letter, then at the sea.

“I thought I was writing to make you possible,” he said.

Wren’s face changed, as if she had just felt the answer touch her skin.

“You already did,” she said.

Then the gulls cried, and the lens turned above them, and the sea went on without end.
