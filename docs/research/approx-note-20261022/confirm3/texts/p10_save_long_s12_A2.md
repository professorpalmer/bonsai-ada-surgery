# Salt and Yeast

The morning the bakers of Brinewick came within a breath of starting a war, the day began, as almost all things in that little town do, with the ferry.

Brinewick is a town that sits where a river gives up to the sea, and it looks the way it has always looked: salt-bleached, leaning, and crowded with gulls. Every morning the little ferry, the Dove, pushes out of the dock with a cough of black smoke, and the whole town wakes to the sound of that engine. The town is small enough that the postman can carry three letters and still reach the harbor before the tide turns. It is also small enough that a disagreement over bread can be seen from the hill.

On the west side of the market square stands Elias Thorne's bakery, a whitewashed building with a blue door and a sign that reads Thorne's Bakes in letters that have been repainted so many times the wood has gone soft. Across the square, under the shadow of the old lighthouse, stands Voss & Vine: a glass-fronted shop that gleams like a piece of ice, with a neon sign that says FRESH PASTRIES and, on rainy days, flickers.

Elias Thorne was sixty-three, thin as a ship's rudder, with hands the color of bread dough. He had been baking in Brinewick for thirty-one years, first under his father, then under his own name, and he had not once changed a recipe. His sourdough, he said, started a century ago — his great-grandmother had brought the starter in a mason jar when she married his grandfather. Whether that is true is a question no one in Brinewick wants to answer, because the answer would mean asking questions of the bread.

Mara Voss was thirty-one, and she had been in Brinewick only three years, and in those three years she had changed the shape of the town. She had learned to bake from a cookbook and from her grandmother, who had been a pastry chef in a hotel in the city before the hotel burned and before her grandmother came down here to bake in a little bakery that she helped open. Mara baked laminated croissants, citrus tarts, cardamom rolls with smoked sea salt, and a bread that people drove forty miles away to buy. Her shop's sign read Voss & Vine. The "Vine" part was a joke no one understood, but she kept it.

They had been rivals since the day Mara's shop opened. The first fight was over the window: Elias had put up a photograph of his rye loaves, and the next day Mara had put up a photograph of a croissant with a face. Elias had painted his sign three inches larger. Mara had painted her neon sign brighter. By the summer of the second year, they had entered what the town began to call the Loaf War, in which every Tuesday one of them sold out and the other's customers grumbled about the taste of the air.

The town had learned to enjoy it the way you enjoy a storm from a window: safely, and from a little too far away.

The Tide Festival was coming. It was held every year on the first long weekend of September, when the boats came back from the deep-water work and the harbor filled with the smell of tar and diesel and fish. Every shop in Brinewick was expected to bring one signature item to the long table in the square. Every year, the festival's judge — a retired woman named Mrs. Doolittle who had never once lost her temper, except in 2009, over a cucumber — would taste every item, and the winner would be given a trophy shaped like a fishhook and the right to put a banner over the square that said BRINEWICK'S BEST, for one year.

For Elias, the festival had always been his own, in a quiet way. The tide loaf was his answer to the sea, shaped with a single cut down the middle like a wave, and it had not changed in his father's lifetime. For Mara, the festival was the only day the town's eyes turned outward, the only day when people from the city drove in and asked, without sarcasm, where this little place was. She wanted the banner. She wanted to be the person who put Brinewick on a map, even if it was only on a banner.

Two weeks before the festival, Elias stood in his bakery at four in the morning, alone, with his hands in the dough, and thought about Rose.

Rose had died six winters ago. She had been his wife, and the one who had kept the books and the ovens and his mother-in-law's patience. The town remembered her more than they remembered him: they remembered the bread, and they remembered her hands. On the mornings after her death, Elias had kept baking at the same hour, and the bread had kept coming out, and no one had been able to say why it kept coming out, except that he was still there.

He did not change a recipe, not because he was stubborn, but because the recipes were his promise to her. A recipe was a promise, he had told his father once, and a promise is a thing you do not negotiate.

Mara worked her festival bread the week before. Her signature would be a cardamom and sea salt roll, a shape she had invented in the hotel, a roll that looked like a small wave and tasted like a storm. She made it every day, and every day it was almost perfect, and every day she threw it away. She wrote in her notebook: more butter. Less butter. The salt has to be flaked, not ground. The cardamom has to be the whole pods, broken, not ground. Her grandmother had said: the recipe is the part that isn't written down, and the rest is just practice.

Mara's sourdough was the weak part. Her bread needed a sourdough base, and her sourdough was sick. It smelled off, and it rose badly, and no matter how she fed it, it kept going flat. She tried her grandmother's recipe, and she tried the one from the book, and she tried one more that she had found in a box in her grandmother's bag. It was no use. She was two weeks from the festival, and her bread was not ready.

She went to Elias's bakery on a Thursday, because she needed a question asked, and because she was too proud to ask anyone else. The door was open. The air was thick with the smell of rye and butter, and the ovens were already breathing.

"Morning," she said.

Elias looked up from his board. "You're a long way from the lighthouse."

"I need to talk to you. About your starter."

He set down his knife. His face did not change, but something moved behind it. "My what?"

"The starter. The old one. I know you keep one in the back." She said it quickly, like a confession. "My own one is gone. I've been trying to save it and I can't, and I remember your grandmother said the old starter was the heart of this town's bread, and I was wondering if you would let me... borrow some."

Elias looked at her for a long time. Then he went to the back shelf, and he brought down a mason jar, clouded with dust, with a label that said Rose's, 1987.

"It's dead," he said. "I've had it for six years. I can't even remember when I last fed it."

"Then maybe it's not dead," she said. "Maybe it's just waiting."

He handed her the jar, and something in his face went flat, like a loaf that has cooled. "Take it. Just take it."

That night, in the back of her shop, Mara fed the jar a handful of flour and a cup of warm water, and she waited. She waited three days. On the fourth day, the jar breathed. It rose, and it smelled, and it had the deep, clean sourness of a bread that had been alive a hundred years. She made her bread that week, and for the first time since she had come to Brinewick, it was right. It was not just good. It was the best bread she had ever made, and she knew it, and it frightened her.

Because she knew where it came from.

She told Elias on a Saturday, in the market square, with the fishmongers' crates still on the ground. She told him plainly: that it was the starter, that it had worked, that she had used it, and that her bread was not what it would have been without it.

Elias listened to her. He did not interrupt. When she was done, he was quiet for a long time.

"You don't get to do that," he said.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"It wasn't about the bread. It was about Rose. That jar was hers. It was the only thing I had left that was hers, and you took it, and you made bread with it, and you called it yours."

"It's not called anything," she said. "It's a starter. It's alive, and it's been fed by everyone in this town for a hundred years. You didn't own it."

"No," he said. "I don't own it. But it was hers."

He walked away before she could answer. The next morning he did not look at her. The day after that, he did not look at her. The town noticed. The fishmongers noticed. The little square, which had been comfortable with its bicker, became uncomfortable with its silence.

Elias did not say a word to her. He did not need to. The silence was enough.

Mara took the jar back to him. She set it on his counter, in front of the ovens, where it had always been. She said, "It's yours. You don't have to use it. But it's yours."

He looked at the jar. He did not touch it.

"I'm not entering the festival," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not going to stand there and serve bread that isn't mine, and I'm not going to let you take it away from me and call it a gift."

He looked at her, and for the first time in weeks, something in his face softened. "You don't have to give it back. You don't have to do that."

"I know. But I have to."

The storm came the week before the festival. It came out of the north, big and slow, with a wind that threw the ferry against the dock and sent a sheet of rain across the square. The boats in the harbor went down in their lines, and the lighthouse flashed its light into the dark, and the whole town went quiet, the way a town goes quiet when it knows it is about to be tested.

Elias was in his bakery when the power went out. He stood in the dark, and he did not know what he was going to do, and he did not want to know, and then the door opened, and Mara came in, with her coat on and her hair wet, and she said, "I can't deliver my rolls. The wind took my van. Can I use your ovens? Just for an hour."

He looked at her. "You don't have to."

"I know. But I want to."

He let her in. She set her dough on his counter, and he turned on the backup gas, and they stood side by side, and the ovens breathed, and the bread rose, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say, and because the silence was different now, and it was the kind that was not empty, but full.

When it was over, Mara set her rolls on the counter and said, "They're not ready. But they're closer."

He looked at them. "They're good."

She looked at him. "You're being generous."

"I'm being honest."

The festival came anyway. The storm had passed, and the harbor was quiet, and the square was set with the long table and the lanterns, and the town was there, and the tourists were there, and Mrs. Doolittle sat at the head of the table with her notebook and her terrible eyes.

Elias brought his tide loaf, one cut down the middle like a wave. Mara brought her cardamom and sea salt roll, one shape like a small wave. They set them side by side on the table, and the town looked at them, and the tourists looked at them, and Mrs. Doolittle tasted them, and she sat for a long time, and then she stood, and she said, "I don't know what to do with this."

The town went quiet.

"I'm going to give it to both of you," she said.

The crowd murmured. The fishmongers laughed. The tourists clapped, and the gulls cried, and the lighthouse flashed its light into the bright sky, and the square was full of sound and smell, and the bread was warm, and the day was the best day the town had had in a long time.

But that was not the ending.

The ending was the next morning, in the market square, with the ferry coughing at the dock and the gulls crying and the two bakeries open. Elias came out with his rye loaves, and Mara came out with her croissants, and they stood side by side, and they did not look at each other, and they did not have to. The sign above his door still said Thorne's Bakes, and the sign above hers still said Voss & Vine, and the neon still flickered on rainy days, and the sign had not been changed, and the rivalry had not ended, and it was still there, but it was different now, and it was not the kind that was empty, but the kind that was full.

Brinewick is a town that sits where a river gives up to the sea, and it looks the way it has always looked: salt-bleached, leaning, and crowded with gulls. The houses climb both slopes of the headland like a flock of geese deciding which way to fly, and the streets are narrow enough that two carriages cannot pass, and the harbor is small enough that you can see every boat from the top of the old lighthouse. Every morning the little ferry, the *Dove*, pushes out of the dock with a cough of black smoke, and the whole town wakes to the sound of that engine, and the gulls cry, and the salt comes in on the wind, and the town is the town. The postman can carry three letters and still reach the harbor before the tide turns. The fishmongers can smell a lie from across the square. It is a small enough place that a disagreement over bread can be seen from the hill, and a forgiveness, if it comes, takes a while to show.

On the west side of the market square stands Elias Thorne's bakery, a whitewashed building with a blue door and a sign that reads *Thorne's Bakes* in letters that have been repainted so many times the wood has gone soft at the edges. Across the square, under the shadow of the lighthouse, stands Voss & Vine: a glass-fronted shop that gleams like a piece of ice, with a neon sign that says FRESH PASTRIES and, on rainy days, flickers.

Elias Thorne was sixty-three, thin as a ship's rudder, with hands the color of bread dough. He had been baking in Brinewick for thirty-one years, first under his father, then under his own name, and he had not once changed a recipe. His sourdough, he said, started a century ago — his great-grandmother had brought the starter in a mason jar when she married his grandfather, and it had been passed down like a heirloom ring, though no one could say with certainty whether it had survived the war or the flood of '83 or the winter his father had not come home. Whether that was true was a question no one in Brinewick wanted to answer, because the answer would mean asking questions of the bread, and the bread did not like to be asked questions.

Mara Voss was thirty-one, and she had been in Brinewick for only three years, and in those three years she had changed the shape of the town. She had learned to bake from a cookbook and from her grandmother, a sharp-eyed woman who had been a pastry chef in a hotel in the city before the hotel burned and before the grandmother came down here to bake in a little bakery she helped open. Mara baked laminated croissants, citrus tarts, cardamom rolls with smoked sea salt, and a bread that people drove forty miles away to buy. Her shop's sign read Voss & Vine. The "Vine" part was a joke no one understood, but she kept it, because the grandmother had said that a name with two parts had two halves of a promise in it.

They had been rivals since the day Mara's shop opened. The first fight was over the window. Elias had put up a photograph of his rye loaves, crust dark as a winter sea. The next day Mara had put up a photograph of a croissant with a face. Elias had painted his sign three inches larger. Mara had painted her neon sign brighter. By the summer of the second year, the town had begun to call it the Loaf War, in which every Tuesday one of them sold out and the other's customers grumbled about the taste of the air, and the fishmongers' wives kept score on scraps of paper, and no one kept score on scraps of paper for long, because everyone's bread, at some point, ended up in everyone's kitchen.

The town had learned to enjoy it the way you enjoy a storm from a window: safely, and from a little too far away.

The Tide Festival was coming. It was held every year on the first long weekend of September, when the boats came back from the deep-water work and the harbor filled with the smell of tar and diesel and fish, and the square was strung with white lanterns that caught the last of the evening light. Every shop in Brinewick was expected to bring one signature item to the long table. Every year, the festival's judge — a retired woman named Mrs. Doolittle, who had never once lost her temper, except in 2009, over a cucumber, and only for a moment — would taste every item, and the winner would be given a trophy shaped like a fishhook and the right to hang a banner over the square that said BRINEWICK'S BEST, for one year.

For Elias, the festival had always been his own, in a quiet way. The tide loaf was his answer to the sea, shaped with a single cut down the middle like a wave, and it had not changed in his father's lifetime. He made it the same way he had at forty, at thirty, at twenty-two, when he was still too proud to admit that his hands were shaking and the dough was listening. For Mara, the festival was the only day the town's eyes turned outward, the only day when people from the city drove in and asked, without sarcasm, where this little place was, and whether the bread was really that good, and whether they could have one more. She wanted the banner. She wanted to be the person who put Brinewick on a map, even if it was only on a banner that the rain would ruin by October.

Two weeks before the festival, Elias stood in his bakery at four in the morning, alone, with his hands in the dough, and thought about Rose.

Rose had died six winters ago. She had been his wife, and the one who had kept the books and the ovens and his mother-in-law's patience and, for a while, his own. The town remembered her more than it remembered him: they remembered the bread, and they remembered her hands on the crust, and they remembered that she had sung while she worked, off-key, in a song that no one had ever been able to name. On the mornings after her death, Elias had kept baking at the same hour, and the bread had kept coming out, and no one had been able to say why it kept coming out, except that he was still there, and the oven was still hot, and the starter was still alive, and the town still had to eat.

He did not change a recipe, not because he was stubborn, but because the recipes were his promise to her. A recipe is a promise, he had told his father once, and a promise is a thing you do not negotiate. His father had been quiet for a long time, and then he had said, "You'll regret that, lad," and Elias had wanted to argue, and then had not, because there had been nothing to argue, and the dough had been waiting.

Mara worked her festival bread the week before. Her signature would be a cardamom and sea salt roll, a shape she had invented in the hotel, a roll that looked like a small wave and tasted like a storm. She made it every day, and every day it was almost perfect, and every day she threw it away. She wrote in her notebook: more butter. Less butter. The salt has to be flaked, not ground. The cardamom has to be whole pods, broken, not ground. The dough has to rest longer. The steam has to be heavier. Her grandmother had said, years ago, in the last good month: the recipe is the part that isn't written down, and the rest is just practice. Mara had not understood then. She understood now, in the way that people understand things only when they lose the chance to ask for a second answer.

Mara's sourdough was the weak part. Her bread needed a sourdough base, and her sourdough was sick. It smelled off, and it rose badly, and no matter how she fed it, it kept going flat and heavy, like a loaf that had given up. She tried her grandmother's recipe, and she tried the one from the book, and she tried one more that she had found in a box in her grandmother's bag, a recipe written in a hand she did not recognize, in a town she did not know. It was no use. She was two weeks from the festival, and her bread was not ready, and the bank was watching, and her rent was due, and she could not say it out loud because saying it out loud would make it true.

She went to Elias's bakery on a Thursday, because she needed a question asked, and because she was too proud to ask anyone else, and because the door was open, and the air was thick with the smell of rye and butter, and the ovens were already breathing.

"Morning," she said.

Elias looked up from his board. "You're a long way from the lighthouse."

"I need to talk to you. About your starter."

He set down his knife. His face did not change, but something moved behind it, a small, slow thing, like a wave deciding to come in. "My what?"

"The starter. The old one. I know you keep one in the back." She said it quickly, like a confession. "My own one is gone. I've been trying to save it and I can't, and I remember your grandmother said the old starter was the heart of this town's bread, and I was wondering if you would let me borrow some."

Elias looked at her for a long time. Then he went to the back shelf, and he brought down a mason jar, clouded with dust, with a label that said *Rose's, 1987* in ink that had faded to the color of old tea.

"It's dead," he said. "I've had it for six years. I can't even remember when I last fed it."

"Then maybe it's not dead," she said. "Maybe it's just waiting."

He handed her the jar, and something in his face went flat, like a loaf that has cooled. "Take it. Just take it."

That night, in the back of her shop, Mara fed the jar a handful of flour and a cup of warm water, and she waited. She waited three days. On the fourth day, the jar breathed. It rose, and it smelled, and it had the deep, clean sourness of a bread that had been alive a hundred years. She made her bread that week, and for the first time since she had come to Brinewick, it was right. It was not just good. It was the best bread she had ever made, and she knew it, and it frightened her, because it was not entirely hers.

She told Elias on a Saturday, in the market square, with the fishmongers' crates still on the ground and the gulls still fighting over the scraps. She told him plainly: that it was the starter, that it had worked, that she had used it, and that her bread was not what it would have been without it.

Elias listened to her. He did not interrupt. When she was done, he was quiet for a long time.

"You don't get to do that," he said.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"It wasn't about the bread. It was about Rose. That jar was hers. It was the only thing I had left that was hers, and you took it, and you made bread with it, and you called it yours."

"It's not called anything," she said. "It's a starter. It's alive, and it's been fed by everyone in this town for a hundred years. You didn't own it."

"No," he said. "I don't own it. But it was hers."

He walked away before she could answer. The next morning he did not look at her. The day after that, he did not look at her. The town noticed. The fishmongers noticed. The little square, which had been comfortable with its bicker, became uncomfortable with its silence.

Elias did not say a word to her. He did not need to. The silence was enough.

Mara took the jar back to him. She set it on his counter, in front of the ovens, where it had always been. She said, "It's yours. You don't have to use it. But it's yours."

He looked at the jar. He did not touch it.

"I'm not entering the festival," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not going to stand there and serve bread that isn't mine, and I'm not going to let you take it away from me and call it a gift."

He looked at her, and for the first time in weeks, something in his face softened. "You don't have to give it back. You don't have to do that."

"I know. But I have to."

The storm came the week before the festival. It came out of the north, big and slow, with a wind that threw the ferry against the dock and sent a sheet of rain across the square and turned the lanterns into ghosts. The boats in the harbor went down in their lines, and the lighthouse flashed its light into the dark, and the whole town went quiet, the way a town goes quiet when it knows it is about to be tested.

Elias was in his bakery when the power went out. He stood in the dark, and he did not know what he was going to do, and he did not want to know, and then the door opened, and Mara came in, with her coat on and her hair wet, and she said, "I can't deliver my rolls. The wind took my van. Can I use your ovens? Just for an hour."

He looked at her. "You don't have to."

"I know. But I want to."

He let her in. She set her dough on his counter, and he turned on the backup gas, and they stood side by side, and the ovens breathed, and the bread rose, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say, and because the silence was different now, and it was the kind that was not empty, but full.

When it was over, Mara set her rolls on the counter and said, "They're not ready. But they're closer."

He looked at them. "They're good."

She looked at him. "You're being generous."

"I'm being honest."

The festival came anyway. The storm had passed, and the harbor was quiet, and the square was set with the long table and the lanterns, and the town was there, and the tourists were there, and Mrs. Doolittle sat at the head of the table with her notebook and her terrible eyes.

Elias brought his tide loaf, one cut down the middle like a wave. Mara brought her cardamom and sea salt roll, one shape like a small wave. They set them side by side on the table, and the town looked at them, and the tourists looked at them, and Mrs. Doolittle tasted them, and she sat for a long time, and then she stood, and she said, "I don't know what to do with this."

The crowd went quiet.

"I'm going to give it to both of you," she said.

The fishmongers laughed. The tourists clapped, and the gulls cried, and the lighthouse flashed its light into the bright sky, and the square was full of sound and smell, and the bread was warm, and the day was the best day the town had had in a long time.

But that was not the ending.

The ending was the next morning, in the market square, with the ferry coughing at the dock and the gulls crying and the two bakeries open. Elias came out with his rye loaves, and Mara came out with her croissants, and they stood side by side, and they did not look at each other, and they did not have to. The sign above his door still said *Thorne's Bakes*, and the sign above hers still said *Voss & Vine*, and the neon still flickered on rainy days, and the rivalry had not ended, and it was still there, but it was different now, and it was not the kind that was empty, but the kind that was full.

And in the back of Elias's bakery, on the high shelf, the mason jar sat in the dust, with the label that said *Rose's, 1987*, and it was still alive, and it was still waiting, and it was still the heart of the bread, and it was still the heart of the town.

The next Tuesday, Elias sold out before noon. The next Tuesday, so did Mara. The fishmongers' wives tore up their scorecard. No one said anything about it. The ferry came in, and the town was the town, and the bread was warm, and the day began, as almost all things in that little town do, with the ferry.
