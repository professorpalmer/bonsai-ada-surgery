# The Green Ember

Maren stirred the pot at 0600 hours, when the galley still smelled of metal and the long night had been forgotten. Beyond the galley's bulkheads, the hydroponic bay glittered under rows of white grow-lights, and four thousand people slept above her while the Meridian turned her slow course through the dark.

The ship carried four thousand souls toward a star none of them had seen. Maren was the ship's cook, and on a generation vessel that title meant something more like a priest, a doctor, a gardener, and a historian all rolled into one. She fed people. And in doing so, she kept a small part of the world they had left behind alive.

On the wall behind her station hung a tin box the size of a bread loaf. Inside were three notebooks, their pages swollen with damp and salt. They were the Salt Books, and they had passed through nine cooks in three centuries, each one adding recipes and subtracting memories. The first notebook belonged to a woman named Elena, a botanist who had sealed the seed vaults before Earth went quiet. The last was Maren's own, its margin pages filled with measurements of light and water.

Everything the crew ate grew in the bay. Wheat, potatoes, leafy greens, the ship's own gray legumes, protein vats that tasted of iron and patience. The food was honest and sufficient. It was also, Maren thought, forgetting how to be anything more.

So it was with particular care that the Council's summons reached her on a Tuesday. Kavi Okoye — the first person ever born aboard the Meridian — was turning five. They wanted a Harvest Feast in the great hall. Not the usual anniversary soup. A real meal, they said, the kind with a story attached.

Maren read the summons twice, and something in her chest tightened. A Harvest Feast required ingredients that were not just grown on the ship, but grown *by* someone. It required a garden.

Her garden was no larger than a foot-square shelf bolted to the inside of a ventilation shaft. On it, in a pot she'd carved from a ration block, stood a single tomato plant.

It was the oldest thing on the Meridian besides the hull itself. Her grandmother, a cook named Ada, had carried one of its cuttings back from the last botanical bay on Earth — the last of the old botanical bays, before the quiet. Each generation, a new cook took the cutting in, tended it through the ship's long winter, and passed it on. Maren had been four years old when Ada pressed the stem into her palm and said, "This is what it tasted like, kid. You don't taste it yet. You'll taste it later."

Maren had not been able to taste it yet. That was the problem with a tomato. It demanded its time.

For two hundred and eighty years, the plant had never fruited on the Meridian. The cuttings had produced leaves and flowers, sure — white flowers that smelled faintly of vanilla and nothing else — but fruit had never ripened. Not once. Maren had grown up reading about tomatoes the way the crew grew up reading about oceans: as a kind of myth, a place where the world used to be softer.

But something had changed this season. The bay's new nutrient recipe ran warmer than the old one, and the vent in the shaft she'd stolen her shelf from blew a steady, gentle warmth in July. The first fruit had appeared in March, small and hard as a knuckle. Then a second. And a third, hanging crooked off the stem like a secret.

Now there were five, and one of them had turned red.

When the Council's message came, Maren asked the only question that mattered. "Can it come from the shelf?"

There was a long pause on the line. "Define 'the shelf.'"

"It's a plant," Maren said. "One plant. It's been here two hundred and eighty years. I think it's ready to show everyone what it tastes like."

She didn't have to argue long. The Council, to their credit, were not the type to waste a story.

The night before the feast, Maren sat on the floor of the shaft and watched the red tomato under a borrowed grow-light. The fruit was the size of her fist, striped like a sunset. She didn't water it. She didn't touch it. She just kept company with it, the way you'd keep company with a friend who'd been sick a long time and had finally gotten well enough to talk.

She thought about Ada's hands, thick with potash, and Elena's notebook, and every cook between them who had carried a green stem through a ship's long dark and told themselves it was worth the trouble.

At midnight she wrote a single line in her own notebook: *The first ripe thing.*

The great hall was a cathedral of white panels and blue light. The whole crew had come, four thousand faces in rows of folding chairs, and the air smelled of the hydroponic bay's clean green. The tables had been laid with the bay's best: roasted legumes, wheat bread with a crust that crackled, greens dressed in an oil Maren had spent three weeks making from sunflower seeds. It was good food. Honest and sufficient.

Maren stood at the head table in a white coat that was a generation too old, the Meridian's own uniform, and she waited.

When the children were brought in first — Kavi at the head, five years old, with a gap in his teeth and a plate that had been made for him by the ship's own kitchen — the hall went quiet in the particular way that happens when a crowd understands they are being shown something.

Maren carried out her last dish.

It was a bowl, wide and shallow, and in it lay four tomato halves and the fifth, the original red one, whole, placed in the middle like a heart. Alongside it: salt, from the very first salt the crew had ever mined from the ship's own ice. Bread. Nothing else. She set it down and stepped back, because some things are not served by a cook. Some things are simply set down in front of people and allowed to be themselves.

Kavi looked at the plate. He reached out, and his small fingers came away from the tomato with a thin string of juice. He wiped it on his sleeve and looked up, and Maren understood, with a shock so sudden she had to lean on the table for support, that he had just touched the oldest thing on the ship.

He tasted the first bite.

The hall did not go quiet. It went something like stillness, the way a forest goes still when the wind stops.

Kavi chewed. He swallowed. He looked at Maren, and then he opened his mouth to say something, but what came out was not a word. It was a sound, small and bright, like a bird finding the sun in a tree.

He took another bite. Then another. And around the great hall, four thousand people who had never seen a tomato, who had grown up eating iron and patience and the clean green of the bay, reached for the bread and the salt and the red fruit.

Some of them knew what they were tasting. Some of them did not. But all of them, without exception, took another bite.

When the hall was finally quiet again — not stillness this time, but the quiet of people who had been full of something and were not sure what to do with it — Maren went to her notebook. She turned to the last blank page, and she wrote:

*For the Harvest Feast of Kavi Okoye, fifth anniversary, 312th year on the Meridian: four tomato halves, one whole, salt from the ice, bread from the wheat. The taste was not of Earth. It was of the ship, remembering. And it was enough.*

Outside the galley, the hydroponic bay glittered under its white lights. Maren walked home through it, row after row of green, and for the first time in three centuries of cooking, she could not wait to see what grew next.

# The Green Ember

Maren stirred the pot at 0600 hours, when the galley still smelled of metal and the long night had been forgotten. Beyond the galley's bulkheads, the hydroponic bay glittered under rows of white grow-lights, and four thousand people slept above her while the Meridian turned her slow course through the dark.

The ship carried four thousand souls toward a star none of them had seen. Maren was the ship's cook, and on a generation vessel that title meant something more like a priest, a doctor, a gardener, and a historian all rolled into one. She fed people. And in doing so, she kept a small part of the world they had left behind alive.

On the wall behind her station hung a tin box the size of a bread loaf. Inside were three notebooks, their pages swollen with damp and salt. They were the Salt Books, and they had passed through nine cooks in three centuries, each one adding recipes and subtracting memories. The first notebook belonged to a woman named Elena, a botanist who had sealed the seed vaults before Earth went quiet. The last was Maren's own, its margin pages filled with measurements of light and water.

Everything the crew ate grew in the bay. Wheat, potatoes, leafy greens, the ship's own gray legumes, protein vats that tasted of iron and patience. The food was honest and sufficient. It was also, Maren thought, forgetting how to be anything more.

So it was with particular care that the Council's summons reached her on a Tuesday. Kavi Okoye — the first person ever born aboard the Meridian — was turning five. They wanted a Harvest Feast in the great hall. Not the usual anniversary soup. A real meal, they said, the kind with a story attached.

Maren read the summons twice, and something in her chest tightened. A Harvest Feast required ingredients that were not just grown on the ship, but grown by someone. It required a garden.

Her garden was no larger than a foot-square shelf bolted to the inside of a ventilation shaft. On it, in a pot she'd carved from a ration block, stood a single tomato plant.

It was the oldest thing besides the hull itself that the crew had ever carried. Her grandmother, a cook named Ada, had brought a cutting back from the last botanical bay on Earth — the last of the old botanical bays, before the quiet. Each cook before her had tended the cutting through the long quiet years and passed it on. Maren had been four years old when Ada pressed the stem into her palm and said, "This is what the world used to taste like, kid. You'll taste it again someday."

Maren had tasted a tomato once, at four, in a greenhouse before the quiet, and she had never forgotten the orange-red softness of it. Everything else she had read about tomatoes was only echo. That was the problem with a tomato. It demanded its time.

No record of the plant existed in any of the ship's official logs. It was the cook's secret, one of those things that only passed from hand to hand, like a lullaby or a family name. And Ada had given Maren a rule, the same rule that had been the rule all along: "Don't let them count it. Let it grow."

For two hundred and eighty years, the plant had never fruited anywhere — not in the botanical bays, not in the old greenhouses before the quiet, not on the Meridian. The cuttings had produced leaves and flowers, sure — white flowers that smelled faintly of vanilla and nothing else — but fruit had never ripened. Not once. Maren had grown up reading about tomatoes the way the crew grew up reading about oceans: as a kind of myth, a place where the world used to be softer.

But something had changed this season. The bay's new nutrient recipe ran warmer than the old one, and the vent in the shaft she'd stolen her shelf from blew a steady, gentle warmth in July. The first fruit had appeared in March, small and hard as a knuckle. Then a second. And a third, hanging crooked off the stem like a secret.

Now there were five, and one of them had turned red.

When the Council's message came, Maren asked the only question that mattered. "Can it come from the shelf?"

There was a long pause on the line. "Define 'the shelf.'"

"One plant," Maren said. "It's been a cutting, a twig, a stubborn little green thing for two hundred and eighty years, and it's never once shown its fruit. I think this time it's ready to show everyone what it tastes like."

She didn't have to argue long. The Council, to their credit, were not the type to waste a story.

The night before the feast, Maren sat on the floor of the shaft and watched the red tomato under a borrowed grow-light. The fruit was the size of her fist, striped like a sunset. She didn't water it. She didn't touch it. She just kept company with it, the way you'd keep company with a friend who'd been sick a long time and had finally gotten well enough to talk.

The grow-light was stolen from the bay's maintenance rack, and she'd been counting its hours like a prayer.

She thought about Ada's hands, thick with potash, and Elena's notebook, and every cook between them who had carried a green stem through the long dark years and told themselves it was worth the trouble.

At midnight she wrote a single line in her own notebook: *The first ripe thing.*

The great hall was a cathedral of white panels and blue light. The whole crew had come, four thousand faces in rows of folding chairs, and the air smelled of the hydroponic bay's clean green. The tables had been laid with the bay's best: roasted legumes, wheat bread with a crust that crackled, greens dressed in an oil Maren had spent three weeks making from sunflower seeds. It was good food. Honest and sufficient.

The Council had asked for a story, and Maren had given them a ship's worth of it. Every table carried a small card with the name of the crop, the bay row, the season it had grown in.

Maren stood at the head table in a white coat that was a generation too old, the Meridian's own uniform, and she waited.

When the children were brought in first — Kavi at the head, five years old, with a gap in his teeth and a plate that had been made for him by the ship's own kitchen — the hall went quiet in the particular way that happens when a crowd understands they are being shown something.

Maren carried out her last dish.

It was a bowl, wide and shallow, and in it lay four tomato halves and the fifth, the original red one, whole, placed in the middle like a heart. Alongside it: salt, from the very first salt the crew had ever mined from the ship's own ice. Bread. Nothing else. She set it down and stepped back, because some things are not served by a cook. Some things are simply set down in front of people and allowed to be themselves.

Kavi looked at the plate. He reached out, and his small fingers came away from the tomato with a thin string of juice. He wiped it on his sleeve and looked up, and Maren understood, with a shock so sudden she had to lean on the table for support, that he had just touched the oldest thing the crew had ever carried.

He tasted the first bite, and the hall did not go quiet. It went something like stillness, the way a forest goes still when the wind stops.

He took another bite. Then another. And around the great hall, four thousand people reached — those who had grown up eating iron and patience and the clean green of the bay, and some of whom had never seen a tomato at all.

Some of them knew what they were tasting. Some of them did not. But all of them, without exception, took another bite.

When the hall was finally quiet again — not stillness this time, but the quiet of people who had been full of something and were not sure what to do with it — Maren went to her notebook. She turned to the last blank page, and she wrote: "For the Harvest Feast of Kavi Okoye, fifth year on the Meridian: four tomato halves, one whole, salt from the ice, bread from the wheat. The taste was not of Earth. It was of the ship, remembering. And it was enough."

Outside the galley, the hydroponic bay glittered under its white lights. Maren walked home through it, row after row of green breathing slowly in the dark, waiting for someone to teach it a new name. And for the first time since the quiet, she could not wait to see what grew next.
