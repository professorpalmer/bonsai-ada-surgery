# The Two Loaves

The bread had to be fed before the light did.

Margaret Calloway was already standing at the worktable when the first grey light reached the harbor road, the hour when Saltwick belonged to nobody but the tides. She held the glass jar in one palm and watched the starter swell against its rim, a slow brown breathing thing she had named Old Brine because a hundred and thirty years ago her family had crossed the sea in a ship by that name. It had outlived her husband, her daughter's wedding, two mayors, and a tabby cat of serious opinions. It was old, and it had never once let her down.

Saltwick was a small town at the end of a headland, of one harbor, one cliff, and one lighthouse on the Gull Rock point where the cliffs stopped and the sea began. It had three churches, a bookshop that sold almost nothing, a harbormaster who took his job too seriously, and two bakeries. That last fact was not a happy coincidence, and it was the first thing every newcomer was told, in the same breath, the same tone, and almost the same words: *watch the door on the second shop if you want bread, but don't stand there too long.*

Margaret mixed her flour at 5:30 the way she had since the year her father died, when she was twenty-six and the keys to Calloway's Bakehouse were heavier than she could have believed. She made the Grey Loaf in seven days — a rye the color of a winter sea, folded and rested and folded again, with a crust that cracked in the oven like old driftwood. She made the shortbread tarts that the ferry passengers carried to the mainland, the salted butter rolls that the trawlers called "the good stuff," and on festival mornings, the First Loaf, which was less a recipe than a responsibility.

The first customer was Davy, the harbormaster, who arrived at five-fifteen in a oilskin the color of the sky. He did not say good morning. He said, "The Grey, and two rolls, and don't put butter on the rolls, I'll tell you where." Behind him came the trawler men, and behind them the usual smaller people — the widow at the end of the road, the postman, a boy from the bookshop who came every morning for a plain white loaf he would pretend not to enjoy. Some customers, the town knew, came to both bakeries. They bought half from each and said it was "flavor balance," which was a polite way of saying they could not choose sides and were too sensible to be honest about it.

The two bakeries stood sixty yards apart on Harbor Lane, and between them fit the whole history of the town's modern age. Calloway's had the faded green door its founder painted in 1890, and the window painted with a simple fish that had not been repainted in decades. The Drift stood at the other end of the stretch, in the old chandler's shop, with a bright yellow door and a sign that read *Elena Voss — Bread, Salt, Butter*.

Elena had come to Saltwick four years ago, young for a baker, quiet for a baker, and — as it turned out — impossible to keep quiet about. She had opened The Drift in a town where the two bakers had shared the same street for a hundred and thirty years and had never once thought to share a recipe. Her bread was different from anything the town had eaten: a focaccia of sea salt and charred olive oil she called the Tide Loaf, a shortbread made with seaweed butter that tasted faintly of a place the town only ever saw at low tide, and a rye-studded sourdough she baked in a wood oven she had built herself from reclaimed boat timber.

The rivalry began, as these things do, with a single morning and a single line.

It was the first festival Elena had entered. The First Loaf Festival, held every year on the first day of October, when the town baked its best loaves and carried them to the lighthouse for the keeper to judge. Elena had entered a Tide Loaf, dark and glossy, with a crust that cracked like the surface of a tide pool. The town had voted, as the town always voted, and Elena had won by a nose. But the keeper, old Sam Wren, had tasted the Grey Loaf that morning before the crowd could vote, set it down, and said, very quietly, that it was the better bread.

Margaret, standing in the back of the hall with her hands behind her back, had heard both versions of the morning and chose her words the way a woman chooses a knife. "She's baked a postcard of a loaf," she told Davy at the counter the next day. "Pretty. And thin."

Elena, passing the shop on her way home that afternoon, heard the story within the hour and came back that evening. She stood in the doorway of Calloway's, rain on her coat, and said, "And yours is a funeral."

The town, which had spent three generations pretending the two women were simply neighbors, decided immediately that it preferred the new version. The rivalry became a sport. The trawler men bet on the festival loaves. The bookshop boy started a small notebook and rated the bread in pencil. Two cats were adopted and given to the bakeries like flags: a fat tabby called Bun, who slept on Margaret's flour sacks, and a grey cat called Crumb, who slept on Elena's wood oven. The neighbors made jokes about the distance between the shops — sixty yards, they said, and the whole history of the town fit in between.

The First Loaf Festival was the town's oldest tradition, older than the bookshop, older than the ferry, older than the green door. It began in 1893, in the year of the great storm, when the supply ship *Brine* came in with a cracked hull and a hold of flour, and the keeper of the lighthouse, Edmund Wren, and a young woman from the mainland named Annetta Calloway, who had come to Saltwick on that same ship, set a common bread on the lighthouse table and fed it to the whole town. The keeper's granddaughter, June, told Margaret once that her great-grandfather had kept a ledger with a single line in it, and that the line was the rule: *bread divided, none of it wasted.*

That was the story. That was the legend. That was the thing that made the festival mean something to people who had never set foot on a lighthouse.

On the morning of the festival, the sky came down.

Margaret was at the worktable with the Grey Loaf in its proofing basket when the radio in the corner of the shop began to crackle. The harbormaster's voice, thin and urgent: *Gale warning, first quarter, don't be on the water.* The light in the bakery went from grey to the color of old wool. She looked at the loaves in their baskets, at the tins of butter on the shelf, at the green door and the window fish and thought, for the first time in thirty-four years, that the day might not be what it had been.

At seven the power went out.

Not everywhere. Not along the cliff road. But the harbor road, the stretch between the harbor and the head, lost its light at the same instant, and the gas main that ran under the pavement was cut somewhere near the bookshop. Calloway's had a gas oven, and the gas line was still breathing. Elena's wood oven was a different matter entirely.

The bell above the yellow door rang at eight, and Elena Voss stood in the doorway of Calloway's Bakehouse with rain running down the front of her coat and a duffel bag over one shoulder. Her hair was wet at the temples. She had the look of a woman who had been arguing with the weather and losing.

"I didn't invite you," Margaret said.

"I know," Elena said. "You're letting me in. Same thing. I've been on both sides of that door for four years."

She set the duffel bag on the flour table. From it she took a small tin of sea salt, a jar of her own starter, and a loaf of bread still warm from a home oven that had been her only option for three days. "The gas line's out," she said. "The wood oven's out too. The storm took the flue." She looked at the grey light through the window. "My Tide Loaf is for the festival. The festival is tomorrow, or it isn't, but I was going to make it."

Margaret stood at the worktable for a long time. She was a woman who had built her life on the principle that the bread had to be fed before the light did, and she was not a woman who gave her oven to a rival. But the storm was out there, and the gas line was cut, and the two bakeries were sixty yards apart, and the town was still out in the dark, waiting for something warm.

"Put on an apron," Margaret said. "And don't touch Old Brine."

They baked through the morning and into the afternoon.

Margaret worked the Grey Loaf through its final fold while Elena shaped the Tide Loaf into a rough, salt-flecked disc. The gas oven came to life with a low roar, and the two of them stood side by side at the worktable, shoulders almost touching, while the bread did its work. The smell of the rye, old and deep, mixed with the sharp salt of Elena's loaf, and for a moment the air between them was not the cold air of the town but the warm, flour-thick air of a kitchen where no one was keeping score.

"Your mother told me about the festival," Elena said, without looking up. "She told me that in 1893, the two women baked from the same dough. One loaf for the lighthouse, one for the town."

"Annetta and Edmund Wren," Margaret said. "My great-great-great-grandmother and the keeper's father. They baked from the same starter. Old Brine's line goes back to that kitchen."

"Mine doesn't," Elena said. "I started mine myself, four years ago, from flour and water and a pinch of sea salt. I was in a city, and the bread I was making there was — I don't know — dead. It had flavor, but it had no life. I left it all behind and came to the coast because the sea gives bread a kind of patience it doesn't have anywhere else." She looked at the grey loaf in the basket. "You have patience. Old Brine has patience. That's why it works."

Margaret did not answer. She was folding the Grey Loaf, and the fold needed her, and the words needed less.

By four in the afternoon the loaves were in the oven and the storm was at its worst. The light in the bakery had gone to the color of a closed eye. The window fish watched them work from the glass. Elena stood at the counter with her hands in her apron and watched the oven door, and Margaret stood at the worktable with a towel over her shoulder and watched the loaf in the basket, and neither of them said the thing that was between them.

The bell rang at six, and the door opened, and the storm came in with the woman who came in with it.

"June Wren," Elena said.

"The keeper's granddaughter." Margaret did not turn around. "You've been here for the festival, then."

"I've been here for the festival," June said. "And for my grandfather. He's stuck on Gull Rock. The boat can't go out, and the cliff road is — well, the cliff road." She looked at Elena. "He said to bring you the loaves. He said the keeper judges at the lighthouse, and if the keeper can't come down, the loaves go up."

Elena looked at the oven. "The lighthouse is on the point. The cliff road is a single lane, and the storm is — "

"I know."

Margaret turned. "Elena, the road is — "

"I know." Elena took off her apron. "I've been on both sides of that door for four years. I'm not leaving my loaf in the oven while the town is in the dark."

She walked out into the storm. The green door closed behind her. Margaret stood at the worktable for a long time, and then she picked up the towel over her shoulder and put it over her arm, and she went to the window and watched the yellow light of a small car disappear around the bend of the cliff road.

The loaves went in at nine. Elena came back at eleven, with salt in her hair and a loaf in her hands, and she set the loaf on the table and said, "He's alive. He's eating. He said to tell you that the bread was good."

Margaret looked at her. "You drove up the cliff road in a storm."

"I drove up the cliff road in a storm," Elena said. "It's the same road every year. I know it better than you do."

The next morning the storm broke. The sea came in silver and flat, and the lighthouse on Gull Rock stood white against a sky that had no business being that blue. The town came to the harbor square, the trawler men with their bets and their coats, the bookshop boy with his notebook, the widow at the end of the road with her thermos. The two bakeries were open, and the two loaves were in the square, on a table that had been brought out from the harbormaster's boat.

Sam Wren came down the cliff road in a small car, his granddaughter in the passenger seat, and he stood in the square with his hands behind his back and looked at the two loaves for a long time.

"I was too old to judge for three years," he said. "I told the town I was too old. I was a liar. I was too frightened." He looked at Margaret. "You baked a good bread, Margaret."

He looked at Elena. "You baked a good bread, Elena."

He reached into his coat and took out a small leather notebook, worn to the color of a river stone. He opened it, and the two loaves waited.

"My great-grandfather's ledger," he said. "One line. *Bread divided, none of it wasted.* That's the rule. It was never a contest. It was a division. The two loaves, the two hands, the two names. That's what it was in 1893. That's what it was always supposed to be."

The square was quiet. The sea was flat. The two loaves sat on the table, one dark and deep, one pale and salted, and the town stood around them and listened.

Sam Wren closed the notebook. "The First Loaf," he said, "is two loaves."

He walked to the table and cut the Grey Loaf. He walked to the table and cut the Tide Loaf. He set the slices side by side on a small plate, and he ate them slowly, and the town watched, and the trawler men stopped betting, and the bookshop boy closed his notebook, and the two cats, Bun and Crumb, came up from the bakeries and sat at the feet of the table, and the green door and the yellow door stood open, and the sixty yards between them were the whole history of the town and nothing at all.

The next morning, Margaret Calloway was at the worktable before the light, and she held the glass jar in one palm, and Old Brine was swelling against its rim, and the door was open, and the grey cat was sleeping on the flour sacks, and the grey loaf was in the basket, and the tide loaf was on the shelf, and the two starters were side by side, and the air between them was warm.

The bread had to be fed before the light did.
