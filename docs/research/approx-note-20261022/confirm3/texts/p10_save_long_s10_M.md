# Salt and Flour

The lane that came off the harbor in Marrow ended, all at once, in a T-shape, and on each side of that little fork stood a bakery. They faced each other across the cracked slate of the old fish market square like two gulls that had both claimed the same ledge. The one on the left, under the sign painted with a weathered fish leaping over a loaf, was Marlow's Bakes. The one on the right, all brushed copper and clean glass, was Voss & Vine. Between them, in the middle of the square, stood the harbor bell, and every morning at six o'clock it tolled, and both bakers heard it before they were awake, and both of them, in a way they would never admit, went to work because they could not stand the thought of their rival beating them to the steam.

Marlow's Bakes had been on that corner for three generations. Its walls were the color of toast and its ovens had been stoking since the days before the lighthouse was automated, before the ferry line was cut, before the sea was this high and the boats were this few. The people of Marrow remembered the first Marlow woman, Bess, who had baked her sourdough in the hull of a beached trawler and called it "the sea remembers." Nell was forty-six, broad-shouldered in the way of women who have spent twenty years fighting a dough, with flour in her hair most days no matter how many times she wiped her forehead. Her recipes were written in a leather notebook whose pages had gone soft at the folds from being handled so often. She made rye so dark it looked like wet pebbles, a sea-salt sourdough with a crust that snapped like a dry branch, and a bread for winter that she called "the long night," a dense, spiced loaf that went into every household in Marrow when the first cold winds came in from the north. She made no laminated pastries. She made no show. She once told a young tourist asking for a croissant that "a croissant is a lie told in butter," and the tourist had laughed, and Nell had not laughed, and that was the end of it.

The rivalry did not begin with words. It began, as most rivalries in small towns do, with a small fact that neither of them could ignore.
