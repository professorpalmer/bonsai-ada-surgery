"The Unwritten"

Elias Wren had kept Skerry Light for forty-one years, and in all that time the island had taught him to trust only three things: the compass in his chest, the sea outside the window, and the sound of the lamp turning. On the first Friday of November, when the mail came in the grey oilskin bag from the mainland, there was a letter inside that he could not explain.

It was addressed in a careful hand, in ink the colour of weak tea: Mr. Elias Wren, Keeper, Skerry Light, Off the North Shore. There was no return address, no postmark, only a small drawing in the corner of a gull perched on a lighthouse and the words, written beneath it, I am not yet born.

He laughed at first, because the sea had made him careful and not foolish, but the laughter died when he opened it. Dear Mr. Wren, my name is Lillian. I am writing to you from a place before me, because I cannot reach my mother and father yet and I want someone to know I am coming. I will be born in January. I will have a mark like a gull's wing on my shoulder. I will call you Lightman when I am old enough to know words. Please keep the light turning.

Elias read it twice. The paper was dry, though the room had been damp for days. He put it in his coat pocket and climbed the tower to watch the horizon, as he always did, but that evening the light did not feel merely a warning to ships. It felt like a promise.

The next letter came with the same supply boat, two weeks later. It was written on the back of a page from a tide table, and it began, You will not believe this, but you will when the north wind brings the smell of copper. It went on to tell him that a diver named Cael would come to the island with a broken knee and a red scarf, and that his wife, Mirella, would be afraid of the dark and would hum a song without words when she was nervous. It said, Lillian will be the daughter of these two, though I have not met them yet. I only know them as facts that have not arrived.

He had no reason to believe it, and still, on the third day, a red scarf appeared in the supply boat's cabin, and a young man with a bent knee stepped onto the jetty, limping but smiling. His wife stood behind him, pale and watchful, her lips moving without sound. Elias gave them both a room in the keeper's cottage and made soup. The woman's humming was faint, but it was there, like a thread pulled from the sea.

By December the letters were coming without fail. They did not arrive from a post office; sometimes they were inside the oilskin bag, sometimes wedged beneath the door, once folded into the pocket of a wool sweater Elias had left on the bench. The handwriting grew neater, then smaller, then more urgent. Lillian wrote that she had seen the lighthouse in her dreams before she could speak. She wrote that the lamp room smelled of brass and rain and that the old keeper would talk to the gulls, and she wrote, Please do not let the light go out on the night I arrive. The sea will be black, and the fog will be so thick that the horn will sound like a voice from under the water.

Elias began to prepare. He cleaned the brass until it gleamed. He mended the east window that had leaked for years, because Lillian had said she would look for the open seam where the light spilled. He bought a small chair from the village and placed it by the stove. He wrote his own letters, though he did not know where to send them. Dear Lillian, he wrote in the logbook. If you are not here yet, be patient. If you are already there, forgive the old man for believing a letter from a child who has not drawn her own breath. I will keep the lamp.

The board from the mainland wrote, too. Your service will end in spring, they said. The light will be automated. You may return to the village if you choose. Elias read the letter and put it in the drawer beside Lillian's first page. He thought of the word automated, a machine that would turn without hands, without watching, without the slow human attention of a man listening to the sea. He thought of Lillian's words, I need someone to know I am coming, and he decided not to go.

When January came, the sea grew mean. The supply boat did not come on the scheduled day. Elias climbed the tower at dawn and saw the light sweep out into a grey nothing, and he felt for the first time that the beam was not enough. At noon he wrote the final letter he intended to send into the past, if that was possible. Dear Lillian, the fog is thick. I am leaving the east window open. If you are born this day, look for the green glass. If you are not, look for me, and know that I have been waiting.

In the afternoon the fog lifted only enough for a shape to emerge from it: a small boat, not the supply craft, but a fishing skiff, its hull low and dark. A woman was in it, alone. Her hair was wet and her face was tight with exhaustion. She called up to the tower, and her voice was raw, not with words but with a kind of strain Elias recognized from his own old body. He took the rope and pulled her onto the jetty.

She did not look at the sea. She looked at the lighthouse, as if it had summoned her. Her hands were shaking. I can't go in there, she said. The dark is too deep. Elias did not ask her name. He took her arm and led her to the cottage, to the small room where the chair waited by the stove. He made hot water. He opened the east window, and the light came in, green and thin, across the floor.

That night the storm broke against the rock. The lamp turned. The horn sounded from the shore, and once, when the wind dropped, the sound was exactly like a voice under water. Elias stood in the doorway of the lamp room, his old heart knocking, and watched the beam go out and come back. He thought of the letters, the careful hand, the gull drawing, the name Lillian written before the name had any reason to exist. He did not know if he was mad, or blessed, or both.

At three in the morning the woman screamed. It was not a scream of terror; it was the sound of a world being born. Elias knelt, and with hands that had handled rope and glass and cold metal, he did what he could. The child came first with a cry, then with a gull's wing mark on her shoulder. The woman held her to her chest and whispered a name over and over. Lillian. Lillian. Lillian.

In the morning the storm had passed. The sea was silver and quiet. Elias set the little girl on the chair by the window, where the east light fell on her face. She looked at him with eyes so dark and new that he could not tell if he was seeing the future or only the present. She opened her mouth, and the first word she spoke, clear as a bell, was Lightman.

He sat down, and the chair creaked, and he wept, though he would have told anyone it was the salt air. After that the letters stopped. The cottage was quiet, the lamp turned, and the island did not change except that Elias's life became shorter and longer at the same time. Lillian grew. She climbed the tower with Cael's red scarf around her neck. She learned the names of the gulls, the hours of the fog, the sound of the horn when the sea was angry. She learned that the keeper's heart was not strong, and that he loved her with the steady, wordless attention of a man who had spent his life making sure that something would not be lost in the dark.

Years later, when Elias had finally gone to the mainland and died in a small bed with the window open, Lillian was nine. She found a box under the floor of the lamp room, filled with the logbook and the letters that had been written before she existed. She read them slowly, and then she opened a page where the old keeper had written, I will keep the light.

She climbed back to the window and looked out at the sea. The lighthouse still turned, though the hands had changed and the light was now made of glass and electricity. A ship passed below, safe. She placed her palm against the cold glass, and she whispered, Thank you, Lightman.

Below, in the water, something like a gull's shadow rose once, then was gone.

## final text

**The Unwritten**

Elias Wren had kept Skerry Light for forty-one years, and in all that time the island had taught him to trust only three things: the compass in his chest, the sea outside the window, and the sound of the lamp turning. On the first Friday of November, when the mail came in the grey oilskin bag from the mainland, there was a letter inside that he could not explain.

It was addressed in a careful hand, in ink the colour of weak tea: Mr. Elias Wren, Keeper, Skerry Light, Off the North Shore. There was no return address, no postmark, only a small drawing in the corner of a gull perched on a lighthouse and the words, written beneath it, *I am not yet born.*

He laughed at first, because the sea had made him careful and not foolish, but the laughter died when he opened it.

*Dear Mr. Wren,* it began. *My name is Lillian. I am writing to you from a place before me, because I cannot reach my mother and father yet and I want someone to know I am coming. I will be born in January. I will have a mark like a gull’s wing on my shoulder. I will call you Lightman when I am old enough to know words. Please keep the light turning.*

Elias read it twice. The paper was dry, though the room had been damp for days. He put it in his coat pocket and climbed the tower to watch the horizon, as he always did, but that evening the light did not feel merely a warning to ships. It felt like a promise.

The next letter came with the same supply boat, two weeks later. It was written on the back of a page from a tide table, and it began, *You will not believe this, but you will when the north wind brings the smell of copper.* It went on to tell him that a diver named Cael would come to the island with a broken knee and a red scarf, and that his wife, Mirella, would be afraid of the dark and would hum a song without words when she was nervous. It said, *Lillian will be the daughter of these two, though I have not met them yet. I only know them as facts that have not arrived. I will be here before I am born, and I will need you to be here when I am.*

He had no reason to believe it, and still, on the third day, a red scarf appeared in the supply boat’s cabin, and a young man with a bent knee stepped onto the jetty, limping but smiling. His wife stood behind him, pale and watchful, her lips moving without sound. Elias gave them both a room in the keeper’s cottage and made soup. The woman’s humming was faint, but it was there, like a thread pulled from the sea.

By December the letters were coming without fail. They did not arrive from a post office; sometimes they were inside the oilskin bag, sometimes wedged beneath the door, once folded into the pocket of a wool sweater Elias had left on the bench. The handwriting grew neater, then smaller, then more urgent. Lillian wrote that she had seen the lighthouse in her dreams before she could speak. She wrote that the lamp room smelled of brass and rain and that the old keeper would talk to the gulls, and she wrote, *Please do not let the light go out on the night I arrive. The sea will be black, and the fog will be so thick that the horn will sound like a voice from under the water.*

Elias began to prepare. He cleaned the brass until it gleamed. He mended the east window that had leaked for years, because Lillian had said she would look for the open seam where the light spilled. He bought a small chair from the village and placed it by the stove. He wrote his own letters, though he did not know where to send them.

*Dear Lillian,* he wrote in the logbook. *If you are not here yet, be patient. If you are already there, forgive the old man for believing a letter from a child who has not drawn her own breath. I will keep the light.*

The board from the mainland wrote, too. *Your service will end in spring,* they said. *The light will be automated. You may return to the village if you choose.* Elias read the letter and put it in the drawer beside Lillian’s first page. He thought of the word *automated*, a machine that would turn without hands, without watching, without the slow human attention of a man listening to the sea. He thought of Lillian’s words, *I need someone to know I am coming*, and he decided not to go.

When January came, the sea grew mean. The supply boat did not come on the scheduled day. Elias climbed the tower at dawn and saw the light sweep out into a grey nothing, and he felt for the first time that the beam was not enough. At noon he wrote the final letter he intended to send into the past, if that was possible.

*Dear Lillian,* he wrote, *the fog is thick. I am leaving the east window open. If you are born this day, look for the green glass. If you are not, look for me, and know that I have been waiting.*

In the afternoon the fog lifted only enough for a shape to emerge from it: a small boat, not the supply craft, but a fishing skiff, its hull low and dark. A woman was in it, alone. Her hair was wet and her face was tight with exhaustion. She called up to the tower, and her voice was raw, not with words but with a kind of strain Elias recognized from his own old body. He took the rope and pulled her onto the jetty.

She was Mirella, though Elias had not yet seen her in the light of the tower, only in the letters. She did not look at the sea. She looked at the lighthouse, as if it had summoned her. Her hands were shaking.

“I can’t go in there,” she said. “The dark is too deep.”

Elias did not ask her name. He took her arm and led her to the cottage, to the small room where the chair waited by the stove. He made hot water. He opened the east window, and the light came in, green and thin, across the floor.

That night the storm broke against the rock. The lamp turned. The horn sounded from the shore, and once, when the wind dropped, the sound was exactly like a voice under water. Elias stood in the doorway of the lamp room, his old heart knocking, and watched the beam go out and come back. He thought of the letters, the careful hand, the gull drawing, the name Lillian written before the name had any reason to exist. He did not know if he was mad, or blessed, or both.

At three in the morning the woman screamed. It was not a scream of terror; it was the sound of a world being born. Elias knelt, and with hands that had handled rope and glass and cold metal, he did what he could. The child came first with a cry, then with a gull’s wing mark on her shoulder. The woman held her to her chest and whispered a name over and over.

*Lillian. Lillian. Lillian.*

In the morning the storm had passed. The sea was silver and quiet. Elias set the little girl on the chair by the window, where the east light fell on her face. She looked at him with eyes so dark and new that he could not tell if he was seeing the future or only the present. She opened her mouth, and the first word she spoke, clear as a bell, was *Lightman.*

He sat down, and the chair creaked, and he wept, though he would have told anyone it was the salt air.

She would ask him about the letters when she was older, and he would tell her the truth as he understood it: some people arrive in the world by blood, some by accident, and a few by the stubborn faith of a keeper who once wrote to a child before the child had a name. Lillian did not ask for more. She simply placed her small hand on the logbook and said, *Then I was never alone,* and he had no answer for that but to nod.

After that the letters stopped. The cottage was quiet, the lamp turned, and the island did not change except that Elias’s life became shorter and longer at the same time. Lillian grew. She climbed the tower with Cael’s red scarf around her neck. She learned the names of the gulls, the hours of the fog, the sound of the horn when the sea was angry. She learned that the keeper’s heart was not strong, and that he loved her with the steady, wordless attention of a man who had spent his life making sure that something would not be lost in the dark.

Years later, when Elias had finally gone to the mainland and died in a small bed with the window open, Lillian was nine. She found a box under the floor of the lamp room, filled with the logbook and the letters that had been written before she existed. She read them slowly, and then she opened a page where the old keeper had written, *I will keep the light.*

She climbed back to the window and looked out at the sea. The lighthouse still turned, though the hands had changed and the light was now made of glass and electricity. A ship passed below, safe. She placed her palm against the cold glass, and she whispered, *Thank you, Lightman.*

Below, in the water, something like a gull’s shadow rose once, then was gone.
