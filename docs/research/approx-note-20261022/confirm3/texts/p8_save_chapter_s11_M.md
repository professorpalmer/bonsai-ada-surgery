**Chapter One**

The first thing a newborn in Cistral notices is the smell. Not the brine that licks the city's stone, nor the sulfur that vents through the deep cracks in the shell, but something older and more patient—the musk of something enormous and breathing just beneath the cobblestones.

By the time children learn to walk, they learn the rhythm. A slow thud, then another, three seconds apart. The heartbeat of the beast beneath. The elders called it "the Hum," and every child is taught to press an ear to the flagstones of the temple square and wait.

Kessan was seven when the Hum changed.

The change came on a morning of pale rain, which had made the city slick with mist. Kessan, on his way to the shell-fish market with her mother's copper ladle balanced on one hip, felt it first as a tickle in her teeth. Then the thud came, wrong. Not a thud at all. A drag, like the sound a great animal makes when it shifts its weight on a bed.

She stopped in the street. Around her, the market was winding down. A fishwife was scraping the last of the morning's catch into a barrel. The stall-keepers were pulling their canvas over their wares. Nobody was looking at the sky. Nobody, it seemed, had noticed that the sky was moving.

Kessan looked up.

The clouds had not parted. The clouds had shifted, the way a hand shifts when it presses against the back of a chair. And where the clouds were parting, the far edge of the city was visible, the wall of the great shell, its surface a topography of scales as large as houses. The scales had been still for Kessan's whole life. The city had been still.

It was not still anymore.

A crack opened in the sky's skin. Not a crack in the sky—a crack in the shell. A black line, thin as a spider's thread, ran from the western wall to the edge of the horizon. Kessan watched, unable to move, as a scale, the size of a cathedral, began to loosen.

The people of Cistral had a saying that went back three hundred years, back to the time their grandparents' grandparents had first landed on the shell and hammered their first nails into the bone. The saying went: "The shell does not move. We move. We are the ones who are always walking."

The shell was walking now.

Kessan's mother found her standing in the middle of the street, the copper ladle forgotten in her hand. The woman's face had gone white. Kessan could not see it in her own face, but she felt it there, in the hollow of her throat, like the first cold note of a song she had not yet learned.

"Kessan. Come away. Come away now."

She did not come away. She was too busy watching the scale come loose.

The sound was not like any sound she had ever heard. It was a groan, a long, deep, animal groan that rose from somewhere in the earth itself and passed through every stone, every beam, every bone in every body in Cistral. Kessan felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the copper ladle. She felt it in the fishwife's barrel, which cracked down the middle and sent the morning's catch spilling out in the rain.

The scale came off the shell.

It fell. Not quickly. Not fast. But it came off, and it fell, and it fell for a long, long time.

In the street, people stood still. Kessan's mother stood still. The fishwife stood still, her apron open, the fish sliding out of her hands onto the wet stones. The stall-keepers stood still, their canvas pulled half-way over their wares.

And then the scale hit the sea.

The sound it made was the loudest sound Kessan had ever heard. A boom, a thunder, a sound that went through her body and out the other side. The sea rose in a wall of white. The city shuddered. The Hum, the great heartbeat she had known since she was a baby, stopped.

In the silence, she could hear the rain. She could hear the fish sliding on the wet stones. She could hear her own mother's voice, very far away, saying her name.

But beneath the rain, beneath the fish, beneath her mother's voice, there was something else. Something small and soft and new. A sound like breathing. A sound like a great animal, waking up.

The Hum was back. But it was different. It was faster. And it was coming from closer.

Kessan looked down at the ground. At the cracks in the cobblestones. At the flagstones of the temple square.

And for the first time in the three hundred years since the first people had landed on the shell, she understood: the city was not built on the back of a giant turtle.

The city was the turtle's back.

And the turtle had finally, after ten thousand years of sleep, begun to think.

The first thing a newborn in Cistral notices is the smell. Not the brine that licks the city's stone, nor the sulfur that vents through the deep cracks in the shell, but something older and more patient—the musk of something enormous and breathing just beneath the cobblestones.

By the time children learn to walk, they learn the rhythm. A slow thud, then another, three seconds apart. The heartbeat of the beast beneath. The elders called it "the Hum," and every child is taught to press an ear to the flagstones of the temple square and wait.

The city rose in terraces along the slope of the shell, a city of grey stone and copper, built into the curve of the great beast like a barnacle. At its center stood the Temple of the Deep, its spire a crooked needle pointing at the sky. And all around, the people of Cistral went about their lives, mending nets, trading fish, and never once thinking to look up.

Kessan was seven when the Hum changed.

The change came on a morning of pale rain, which had made the city slick with mist. Kessan, on her way to the shell-fish market with her mother's copper ladle balanced on one hip, felt it first as a tickle in her teeth. Then the thud came, wrong. Not a thud at all. A drag, like the sound a great animal makes when it shifts its weight on a bed.

She stopped in the street. Around her, the market was winding down. A fishwife was scraping the last of the morning's catch into a barrel. The stall-keepers were pulling their canvas over their wares. Nobody was looking at the sky. Nobody, it seemed, had noticed that the sky was moving.

Kessan looked up.

The clouds had not parted. The clouds had shifted, the way a hand shifts when it presses against the back of a chair. And where the clouds were parting, the far edge of the city was visible, the wall of the great shell, its surface a topography of scales as large as houses. The scales had been still for Kessan's whole life. The city had been still.

It was not still anymore.

A crack opened in the sky's skin. Not a crack in the sky—a crack in the shell. A black line, thin as a spider's thread, ran from the western wall to the edge of the horizon. Kessan watched, unable to move, as a scale, the size of a cathedral, began to loosen.

The people of Cistral had a saying that went back three hundred years, back to the time their grandparents' grandparents had first landed on the shell and hammered their first nails into the bone. The saying went: "The shell does not move. We move. We are the ones who are always walking."

The shell was walking now.

Kessan's mother found her standing in the middle of the street, the copper ladle forgotten in her hand. The woman's face had gone white. Kessan could not see it in her own face, but she felt it there, in the hollow of her throat, like the first cold note of a song she had not yet learned.

"Kessan. Come away. Come away now."

She did not come away. She was too busy watching the scale come loose.

The sound was not like any sound she had ever heard. It was a groan, a long, deep, animal groan that rose from somewhere in the earth itself and passed through every stone, every beam, every bone in every body in Cistral. Kessan felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the copper ladle. She felt it in the fishwife's barrel, which cracked down the middle and sent the morning's catch spilling out in the rain.

The scale came off the shell.

Below, far below, the sea spread out in every direction, a dark and endless thing that the people of Cistral had always believed was all there was to the world. The scale hung in the air for a moment, a great dark plate turning slowly, catching the pale light of the morning. And then it began its fall.

It fell. Not quickly. Not fast. But it came off, and it fell, and it fell for a long, long time.

In the street, people stood still. Kessan's mother stood still. The fishwife stood still, her apron open, the fish sliding out of her hands onto the wet stones. The stall-keepers stood still, their canvas pulled half-way over their wares.

And then the scale hit the sea.

The sound it made was the loudest sound Kessan had ever heard. A boom, a thunder, a sound that went through her body and out the other side. The sea rose in a wall of white. The city shuddered. The Hum, the great heartbeat she had known since she was a baby, stopped.

In the silence, she could hear the rain. She could hear the fish sliding on the wet stones. She could hear her own mother's voice, very far away, saying her name. And she could hear the sea, far below, the sea that had always been the floor of her world, beginning to pull back.

But beneath the rain, beneath the fish, beneath her mother's voice, there was something else. Something small and soft and new. A sound like breathing. A sound like a great animal, waking up.

The Hum was back. But it was different. It was faster. And it was coming from closer.

Kessan looked down at the ground. At the cracks in the cobblestones. At the flagstones of the temple square.

And for the first time in the three hundred years since the first people had landed on the shell, she understood: the city was not built on the back of a giant turtle.

The city was the turtle's back.

And the turtle had finally, after ten thousand years of sleep, begun to think.

And in the streets of Cistral, in the hollow of a seven-year-old girl's throat, something new and terrible began to breathe.
