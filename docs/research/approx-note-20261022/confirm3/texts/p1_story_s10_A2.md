The light at Crag Point turned once every ten seconds, a slow silver needle stitching the black water to the cliff. Elias Voss had tended it for thirty-one years, and in all that time the island had sent him nothing but weather, gulls, and the occasional postmarkless letter. On the morning the first of them arrived, the tide was low enough to show the teeth of the rocks, and a single envelope lay on the landing as if it had been placed by a patient bird.

It had no return address. The paper was thin and smelled of salt and orange peel. Inside, a hand wrote in neat blue ink:

Dear Mr. Voss,
I am not yet born. Please do not be afraid. I am only a promise the sea has made, and promises can be frightened. My name will be Liora. I do not know what I look like, but I know that when I am old I will remember this island, and I would like you to remember me now.

Elias read the note three times. He looked toward the postbox, the path, the water. Nothing moved. He thought of the old lighthouse jokes, the drunk who once sent a bottle with a message asking for a dance. He put the letter in his shirt pocket and climbed the spiral stairs to trim the lamp.

By the second week, four more letters had come.

The second said, "The fog bell is wrong. It should sound one note, not two. If you oil the hinge before Thursday, it will sing like a ship's bell in a dream." Elias had not intended to repair the bell. The third said, "There is a crack in the north window. The cold finds it before breakfast. I would like to know the color of the sky when you fix it." He patched the glass and wrote, in his own private journal, that the sky was the pale green of old money.

The fourth letter was shorter. "Mother is afraid of being alone. Father is afraid of forgetting. I am afraid of never having been loved before I exist. But I think the light will love me, because the light loves everyone it touches."

Elias sat on the cold landing with the letter in his hand and felt, for the first time in years, that the lamp was not merely a duty but a conversation.

The next letter described a woman. "She has brown hands and a scar on her left thumb from a knife. She hums when she is thinking. She is not yet here, but I have met her in a place I cannot name. Her name is Maren." The following letter described a man. "His name is Callum. He laughs too loud and carries tools in a bag that always needs more space. He will come with Maren when the ferry is calm."

Elias did not believe in prophecy, but he had lived long enough to know that the sea had a way of making the impossible look like bad weather. He began to keep the letters in a tin box beside the oil drum. Each evening he read them aloud to the lamp, as if the flame might answer.

Then, one morning in late autumn, the ferry appeared.

Maren came off the boat first, carrying a canvas bag and a look that seemed to search the lighthouse for a reason to be afraid. Behind her, Callum carried tools, a coil of rope, and the easy confidence of a man who believed he could repair anything that would let him. They had come to the island, Elias learned, to restore the old keeper's cottage and to spend a year among the rocks before deciding whether to stay.

"I read the letters," he said to Maren, though he had not written anything. The words were in his head, and he could not keep them there. "About your daughter."

Callum frowned. "Daughter?"

"A girl. Liora. She wrote to me. She says she is not yet born, but she knows your names."

Maren went still. The wind moved the ropes on the landing. For a long moment, no one spoke. Then she touched the scar on her left thumb and said, "I have had a dream of this island. I have felt a voice inside me saying my name, but I did not believe it."

Elias felt the ground shift beneath him. "When?" he asked.

"I do not know," Maren said. "I have not told anyone."

He wanted to tell her about the letters, the precise details, the orange-peel smell, the fog bell. Instead he handed her a key to the cottage and said, "The cottage is yours if you like. I can show you where the water is safe and where it bites."

The weeks after that were strange and warm. Liora's letters continued to arrive, sometimes two or three at a time, sometimes only one. They grew more specific. "Mother is painting the blue room," one said. "Father has taught the gulls the name of the light. I can hear the lamp from here, though I do not know if it is a sound or a feeling."

Elias began to notice the small changes in Maren. She moved more slowly. She paused at the window and pressed her hand to her stomach, not with fear but with attention, as if listening to a radio station only she could find. He told himself he was being foolish. He was an old man with a lamp, not a prophet. And yet when Maren asked him to sit with her in the cottage kitchen because the quiet was too large, he did not refuse.

One evening, as the sun set behind the headland, Liora's letter arrived. It was longer than the others and written in a hand that trembled slightly.

Dear Mr. Voss,
I am coming. Not yet, but soon. I can feel the sea pulling at me, and the light is turning its silver wheel. I am afraid I will be small and the world will be too big. If I do not remember you, please remember that I once wrote to a keeper who lived with a lamp and a cat and a tin box full of letters. Tell her I was not alone before I was born.

Elias read it and then wept, which was a thing he had not done since his wife died.

The storm came three days later.

It rose out of the west with a black roof of cloud and a sound like the sea was trying to enter the land by force. The ferry was canceled. The light had to be kept. Elias climbed the stairs in the rain, the wick low, the oil sputtering in the cold. At the top, he trimmed the lamp and watched the beam sweep the water. Below, he saw the cottage window lit, and he understood that Maren was inside.

The wind shook the tower. A crack appeared in the glass, a thin line like a river on a map. The fog bell began to ring, though the fog had not come. One note, then two, then a wild overlapping crash. Elias remembered the letter. He descended, soaked, and found the bell's hinge dry and swollen. He forced it open with a file and oiled the metal until the bell settled into a single clear voice.

When he returned to the lamp, the storm had begun to bend. Maren stood at the cottage door, pale and trembling, holding her stomach. Callum was beside her, his face gray.

"It is coming," Maren said. "Now."

Elias should not have been there. He was a keeper of light, not a witness to birth. But the sea had given him the letters, and the letters had given him the name, and the name had become a hand reaching toward him through time. He took Maren's hand and walked with her inside, while Callum ran for the boat to fetch help.

The delivery was long and hard. Rain hammered the roof. The lamp outside turned, and every time its light passed over the cottage window, Elias saw his own face in the glass, older than he wished. Maren screamed, and he thought of the letter: the sea pulling, the world too big, the keeper who lived with a cat. He held her hand and said, "I know. I am here. You are not alone."

When the baby came, she was small and red and furious with the first cry the sea could not silence. Callum, returning from the boat, took the child into his arms. Maren looked up at Elias. Her eyes were wet and bright.

"Liora," she said.

Elias nodded.

In the days that followed, the island settled into a new rhythm. The cottage was repaired. The fog bell sang one clear note. The lamp turned its patient wheel. Maren and Callum decided to stay, and Elias found that he did not mind sharing the island, because the light had a larger purpose now. It was no longer only a warning to ships; it was a promise kept.

One evening, Liora, two years old, sat on the landing with a bucket in her lap and watched the beam sweep the water. She was brown-eyed and serious, with a small scar on her left thumb from a bread crust she had pressed too hard against. When Elias sat beside her, she did not look at him.

"What are you writing?" he asked, holding out the tin box.

She pointed at the letters. "Her," she said.

"Who?"

The girl considered this with the gravity of a small creature handling a great mystery. "The one before me," she said. "She says I should keep the light."

Elias opened the tin box and placed Liora's hand on top of the old letters. The paper was soft as cloth, and the blue ink had faded to the color of twilight. Outside, the lamp turned. Inside, the child listened. And somewhere beyond the reach of time, a promise was finally made real.

**Before the Name**

The light at Crag Point turned once every ten seconds, a slow silver needle stitching the black water to the cliff. Elias Voss had tended it for thirty-one years, and in all that time the island had sent him nothing but weather, gulls, and the occasional letter. On the morning the first arrived, the tide was low enough to show the teeth of the rocks, and a single envelope lay on the landing as if placed by a patient bird.

It had no return address. The paper was thin and smelled of salt and orange peel. Inside, a hand wrote in neat blue ink:

Dear Mr. Voss,  
I am not yet born. Please do not be afraid. I am only a promise the sea has made, and promises can be frightened. My name will be Liora. I do not know what I look like, but I know that when I am old I will remember this island, and I would like you to remember me now.

Elias read the note three times. He looked toward the postbox, the path, the water. Nothing moved. He thought of old lighthouse jokes. He put the letter in his shirt pocket and climbed the spiral stairs to trim the lamp.

By the second week, four more letters had come.

The second said, “The fog bell is wrong. It should sound one note, not two. If you oil the hinge before Thursday, it will sing like a ship’s bell in a dream.” Elias had not intended to repair the bell. The third said, “There is a crack in the north window. The cold finds it before breakfast. I would like to know the color of the sky when you fix it.” He patched the glass and noted that the sky was the pale green of old money.

The fourth letter was shorter. “Mother is afraid of being alone. Father is afraid of forgetting. I am afraid of never having been loved before I exist. But I think the light will love me, because the light loves everyone it touches.”

Elias sat on the cold landing with the letter in his hand and felt, for the first time in years, that the lamp was not merely a duty but a conversation.

The next letter described a woman. “She has brown hands and a scar on her left thumb from a knife. She hums when she is thinking. She is not yet here, but I have met her in a place I cannot name. Her name is Maren.” The next said, “His name is Callum. He laughs too loud and will come when the ferry is calm.”

Elias did not believe in prophecy, but he had lived long enough to know the sea could make the impossible look like bad weather. He kept the letters in a tin box beside the oil drum.

Then, in late autumn, the ferry appeared.

Maren came off the boat first, carrying a canvas bag and a look that seemed to search the lighthouse for a reason to be afraid. Behind her, Callum carried tools, a coil of rope, and the easy confidence of a man who believed he could repair anything. They had come to the island to restore the old keeper’s cottage and spend a year among the rocks before deciding whether to stay.

“I read the letters,” Elias said to Maren, though he had not written anything. The words were in his head, and he could not keep them there. “About your daughter.”

Callum frowned. “Daughter?”

“A girl. Liora. She wrote to me. She says she is not yet born, but she knows your names.”

Maren went still. The wind moved the ropes on the landing. For a long moment, no one spoke. Then she touched the scar on her left thumb and said, “I have had a dream of this island. I have felt a voice inside me saying my name, but I did not believe it.”

Elias felt the ground shift beneath him. “When?” he asked.

“I do not know,” Maren said. “I have not told anyone.”

He wanted to tell her about the letters, the precise details, the orange-peel smell, the fog bell. Instead he handed her a key to the cottage and said, “The cottage is yours if you like. I can show you where the water is safe and where it bites.”

The weeks after that were strange and warm. Liora’s letters continued, sometimes two at a time, sometimes one. They grew more specific. “Mother is painting the blue room,” one said. “I can hear the lamp from here, though I do not know if it is a sound or a feeling.”

Elias began to notice small changes in Maren. She moved more slowly. She paused at the window and pressed her hand to her stomach, not with fear but with attention, as if listening to a station only she could find. He told himself he was being foolish. And yet when she asked him to sit with her in the kitchen because the quiet was too large, he did not refuse.

One evening, as the sun set behind the headland, Liora’s letter arrived. It was longer than the others and written in a hand that trembled slightly.

Dear Mr. Voss,  
I am coming. Not yet, but soon. I can feel the sea pulling at me, and the light is turning its silver wheel. I am afraid I will be small and the world will be too big. If I do not remember you, please remember that I once wrote to a keeper who lived with a lamp and a cat and a tin box full of letters. Tell her I was not alone before I was born.

Elias read it and then wept, which was a thing he had not done since his wife died.

The storm came three days later.

It rose out of the west with a black roof of cloud and a sound like the sea was trying to enter the land by force. The light had to be kept. Elias climbed the stairs in the rain, the wick low, the oil sputtering in the cold. At the top, he trimmed the lamp and watched the beam sweep the water. Below, he saw the cottage window lit, and he understood that Maren was inside.

The wind shook the tower. A crack appeared in the glass, a thin line like a river on a map. The fog bell began to ring, though the fog had not come. One note, then two, then a wild overlapping crash. Elias remembered the letter. He descended, soaked, and found the bell’s hinge dry and swollen. He forced it open with a file and oiled the metal until the bell settled into a single clear voice.

When he returned to the lamp, the storm had begun to bend. Maren stood at the cottage door, pale and trembling, holding her stomach. Callum was beside her, his face gray.

“It is coming,” Maren said. “Now.”

Elias should not have been there. He was a keeper of light, not a witness to birth. But the sea had given him the letters, and the letters had given him the name. He took Maren’s hand and walked with her inside, while Callum ran for the boat to fetch help.

The delivery was long and hard. Rain hammered the roof. Every time the lamp’s light passed the window, Elias saw his face in the glass, older than he wished. Maren screamed, and he thought of the letter: the sea pulling, the world too big. He held her hand and said, “I know. I am here. You are not alone.”

When the baby came, she was small and red and furious with the first cry the sea could not silence. Callum, returning from the boat, took the child into his arms. Maren looked up at Elias. Her eyes were wet and bright.

“Liora,” she said.

Elias nodded.

In the days that followed, the island settled into a new rhythm. The cottage was repaired. The fog bell sang one clear note. The lamp turned its patient wheel. Maren and Callum decided to stay, and Elias found that he did not mind sharing the island, because the light had a larger purpose now. It was no longer only a warning to ships; it was a promise kept.

One evening, Liora, two years old, sat on the landing with a bucket in her lap and watched the beam sweep the water. She was brown-eyed and serious, with a small scar on her left thumb from a bread crust she had pressed too hard against. When Elias sat beside her, she did not look at him.

“What are you writing?” he asked, holding out the tin box.

She pointed at the letters. “Her,” she said.

“Who?”

The girl considered this. “The one before me,” she said. “She says I should keep the light.”

Elias opened the tin box and placed Liora’s hand on top of the old letters. The paper was soft as cloth, and the blue ink had faded to the color of twilight. Outside, the lamp turned. Inside, the child listened. And somewhere beyond the reach of time, a promise was finally made real.
