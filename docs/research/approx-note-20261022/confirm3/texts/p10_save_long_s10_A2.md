# The Salt and the Sourdough

The town of Brinewatch had two bakers, and it was a fact of the town the way the tide was a fact, the way the fog was a fact, the way the cliff path was a fact. Every morning for three hundred and sixty days, the two doors on Gull Street opened at different times by design, so that the women and men of Brinewatch could choose, and for forty years the choosing had been settled, and then the choosing had not been settled, and now, at last, it was settled in a way that would surprise the people who had never expected surprise in a place that ran on bread.

Mira Solano's bakery had a sign that read SOLANO, in letters painted by her father's father, a man who had come from the mainland with nothing but a recipe box and a grudge against humidity. The letters were worn smooth on one edge by the wind that came off the water, and the 'O' had a crack in it that Mira kept meaning to fix and had never fixed, because the crack made the letter look like a mouth, and her father said that a bakery should be able to speak. The bread inside the glass cases was round, brown, and unapologetic: sourdough loaves with skins like old leather, a seeded rye the color of wet slate, and in the winter months a dense, dark pumpernickel that could split a spoon. The smell of Mira's bakery was the smell of her whole childhood, a smell that had a history to it, something almost old enough to be a memory itself.

Across the street, with a single lamppost between them and a stretch of cracked pavement that the town council had promised to fix since before either of them had been born, was the bakery that had arrived in Brinewatch seven years earlier. Its sign read HALE & BRIGHT, in a clean modern face that a printer on the mainland had chosen from a catalogue, and the windows of the place were so clean they made the morning light look like an accident. Tomas Hale was a young man from the city, a baker who had once worked in a place with eleven ovens and a chef who measured everything in grams to the decimal, and when he had come to Brinewatch he had told the town that he intended to bring them "bread that was honest about its own technique." People had laughed at that, the way people laugh at things when the thing is not yet a joke.

For six of those years the two bakers had ignored each other with the discipline of people who have agreed not to have a war. Mira's regulars would cross Gull Street without looking at the door on the other side, and Tomas's regulars would do the same on his side. The only point of contact the town shared was the Saturday market, and even that was a thing of sharp, careful distance. They did not nod. They did not say good morning. They did not, on the three separate occasions in which the two of them had been within ten feet of each other at the market, pretend that the other did not exist, because the third time the pretense had cracked and they had both looked at each other with an expression of such open, mutual irritation that an old woman selling mussels had whispered to her husband that the bakers were going to settle something, and her husband had said it was only bread, and he had meant it, and it had been true, and it was, in the end, still true.

It was a Tuesday in November, the kind of November that the sea treats as personal insult, when the storm that came out of the west began to change the shape of the week. The fog that usually rolled in slow and thick and forgiving came instead in hard sheets, and the light that followed, when it came, was a flat white that made the cliff face look like a photograph of itself. Mira's sourdough starter, the thing she called Grandfather, which she had kept in a jar on her back wall for eleven years, was behaving badly. It was collapsing in its own smell, a sour, almost sweet rot, and it was not a thing that could be fixed by patience the way other things were fixed. It was a thing that needed heat and time and a decision, and Mira had a decision already in mind, but it was the kind of decision that could not be taken before the town had seen it.

The decision was that the market was on, and that she was going to bring her best loaf to the market, because the market was the one place the town still went that did not involve a decision about which baker they loved, and because the other baker, she told herself, was not her rival, but a man who had come to a small town and decided that the small town needed a lesson, and she would not be the one to be taught in public. The loaf she was making was a thing she had been building toward all winter: a round of dark flour with a crumb that was dense and open at once, a thing that would not break under the weight of the season, a thing that would hold up to the kind of weather that was about to make the whole town feel small. It was the bread, in a sense, of Brinewatch itself: dark, weathered, and not a little proud of it.

She was at the point of shaping when the door on the other side of the street opened, and a man walked out in a wet coat carrying a paper bag that was clearly not from his own bakery, and she watched him cross the street and stand in front of her door with the bag in his hand and the rain coming down on him, and she did not open the door, and he did not knock, and he stood there for a while, and then he said, "I don't want to sell you anything. I want to ask you something, and if you don't want to answer you can say no and I will leave and you will not have to think about it."

She opened the door, because the rain was in her face and she was too tired to be clever, and the man who was standing there was not Tomas Hale.

It was the town clerk, a heavy-set man named Gideon Marsh who had the sort of face that looked like it had been made by the town itself, by all the years of small meetings and small decisions, and he was not holding the paper bag so much as he was holding it out of his hands, and inside the bag was a loaf, a plain round of pale flour, a thing that looked like nothing in particular, and he said, "The market is off," and then, "They're not coming today. The road is closed and the market is off and the only thing I can do is tell you what the other man is doing, and I think you should know before you go out and make the thing you've been making for four months and then find out the whole town is sitting in their kitchens eating what they had to make for themselves, and that it would be a small kindness if you brought the loaf you made to the market on the day it was supposed to be on, even if the market is off, because that's what the thing is for, and that's the only thing I can say, and I'm sorry, and I'll leave you to it."

He left, and the door was shut, and the rain was still coming, and the loaf was still in the oven, and the thing Mira did next was to put on her coat and walk across the street to the bakery that had a sign in clean modern letters, and she did not knock, and she did not ask, and she stood in the doorway of the place that smelled of butter and something sharp and clean and something else she did not have a name for, and she looked at Tomas Hale, who was standing in front of a bench with a tray of something small and round on it, and he looked up, and she said, "The market is off."

And he said, "Yes."

And she said, "Why are you making small rounds."

And he said, "Because I don't know what the town is going to do today, and I don't know if I should be making a loaf, and I don't know if I should be making a loaf and bringing it to the market on a day that isn't on, and I've been thinking about that for about three hours, and I don't know what to do, and I'm sorry for not telling you, and I'm sorry for not telling anyone, and I don't know what else to say."

She did not know what to say either. She had a loaf that was almost done. He had a tray of small rounds that were not quite done, and the two of them stood there in the doorway of a bakery that was not her bakery and a bakery that was not his, and the rain came down, and the street was empty, and the town was somewhere out in the white and the water and the dark, and she said, "You could bring them to my place."

And he said, "Your place is across the street."

And she said, "It is a street, and a street is a place, and you could bring them to my place across the street, and you could leave them in the case, and you could come back after the storm, and I would not eat them, and you would not eat them, and they would be there, and the town would come back and they would be there, and that would be the thing."

And he said, "That's not how it works."

And she said, "It is how it works. That is how it works here. That is how it has worked here for forty years, except when it hasn't, and the excepting is the point, and the excepting is the thing that makes it work, and you could bring the rounds across and leave them and come back after, and I would not say a word to you about it, and you would not say a word to me about it, and the town would know, because the town knows these things, the way the town knows the tide and the fog and the crack in the 'O' and the way the street is a street and the street is a place and the place is the thing."

He stood there for a while, and then he picked up the tray, and he walked across the street, and she let him in, and he set the tray in the case next to the dark loaf, and the small rounds were pale and neat and a little underdone, and the dark loaf was almost done and a little overdone, and the two of them stood in the case light, and she said, "You could make the next ones a little darker."

And he said, "You could make the next ones a little lighter."

And they both laughed, and it was a short laugh, and it was the first sound either of them had made in that year that was not a sound of work or argument or the small, careful distance they had been keeping between themselves, and the laugh stayed in the air for a second, and then the storm came up around them and the lights went out and the two of them were standing in the dark in a bakery that was not theirs and the two of them were the only ones who knew where they were, and she said, "The generator is in the back," and he said, "The generator is not in the back," and they both went to the back, and they found it, and they turned it on, and the lights came back, and the case was full, and the storm was outside, and the town was out in the white, and the two of them stood in the light and they did not say anything for a while, and then she said, "You could come back after."

And he said, "I will come back after."

And she said, "I will leave the case open."

And he said, "I will close the case after."

And they walked to the door, and she opened it, and the rain was in her face again, and the street was empty, and the two of them were at the doors of two bakeries across the street from each other, and the sign on her side had a crack in the 'O', and the sign on his side was clean, and the street between them was cracked pavement that the council had not fixed, and the two of them were at the doors, and they did not cross the street, and they did not say good evening, and they walked away in opposite directions, and the storm took the road and the market was off, and the next day the road was open, and the market was on, and the two of them were at their stands, and the rounds were still in the case at the back of her bakery, and she had not eaten them, and he had not come back yet, and he came back at the end of the week, and he closed the case, and he left, and the town ate the rounds, and the town ate the loaf, and the town said nothing, because the town had always known, the way the town knows the tide and the fog and the crack in the 'O', and the way the street is a street and the street is a place and the place is the thing, and the thing is the thing that was made by the two of them across the street from each other, and the thing was good, and the thing was enough, and the thing was the only thing that had ever needed to be, and it was, in the end, the only thing that was.
