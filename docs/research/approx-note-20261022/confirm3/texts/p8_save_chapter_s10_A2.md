# Chapter One
## What the Turtle Knew Before We Did

The heartbeat of Mam was not a sound so much as a weather.

Wren could feel it first in her soles, the way farmers feel rain coming in the ache of old wounds. Then it crept up her shins and settled in her teeth, in the fillings, in the soft place behind her sternum, until for a moment she had two pulses, one hers and one beneath the whole city. Then the breath. The long, slow draw that lifted Thal — all its chimneys and its laundry lines and its six hundred houses — two feet into the air, and then the exhale, and the settling, and the creak of every floorboard in the carapace-plain.

One, two. One, two. The whole city rose and fell on the old thing's lungs.

No one in Thal had ever seen Mam's head. The ridge line of the carapace fell off into gray mist about two miles from the city wall, and below that the mist did not clear. The elders called it the Down, and children who were told not to look down too long were not, in truth, entirely wrong about what they saw. But they were too young to understand the weight of it, that the mist was not the sea but the absence of one — that the world ended in a way no map in the tower could name, because the map had to end somewhere.

Mam was a creature so large that her name had become a sound the city made to itself, the way children call for a mother in their sleep. *Mam, mam*, they whispered to the cobblestones, and the cobblestones hummed, and that was the whole liturgy of Thal.

Wren's hands knew the city better than her mind did. She was a ridge-keeper, one of the three climbers who walked the carapace-plain's spine, the long line of raised scales where the streets did not run but where the houses leaned in and out of the ridges. Her job was simple in the way that tides are simple: to read the shell.

The shell spoke. That was the first thing they taught you at the guildhouse, before the ropes and the climbing hooks. It spoke in the way the ridges swelled, in the heat that moved under the plates like weather moving over a field, in the faint, almost imagined smell of salt and iron that came up through the grates of the under-halls. A ridge that ran cold was a ridge that wanted to shed. A ridge that ran warm held a scale ready. A ridge that ran warm in places and cold in others —

That was the one you climbed on your knees.

Wren had been climbing the spine for four years when the ridge began to run warm in the wrong places.

She found it on the third watch, when the city below had gone dark and the only light was the pale glow of the shed-scales, the flakes of old shell that the turtle shed in autumn and that the city collected and ground into glass and sold to the ports. The shed-scales were Thal's coin, its wine, its walls. Wren had grown up with the smell of them, sweet and faintly green, like the inside of an oyster.

This morning, climbing the north spine with a cup of tea in one hand and the ridge-reading slate in the other, she noticed that the warmth had moved.

It should have started at the low point, where the ridge began to crown, and crept toward the tip. Instead, it pooled in a crescent around a scar she had known all her life: a pale line in the shell, no wider than a finger, where an old scale had split clean off and the raw shell beneath had sealed over years ago. The scar had been dry and dull. Now it was warm. And it was spreading.

She set down her cup and pressed both palms to the ridge.

The heartbeat was still there. One, two. One, two. But it had a new quality, like a drum played in a large empty room. And beneath it — she would not have sworn to it on a oath, but she would have sworn it anyway — a second rhythm. Slower. Lower. Something that was not the old thing's heart at all but something moving up out of the dark below the shell, a pulse older than the pulse of a living thing.

Wren stood very still. The mist at the edge of the world breathed, and for a moment she could have sworn she saw something in it: a shape, or the memory of a shape, or the place where a shape had been and was no longer.

She called the watch below, her voice thin in the cold, and told them what she had found.

They came with the lanterns. They came with the bell.

The Council came at dawn, with their soft shoes and their maps that had to end somewhere, and they listened to the ridge-keeper's report the way people listen to a neighbor's story about a crack in the wall: with the careful, polite hope that it might not be a crack after all.

"The shell is warm," Wren said, "and the scar is warm, and the scar is spreading. And there is a second beat."

Old Marren, the first Councilor, rubbed his chin. "Second beats happen. You know the shell. Sometimes a plate shifts and the whole ridge feels it. It's the turtle turning in her sleep."

"It is not a turning," Wren said. "A turning moves from the low ridge to the high. This is not moving. It is waiting."

The Council looked at one another. They looked at the ridge. They looked at the mist.

And it was then that the scar cracked — not along the old line but across it, a new fracture, thin as a hair, running out of the pale scar in a direction Wren could not name, a direction the ridge had never cracked before and would never crack again, because it was not the direction of the shell but the direction of something beneath the shell, something that had been patient in the dark for longer than the word *longer* had any claim to.

Wren pressed her hands to the ridge again, and this time the heartbeat stopped.

Not faded. Not slowed. Stopped, as if the whole old thing, the whole vast and breathing world, had held its breath.

In the silence, the city of Thal — all its chimneys and its laundry lines and its six hundred houses — could hear it.

And in the mist at the end of the world, something answered.
