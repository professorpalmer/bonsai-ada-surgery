# Chapter 1: The Tides Beneath

The first thing anyone who grew up in Tessara knew was that the sky breathed.

Every morning, the sun rose not over a horizon but over the rim of a shell that stretched to the edge of the world — the vast, barnacled dome of a turtle older than the kingdoms that called its back a city. The old maps had no name for the creature. The scholars had three theories, and none of them survived contact with the truth.

The second thing anyone knew was the rhythm.

Down in the shell, in the deep water where no light reached, something turned. Slow as a millstone, vast as a continent, and it turned the city on its back with a sound like the earth clearing its throat. The whole of Tessara swayed. The houses creaked in their moorings. The bells of the High Spire rang without being struck, because their clappers had long ago learned the rhythm, and had begun, the elders said, to answer it.

Mira Chen stood at the rail of the Shellwalk and felt the morning's turn begin.

She was nineteen and had the look of a girl raised by the sea and the sky, and not by people. Her hair was the color of kelp, tied with a strip of shark-skin. Her mother had been a diver — a Shell-dive, the official term, which meant she went down through the vents and came up with whatever the deep had to offer — and Mira had learned the old names before she learned to read: the names of the currents, the names of the bones, the names of things that should not be named.

"Another turn," she said to no one.

"Another turn," her apprentice agreed. The boy was twelve, with a nose too wide for his face, and he was already reaching for the tide-tables and scratching out the day's heading with a charcoal stub. He called himself Jem, which was a lie; the real name was something from the deep, something his mother had given him before she went down and did not come back.

They had been walking for an hour along the Shellwalk, past the tide-farmers and the shell-pearl carvers, past the great market where the salt from the vents was sold by the fistful and the fish that lived in the cracks of the shell were sold by the scale, past the houses of the Underward, who lived in the shadow of the dome and had never seen a full sun.

The turn was coming. It came every day, at roughly the same hour, and the people had built their whole lives around it — their work, their prayers, their sleep.

Mira watched the sky. The rim of the world was black and gold, and beyond it, the water. The water of the Deep, which was the only thing the people of Tessara feared and the only thing that gave them life. It was the water that the turtle swam through, and it was the water that the city swam through with it, because the turtle did not know it was carrying the city, and the city did not know that it was a back.

They knew enough.

They knew that the vents gave them salt and heat. They knew that the bones, the great white pillars that rose from the shell like the ribs of a ship, were the turtle's own bones, broken and worn smooth over centuries, and that the Underward had built their entire faith around the bones as if they were the fingers of a god reaching up from the sea. They knew that the sky breathed, and that the turn came, and that on the day the turtle stopped, the city would sink.

That last thing was not written in any map.

That last thing was the thing that had no name.

"Jem," she said, "look."

The first thing anyone who grew up in Tessara knew was that the sky breathed.

Every morning, the sun rose not over a horizon but over the rim of a shell that stretched to the edge of the world — the vast, barnacled dome of a turtle older than the kingdoms that called its back a city. The second thing anyone knew was the rhythm.

The bells of the High Spire rang without being struck, because their clappers had long ago learned the rhythm, and had begun, the elders said, to answer it.
