## final text

The lighthouse had a voice like a held breath. Each night, Ewan lifted the lantern and the old glass shivered, and the beam swept the black water, a slow blade cutting the dark. He knew every creak of the stair, every leak in the west wall, every gull that nested in the lamp room. He had kept Blackrock Light for thirty-one years, long after his wife had gone, long after his sons had stopped coming up the hill. Now the house stood alone on the headland, and he slept with one ear listening for the sea.

The first letter arrived on a morning when the fog came in so thick that the light seemed to shine inside a white room. Ewan found it on the doorstep, folded into a square, with no stamp and no address except a small line of ink in a hand he almost recognized: From Mirelle. He turned the paper over. The seal was a wick, drawn with blue thread. He should have dropped it in the bin for junk, but something in the single word, Mirelle, made his hand pause.

Inside, the words were plain. I am writing from a place you have not yet visited. I am not yet born, but I know the sound of your lamp, the way you clear the salt from the wick, the small cough you keep hiding from the weather. Please do not tell anyone. Save the trawler on the thirteenth. If you do, I may be able to stay in the world a bit longer. It had no signature, only a date: four months out.

He read it again while the kettle screamed on the stove. A trawler on the thirteenth. He checked the logbook for the date and found no entry yet. The thirteenth of November lay ahead, cold and flat, but the sea had no respect for calendars. He laughed once, a dry sound, and wrote no reply. The next morning another letter waited by the lamp room door. It carried no stamp, no postmark, and no smell of the postman's coat, only a faint tide-scent, like rain on the rockpool. This one said, You will find the black stone on the north path. Do not move it.

He went out at noon to look for the black stone, telling himself he was checking the path for winter ice. On the north path, half buried under grass, lay a flat black stone, shaped like a thumbprint. It was cold, and when he tried to lift it, the letters seemed to press against his skin: Do not move it. He left it where it was. That night, a foghorn moaned off the harbor, and he climbed the tower to test the lens. The light swept the water and, for a moment, the beam seemed to lean toward the shore as if searching for something small and far away.

On the thirteenth, a trawler named the Petrel came too close to the rocks. The wind dropped without warning, and her mast shuddered against the rigging. Ewan saw from the gallery that her crew were lashed to the wheel, their faces white in the spray. He lit the flare and sent a boat out with the old rope, the one that had a knot named after his mother. The Petrel caught it and turned, and for a long time no one spoke on the deck. Then the captain climbed into the boat and said, I have a girl named Mirelle. We were supposed to name her after my mother.

The captain's wife was asleep below, her hand on her belly, and the baby was due soon. Ewan did not know if Mirelle was already in the world or still only a name waiting in her mother's mouth. He told himself it was a coincidence, a cruel joke of the sea, and he put the letters in the drawer beside the oil and the spare fuses. But the drawer would not stay shut. Each time he opened it, the papers smelled of lamp oil and new wool, and the ink had begun to dry in a pattern like a wick, a small flame drawn over and over.

More letters came. They described the lighthouse before it had been repaired, a crack in the second-floor window that would open only after the spring storm. They described a fox that climbed the service stairs and sat beneath the lamp. They described a woman with grey hair who would arrive with a child in a blue coat and ask for shelter. Ewan began to count the days. He counted the creaks, the gulls, the number of times the beam touched the north rock. He stopped dreaming of his sons and started dreaming of a room he had never seen: a room with a window, a small bed, and a lamp that burned green.

One night, in the week before the spring storm, a crack opened in the second-floor window. The next morning, a fox sat beneath the lamp, watching him as if it had been invited. On the third day, a woman with grey hair arrived in a small car, followed by a boy in a blue coat who refused to run. The woman said she was from the village, that her husband had died, and that the boy had begun speaking of a lighthouse keeper before he had ever seen one. She looked at Ewan with eyes that were tired but not unkind. His name, she said, was Mirelle.

Ewan stood in the doorway and felt the house settle around him, as if every board remembered the weight of the name. The boy looked at the tower and said, I have been waiting for you to come down. I have been writing to you, though you do not know it yet. The woman sighed and touched his shoulder. Ewan looked at the letters in his hand, now soft at the edges, and understood that the voice in them had not come from the future alone. It had come from a life that was being built, slowly, from the words themselves.

The woman was not Mirelle, nor was the boy. Mirelle was the name the letters had made, and the letters had made it true. Ewan took the boy upstairs and showed him the lamp. The boy touched the glass and said, It feels like someone is listening. Outside, the wind changed. The sea became a flat grey field, and the light moved across it, not as a warning but as a road. Ewan thought of all the ships that had been saved, and all the ones that had not, and he wondered how many small lives were hidden inside the dark, waiting for a name.

The boy stayed the night, then the week, then the month. The woman said he was well, but his eyes kept moving, as if he were watching something beyond the window. Ewan wrote letters of his own, though he had no address to send them to. He wrote about the oil, the wind, the first frost on the grass. He wrote, If you are not yet, then do not hurry. I will keep the light. The paper did not carry away, but each morning a new letter appeared, older than the day before, in ink that smelled of sea salt and a baby's milk.

Autumn came. The boy began to speak in dreams of a lighthouse that had no keeper, and Ewan knew the letters were fading. One evening, after the light had been swept and the wick trimmed, he found a final note on the windowsill. It was written in a child's hand, but the words were sure. I am coming into the world tomorrow. I am sorry for the time I borrowed from you. Thank you for being the lamp that knew my name before I had a face.

He did not tell the woman, or the boy, or the village priest, who came the next day with flowers and questions. He put the note in his coat pocket and went down to the lamp room. The glass was cold. The oil burned low. He lit the wick and watched the flame take shape, a small green thing at the center of the dark. Then he climbed to the gallery and turned the great lens. The beam rose, steady, and carried out over the water like a promise kept to a name not yet born.

Years later, when the lighthouse was automated and the cottage sold, the boy grew into a man with quiet eyes. He returned in winter to the north path where the black stone still lay. He knelt beside it and said, Goodbye, Ewan. The wind answered, or maybe it only moved the grass. From the tower, no light burned, but if a ship passed close that night, the sea seemed to glow for a moment, green and steady, as if someone unseen had turned a lamp and whispered, I have you.
