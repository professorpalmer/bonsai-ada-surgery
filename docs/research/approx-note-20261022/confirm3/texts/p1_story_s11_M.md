## final text

Tomás Marlow had kept the Gull Rock light for eleven years before the first letter arrived. The island rose from a black sea of rocks, and the lighthouse stood like a white bone at its center. He climbed one hundred and fourteen stairs each morning, trimmed the wick, and watched the beam sweep the water. No visitors came. No mail came, except the small official reports from the mainland. Still, on a gray Tuesday in November, a single envelope lay under the door. It was soft, folded neatly, and smelled of salt, coldness, and memory from the old winter dark.

He unfolded it carefully at his small desk, where the sea wind pressed against the glass. The paper was pale blue, and the handwriting was small, slanting, and certain. Dear Mr. Marlow, it began, I am writing to you from a morning that has not yet happened. I have not seen your face, but I have read the names of the ships that have passed your light. My name is Eleni. Please do not be afraid of me, because I am not yet in the world. She asked him to keep the lamp burning no matter what the tide did.

Tomás set the letter on the table and listened to the sea. A prank seemed unlikely, because no one on Gull Rock had the time or reason to write such a thing. He examined the envelope. There was no stamp, no return address, no postmark, only a thin line of wax near the fold. The wax was pale yellow, and it looked old though it had been pressed that morning. He turned the paper over and found a second line of text. I will write again when the fog lifts, and soon you will know I am true before the first snow falls.

The fog lifted on Thursday, after a long night of gray silence. Tomás climbed the stairs with his hands trembling, but he found no second letter. On Friday morning, however, a folded page was wedged beneath the door latch. This time it predicted that the oil drum would leak when the tide was highest, and that a seagull would break against the south window. Both things happened. He watched the seagull strike the glass and heard the oil drip into the basin. He wrote only one word, yes, and tucked the page beside the lamp in the pale morning light.

Letters came after that, though not in the mail. They appeared on the kitchen table, on the landing rail, in the oil room, and once inside a tin of candles. Eleni told Tomás that her words traveled by light, because the lighthouse had been a place of waiting for longer than anyone could prove. She asked him to keep the south window cracked, even though it admitted salt and cold. Do not make the island whole, she wrote, or I will not arrive. The letters asked nothing else, only that he keep his voice low when the sea was angry.

For a while, Tomás thought Eleni was a trick of solitude. A man alone on an island might imagine a companion, especially when the gulls screamed like children and the clock in the kitchen ticked too loudly. Yet the letters continued, and they did not contain the kind of nonsense a lonely mind would invent. They described the weather with precision. They named the ships. They knew the exact shade of the sea at dawn, and they warned him about the cracks in the lower steps. He began to trust them, but never enough to stop asking questions all day.

In February, a letter arrived with no ink, only the faint smell of rain. It said a storm would come with a north wind that had no name, and that a boat called the Selkie would be driven toward the rocks. She told Tomás to sound the bell three times before the first wave broke. If he did, a sailor in a red coat would live, and his name would be added to the ledger of the sea. Tomás read the words again and again, feeling the sea rise in his chest while the lamp burned steady above the island.

The storm came three nights later. The sky became a bruise of black and green, and the waves climbed the rocks like living things. Tomás climbed to the gallery and struck the bell as the Selkie appeared in the mist, her hull splitting against the stone. He saw the sailor in the red coat leap into the water, and then the boat was gone. In the morning, a body was found on the eastern shore, alive and wrapped in an oilskin, muttering about a light that had come to save him. He did not ask what she meant by it.

After the storm, Eleni wrote again. Her hand had changed. The letters were longer, more careful, and they spoke of a village on the mainland, a school with a blue door, and a girl who would be born in a winter much like the one Tomás was living. She said the sailor had survived to become a father, and that his daughter would carry the name of the sea in her eyes. She asked him to keep the logbook open, because she would one day read it and find herself in the margins with ink that glowed faintly at night.

Tomás began to leave a chair set by the lamp each evening, as if a visitor might arrive. He wrote back in the logbook, though he knew no one would read it until much later. He wrote: I hear you, Eleni, though I cannot see you. I do not understand how a letter can come from a life not yet begun, but I am here, and the light is steady. The words did not vanish, which surprised him, because he had expected the island to laugh him into silence and he was grateful, in the end, for that strange comfort.

Years passed, and the world beyond the island changed. Steam gave way to engines, and engines gave way to radio. The mainland sent notices that the lighthouse would be automated in spring, and a young assistant would take over the tower. Tomás read the notices as if they were weather warnings. Each spring, he waited for the first letter of the year, and each spring it came, thinner and more urgent. Eleni told him she was coming close to being born. She described a hospital room with a window facing the sea, and a baby crying in the blue dawn.

Tomás did not ask her to explain the impossible, because he had learned that some things only reveal themselves through doing. He kept the south window cracked. He polished the lens until it sparkled. He answered each letter with a line of ink in the logbook: the light is still here. The words were not enough, but they became a kind of prayer. He began to believe that a person could exist in a single act of waiting, even before the body arrived in the world and the sea would carry her letters to the living when they were needed.

The last letter came in the autumn of his seventy second year. Tomás's knees ached, and his hands no longer climbed the stairs without pain. The lighthouse still turned, but now it did so by itself. The young assistant had long since left, and the island was quiet except for the wind. Eleni wrote that she was coming to Gull Rock. I am old enough to stand, she said, and I have kept your logbook safe. I want to see the light one last time before you forget my name and I have waited a long time for this morning.

A gray boat appeared in the cove on the morning the letter had promised. It came low in the water, and a woman stood at its bow, though she did not need to stand, because the sea had grown calm. She wore a green coat and a scarf, and her hair was white. She did not look like a stranger. She looked like someone who had been expected for a very long time. Tomás felt the island narrow beneath his feet, and the lighthouse beam seemed to pause in the glass and he understood that she had come at last.

She stepped onto the rocks and walked slowly toward the tower. Tomás met her at the base, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Then she took a small blue page from her coat and placed it in his hand. It was one of his own answers, written in the logbook, and it smelled faintly of salt and coldness. Eleni looked up at the light and smiled. I came from a life that was not yet real, she said, but you made it possible and now I am old enough to carry the light home to the sea again.
