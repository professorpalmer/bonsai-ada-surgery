Chapter One  
The Line Remembers

The rain came into Cold Hollow in long gray sheets, as if the sky had forgotten which way the mountain faced and was simply letting everything fall at once. It ran down the steep sides of Kettle Ridge, threaded the bare spruce, and slid along the abandoned rail line before it found the town. Elias Thorne stood at the kitchen window of the small house above the post office, watching the water bead and slide down the glass, and for a moment he thought the whole valley had been washed clean.

It had not.

Cold Hollow sat in a narrow fold of rock and pine, with the church steeple, the old grist mill, and the rusted water tower marking the places where people had once believed the mountains would let them live. The main street was so short that a man could walk from the post office to the general store and back again before his knees ached. Elias’s knees ached. He was sixty-nine, retired, and his body had begun to keep its own ledger of every winter he had not paid attention to.

He had not worn a uniform in twenty-one years, but he still woke before dawn and listened. Trains no longer ran through Cold Hollow. The Kettle Ridge Branch had been closed since the last freight car ground itself out of the valley with a scream that half the town remembered and the other half had invented. Still, Elias heard the mountain moving in the language of rails and signals, of gravel under pressure and the soft tick of metal cooling in the rain. He had spent forty-three years riding as a conductor on that line, and a man does not simply retire from noticing things.

He set the coffee pot on the stove without turning around. The water was cold in the rain. The house smelled of wet wool, paper, and the faintly sweet dust that clung to old books. Across from the stove hung the conductor’s badge he had taken off his neck the day the railroad company changed its name, then changed it again, then folded its books and left town for good. The badge was brass, dark with age, and his name was worn almost smooth: E. THORNE. He had not polished it in years, but he had never hung it lower than it wanted to be.

It was the light that drew him from the kitchen.

He had seen it once before, on the abandoned siding near the trestle, a faint red glint where the old signal lantern had been left hanging. The town had never quite agreed whether it was a lantern at all. Some said it was a scrap of red canvas from a wreck. Others said it was a piece of paint from a boxcar the children used to chase across the ballast. Elias had always called it what it looked like: a warning.

He pulled on his boots.

The front door opened onto a world of falling water. The yard below the house was a tangle of ferns and broken stone, and the road to the siding dipped behind it like a tongue pushing out of a mouth. Elias pulled his coat over his shoulders and stepped into the rain. His umbrella was in the hall by the door, but he had not opened it. The walk was short, and the rain, though steady, was not yet cold enough to bite.

By the time he reached the old rail line, the road had disappeared under a thin skin of mud. The rails ran north from the abandoned freight house, disappearing into the trees before they curved up toward the trestle. The grass had grown between the ties in dark green ribs, and the old signal post stood like a finger pointing at the sky. The red glint was there. It had not moved. Elias knew that because the glint had been exactly where it had been every morning for the last ten years.

He stopped.

A piece of paper lay in the mud between the rails.

It was not much: a strip of white, the kind of paper used for old train tickets, creased at one corner and soaked at the other. Elias bent to look at it, and for the first time since he had come out, the rain seemed to fall more heavily around him. He did not touch the paper. He simply held his breath and read it in the gray light.

Kettle Ridge Branch.  
Last Run.  
11:58.  
No. 817.

The number was printed in faded blue, the ink softened by age. The paper itself was not new. It had been damp for years, perhaps decades. Elias had seen tickets like it in the station office, clipped to ledgers and pinned to the wall beside photographs of crews that were long dead. He knew what the Last Run had meant. It was not a real schedule. It was a rumor the passengers had made into a habit, a story told about the final train that ever left Cold Hollow before the winter the line was closed.

He had been on that train.

He had been the conductor.

A sound behind him made him turn.

At first he thought it was the rain. Then he saw the car.

It was a black sedan, parked halfway up the road at a strange angle, as if the driver had stopped too quickly or been afraid of what the next thirty yards might show. The headlights were off. The windows were dark. The driver’s door was closed. Elias stood on the rail line, rain running into his collar, and watched the car until his eyes ached.

Then he heard footsteps on the wet gravel.

They came from the freight house, slow and careful, the kind of steps a person makes when trying not to disturb the ground. Elias did not move. He had learned, over many years, that a body was not a thing to be found by accident. It was a thing left for someone to find.

The footsteps stopped a few feet away.

“Mr. Thorne?”

The voice was low and rough, but calm in the way people are calm when they are trying not to be. Elias turned. Sheriff Nora Calloway stood in the doorway of the freight house, her dark hair pulled back, her rain jacket shining at the shoulders. She had a flashlight in one hand and a set of keys in the other, and she was looking at him with the careful neutrality of a woman who had been driving through the valley for twenty years and still had not gotten used to the way the mountains made everyone look like a suspect.

“I heard a bell,” she said.

There had been no bell.

Elias looked toward the siding.

The woman was there.

At first it had looked like a coat. A red coat, half sunk into the mud, with a long shadow of rain falling across it. Elias had known better, of course. He had known before he reached the rail line, because the stillness of the body was the first thing one noticed. A living thing could be quiet. A dead thing was still in a way that could not be argued with.

She lay on her side, her face turned toward the ground, one hand resting near the track. The red coat was old-fashioned, cut close at the waist, with a high collar that had kept most of the rain from her hair. Her hair was dark, not gray, though it was streaked with silver at the temples. A small pearl earring caught the light. Her coat buttoned to the throat. Her gloves were gone, but her hands were not muddy. They had been washed.

Elias looked at the ticket in the grass.

Then he looked at the woman.

“Who is she?” he asked.

Nora’s eyes went to the body, then back to him. “That’s what I wanted to ask.”

Elias did not answer at once. The rain fell between them. Somewhere up the mountain, a branch cracked under its own weight and fell with a sound like a door closing. He watched the woman’s hand. The fingers were long, the knuckles strong. There was a thin band of skin around the base of her left wrist, the kind a watch would have pressed into. It was pinker than the rest of her hand, as if she had taken it off recently.

He saw something else.

A key.

It was not on the track. It was in her hand, folded between thumb and forefinger so that the head of it had been hidden. The key was brass, small and heavy, with a round bow and a shaft that had been worn smooth. Elias had seen keys like that in stations all over the world, keys that opened more than doors. They opened boxes, ledgers, mailboxes, the small metal locks in the walls of old freight houses where records were kept when the company wanted them forgotten.

“Don’t move her,” Nora said.

Elias had not moved.

“Call the medical examiner,” he said.

“I’m calling them now.”

She pulled out her phone and stepped closer, but Elias saw that her hand was trembling slightly, the way a person’s hand trembles when they are trying to make a decision they already know they will regret. The rain had darkened her hair at the temples. Her badge hung at her belt. She had been a good sheriff, though the town had begun to call it that the year the mill closed and the younger people started leaving.

“She’s not a drifter,” Elias said.

Nora looked at him. “You’ve met her?”

“I’ve seen her.”

“Where?”

“Library. Church supper. The funeral.”

Nora’s mouth tightened. “Abby Crane.”

Elias nodded slowly.

Abby Crane had been the town’s librarian, which was a title that in Cold Hollow meant she knew who was married, who was widowed, who had a child in the city, and which families still kept their old papers in tin boxes beneath the floor. She had been small and quiet, but not in the way people usually meant. Quiet people could be harmless. Abby Crane had been quiet the way a door is quiet before it opens.

“She was in the library yesterday,” Nora said.

“I saw her.”

“You were walking the line every morning. You’ve always walked the line every morning.”

“It’s not a walk. It’s a route.”

Nora almost smiled. “She was looking at the old records.”

Elias felt the rain settle into the collar of his coat. “What records?”

“The ones from the station. The company ledgers. The derailment report.”

The word fell into the mud.

The 1983 derailment.

Elias had been on the 11:58 that night. He had carried the conductor’s lamp and the passenger list and the small brass token the company used to mark the last train before the line was closed. He had stood in the door when the car swerved onto the trestle. He had seen the lights of the freight house go out. He had seen the rail catch the wrong curve and the whole world tilt toward the water below. He had been the last one to walk away from the wreck, and he had told the company he was all right. He had told the company many things that night that were not entirely true.

He had not told them about the body in the snow.

There had been a body in the snow.

Elias knew this because he had found it, half-hidden under the broken boards of the freight house, and he had known the face before he knew the name. A man from the crew. A man who had not been on the official list. A man who had been there, Elias knew, though no one else had been allowed to admit it. The company had called the death an accident. The town had called it a tragedy. Elias had called nothing, because a conductor’s silence was the last thing a railroad wanted to hear.

He looked back at Abby Crane.

Her face was turned away from him, but her mouth was slightly open, as if she had been trying to say something and had not finished. Her hand still held the key. The ticket lay in the grass, half covered in mud, the blue numbers smeared where the rain had reached it.

“Last Run,” Elias said.

Nora followed his gaze. “She’s holding a key.”

Elias did not take his eyes from the body. “Not just any key.”

“What do you mean?”

“The old station had a lock. A small brass lock built into the wall behind the office. No one knew about it except the conductors. We used it to keep the night ledger separate from the day books. The company wanted a record that couldn’t be read in a hurry.”

Nora’s voice became quieter. “Did you know she had found it?”

Elias thought of Abby Crane in the library, standing before the filing cabinet, her fingers moving over the labels with the patience of a woman who knew that paper, like mountain stone, could keep a secret for a hundred years if it was given enough time.

“I thought she was looking,” he said. “I thought she was looking for something.”

Nora crouched, keeping her distance from the body, and examined the ticket without touching it. “This is from the derailment.”

“It’s from the last train.”

“That’s what I’m saying.”

Elias stood still, listening to the rain. The car on the road was still parked. Its engine was off. The driver, if there was one, had either gotten out and gone far enough to be unseen or never been there at all. The mountains were full of people who knew how to disappear, and the rain made hiding easy.

“Who else knows about the key?” Nora asked.

Elias did not answer right away.

He thought of the freight house, with its broken windows and its old coal dust. He thought of the trestle, where the rain had collected in the iron girders and dripped into the dark water below. He thought of Abby Crane’s hand, clean and closed, holding the thing that had been locked away for forty years.

“Three people,” he said at last.

Nora looked at him.

“The company kept the master copy in the office. The county kept the copy in the courthouse records room. And there was a third,” Elias said. “The one I gave to the man who owned the freight house before the wreck. He kept it in the wall. I know because I put it there.”

Nora was silent for a long moment. The rain fell. The black car did not move. Somewhere in the trees, a crow called once, sharp and dry, as if the sound had been torn from the air.

“You gave it to him?” Nora asked.

“Because he needed to know where the records were. The company was closing the line. They wanted the station torn down by winter. He wanted the papers saved.”

“And you thought it was a safe place.”

“It was as safe as any place in this town.”

Nora stood and looked at the body again. Abby Crane’s red coat had begun to blur with the rain. The mud had spread around her like a dark stain that was slowly losing its shape. Nora’s face was very still, but her hand moved to the radio at her belt and she pressed the button with one finger.

“Dispatch, this is Sheriff Calloway. I need the medical examiner and a forensic team at the abandoned siding near the trestle. Yes. It’s a homicide scene. No, not a vehicle. A woman. Red coat. Use the north road.”

She waited for the answer, then looked up at Elias.

“Do you know if she was alone?”

Elias looked toward the freight house.

The windows were dark. The door stood slightly ajar. The rain had begun to collect on the sill in thin silver beads. He had been there once, in 1983, the morning after the wreck, when the company men came with their clipboards and their careful questions. They had searched the office. They had searched the wall. They had not found the lock, because no one had told them where it was.

He had told Abby Crane where it was.

He had not thought it would matter.

“I don’t know,” he said. “But she was holding a key.”

Nora nodded slowly, as if the sentence had changed something in the air. Then she turned toward the road, toward the black car, and called back without looking. “Mr. Thorne.”

Elias stopped.

“We should talk about where you were this morning.”

“I was here. I come here every day.”

“I know.”

He looked at the body again. Abby Crane’s hand was so still that it seemed to belong to someone who had not yet learned how to be afraid. The key was still in her fingers. The ticket was still in the grass. The rain kept falling, washing the mountain clean and filling the tracks with small, cold lights.

Elias reached into the pocket of his coat and found the brass token.

It had not been there when he had started the walk.

He turned it over once, slowly, and looked at the number stamped on the back.

817.

The last train.

The last number.

The last thing the company had wanted anyone to remember.
