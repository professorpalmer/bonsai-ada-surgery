## final text

By the time the first letter reached Elias Vane, the lighthouse at Gull's Reach had been automated three winters before, and he was the only person on the island who still climbed its spiral stair for the joy of it. He had been the last keeper. The island had no school, no shop, no road that went farther than the cliff path.

Each morning he wound the clock, polished the brass, and checked the oil that no one ever used. In the afternoon he walked to the harbor where the stone boat had long since crumbled to white powder. In the evening he watched the sea for something that had no name and wrote the date in the logbook.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, carried by a small boat from the mainland that did not appear in any schedule. It was sealed with black wax. The stamp bore the tide of tomorrow, a crescent moon above the words: For the Keeper Who Stays. Elias set it on the kitchen table, then looked at the sea as if it might explain itself.

He broke the seal and read the first line aloud to the empty room. My name is Nora, and I am writing to you from before I exist. I do not know how this letter reached you. I only know that if you keep the light, I will be born into a world that still remembers my mother's name.

The rest of the page was plain. Do not be afraid. I have no address because I have no body. I have no photograph because my face has not been carved by the world. But I can tell you this: you will pour tea for one, and then for two, expecting someone to answer.

Elias laughed until the sound frightened him. He had been a keeper for forty years. He had learned to trust fog, weather, and the slow arithmetic of tides. He did not learn to trust a letter with no stamp and no body behind it, yet he put it in the drawer beside the logbook and did not throw it away.

More letters followed. They came on days when the mail boat was late, on days when the sea was flat and grey as a closed eye. Each one bore the same stamp. Each one described small things that had not yet happened: a gull nesting in the west window, a crack in the tea bowl, a ship called the Meridian passing the northern rocks.

One said: You will answer me in your journal, though I cannot receive your words. Another said: Do not repair the fence. The wire is not for keeping people out; it is for reminding you that you once belonged to a village.

He wrote in the journal anyway. I am sixty-two. My knees complain when the wind turns. I have not seen a child in ten years. If you exist, tell me what it is like to be expected. If you do not exist, tell me what it is like to be remembered before you are real.

The next letter answered. You are more lonely than you know. I know because I have read the shape of your silence. I know because the island holds your footsteps in the wet sand like a question. I am almost here, in the way a word is almost here, on the tip of a sleeping mouth.

The authority on the mainland sent a notice: the lighthouse would be dismantled after the next winter. The lamps were to be replaced by sensors, the stairway sealed, the keeper's cottage given to a caretaker who would sleep in the mainland and return only to check the battery. Elias read the notice once and pinned it to the wall.

He did not argue. He only added a line to his log: The light will be kept until the woman from the letter arrives, if such a woman arrives. The pen trembled, not with age, but with the strange fear of promising something to a person who had not yet been born.

Autumn came down the coast in silver sheets. The gull nested in the west window, just as the letter had said. The tea bowl cracked on the third morning, and Elias found a sliver of porcelain in the sand by the harbor, white and sharp as a small tooth.

He wrote: You are becoming true. The next letter was shorter. I am in a body now, though I cannot see it. I am in the belly of a woman on the Meridian. Her name is Wren. If the light fails, the rocks will take her. If the light holds, I will be born, and I will not forget the man who kept my first hour.

Elias looked at the lamp. It had been automated, but he had never stopped the old mechanism because his hands remembered its language. He wound the clock until the gears sang. He filled the oil reservoir. He opened the door to the gallery and let the wind cross the glass with a long, thin note.

Storms gathered. For three nights the Meridian appeared off the northern rocks, a dark shape with one red light. Elias stood in the gallery and shouted, though the sea swallowed his voice. He swept the beam across the water, over the rocks, over the place where the sea had teeth.

On the fourth night the beam struck the Meridian and held. The ship turned. Elias saw Wren at the rail, a woman with rain in her hair and one hand on her stomach. She did not look at the light. She looked at him, and for an instant the island was only two people separated by the distance between a name and its bearer.

The ship passed. The sea calmed by morning. The next letter came from a hospital on the mainland, stamped with a date that was still to come. Dear Elias, I was born at midnight. The first thing I saw was the light. I do not know why, but I think of you when I close my eyes.

He read it three times. The lighthouse was still standing, though the notice on the wall had grown yellow. He had saved the woman, or perhaps he had saved only the ship. But Nora had arrived in the world with a memory of a beam, and that made the old mechanism matter again.

The years after that were quiet. Elias kept the log. He wrote of tides, weather, and the small repair of the west window. He did not know whether Nora was a girl or a boy, though the letters suggested a girl who liked maps and had a scar on her thumb.

Once, on a clear evening, a boat came from the harbor. Wren stood on the dock with a child on her hip. The child looked up, and the child's eyes were the same grey as the sea just before rain.

Elias opened the door. Wren said, My daughter is Nora. She has been writing to you since she was a baby. We thought you might not believe us, so we brought the first letter.

Nora took the letter from him. Her fingers were small and warm. She held it against her cheek and said, Thank you for keeping me, though I did not know you yet.

He looked at the lighthouse behind them. The old lamp had been replaced by a new light, but the spiral stair still remembered the weight of his boots. He thought of all the letters he had written to an unborn girl, and how she had written back with the certainty of someone who had already learned to hope.

When Nora pressed his hand, Elias felt the cold of the sea leave him. The light above them turned, slow and sure, over the rocks, over the water, over the long distance between a name before it is real and the face that finally answers it.

Wren sat on the stone bench while the tide came in. She told Elias about Nora's first words. She had said light. Then she said keep. She had asked for a picture of the lighthouse, and Wren had drawn one from memory, because she had not been able to visit before.

Inside, Nora held the old letter against the window. The black wax had cracked with age. She traced the crescent moon and said, I have read these so many times that I know the smell of the paper better than I know my bedroom. The child had asked every night to see the keeper on the island above.

Elias took the chair by the window and looked at the child who had once been only a name. Outside, the new light swept the sea. It did not sound like the old mechanism, but it was enough. It was a voice saying: I am here. I have come. I have been kept.
