The smell reached the mess hall before the loaf did.

"Is that real wheat?" the child asked.

Maren smiled. "A little. Mostly it's the Meridian."

The child ate anyway, chewing slowly, and for a moment looked less like a passenger and more like a human being.

The trouble began that afternoon.

"We found a discrepancy in the protein budget," Osei said.

Maren kept her face steady. "The demonstration wheat was approved."

"The harvest was approved. The mill, the extra proofing cycle, the nutrient adjustment to your bread program—none of that was logged. You've exceeded your allocation by six percent."

Osei looked at her for a long moment. "The note matters less than the precedent."

That was the line that stayed with her. Not the number. The precedent.

That evening, the mess hall felt heavier. The bread was gone, but its memory remained. People sat around trays of nutrient paste and algae rice, talking in the low, careful voices of a community that had learned to be grateful for very little. Every gram had to earn its place. Maren had accepted that. She had even learned to love it, in a way. Scarcity had made her better at her work. It had forced her to listen to ingredients, to waste less, to make each bite mean something.

But that night, standing at the small sink in the galley, she opened her mother's notebook and turned to a page she had not read in years. It was a recipe for rye bread, written in a pen that had faded to the color of weak tea. Beneath it, her mother had added a note: When the future gets too thin, feed it something thick.

Maren stared at the words until they blurred.

In one narrow alcove, beneath a row of damaged communication modules, she found a tin box no larger than her hand. Its label was corroded, but she recognized the script from her grandmother's stories.

She opened it.

Inside were three dried sprigs of something green and brittle, a small glass vial of dark powder, and a folded paper packet. Maren's hands trembled as she unfolded it. The paper was thin and soft, but the words inside were clear.

"To whoever finds this: these are the last known viable samples of common wheat, oats, and rye from the North American fields. Do not waste them. Do not forget. Bread is not only fuel. It is a promise that we were once on a world where the sky had no ceiling."

The vial contained a culture of starter—dried, dormant, waiting. She had no way to verify if it would still live. She had no approved reason to grow it. And yet, in the quiet of the vault, she understood that the Meridian had been running on logic for so long that it had begun to forget one essential ingredient: hope.

Back in the galley, she set the vial on a sterile tray and began the slow work of revival. There were protocols she would have to bend. There were risks she could not fully calculate. A failed culture would be discarded. A successful one would require space, water, and nutrients. It would take time. It might never produce enough grain to matter.

But it might.

For three cycles, Maren told no one. She used a small hidden compartment under the sink, a place where she stored broken tools and spare filters. The vial sat in a sealed chamber with a thin stream of filtered water and a light set to a warm amber. Each morning she checked it. Each evening she wrote a note in her mother's notebook.

On the first cycle, nothing.
On the second, a faint cloud appeared at the edge of the water.
On the third, a tiny bubble formed, then another, and the smell of yeast rose through the galley like a question.

Maren smiled so hard her jaw ached.

She did not make a loaf immediately. She waited until the starter was strong enough to be trusted. She waited until she could measure exactly what it needed. She waited until the ship's next review of the hydroponic bay, when the botanists were already arguing about the experimental wheat and the future of grain on the Meridian.

One loaf. One shared table. One proof that the ship could still carry something more than survival.

Chief Osei listened in silence. The botanists argued. The council's representative asked for data. In the end, they agreed to a monitored test: a single batch, no more, with full logging of every input and output. Maren accepted every clause. She would log the mill, the water, the nutrient additions, even the time of day she checked the dough.

It was thinner, denser, more mineral, with a faint bitterness that reminded the crew of soil. But when Maren cut it open, the steam rose in a slow, golden cloud, and the smell filled the galley like a memory returning to someone who had thought it had been lost.

She carried the loaf to the central mess hall and placed it on the long table near the observation port. Outside, the stars remained exactly where they had always been. Inside, four thousand people turned to look.

"Does this mean we're almost there?" she asked.

She knelt so the child could hear her.

"It means we're still here," Maren said. "And we're still feeding each other."

It did not change the fact that the Meridian still carried a fixed mass through an infinite dark. But it changed something else. It gave the crew a reason to gather around a table that was not only functional. It gave them a taste of a world they might never see, a reminder that the sky had once been open enough to hold a harvest.

For the Meridian loaf, use water, grain, salt, and time. Add, if you have it, a little memory. If you do not, make room for it.

# The Last Loaf

The ovens on the Meridian breathed in slow blue cycles, a low pulse the crew called the ship's heartbeat. Maren pressed her palm to the door of the main bread oven and listened. Somewhere beneath the heat, a loaf was swelling, its crust hardening, its interior softening into the warm, sour smell of home.

She had been ship's cook for nine years, which meant she had spent most of her life translating algae, soy blocks, and fungal butter into something that felt like eating rather than fueling. The Meridian carried four thousand people and one way of life. Every meal had to be measured, recycled, and justified. Still, each morning Maren set aside a small amount of resources to make something slightly illegal in spirit: bread that tasted like memory.

The ship left Earth in 2132. Now it was 2196, and the Meridian was still more than half its journey to Tau Ceti. The stars outside the observation ports had not changed. When you were born on a ship and never knew a horizon, the sky became a ceiling. The ocean became a sealed tank. The idea of a world you could walk across without permission was less a fact than a rumor passed between engineers, teachers, and children.

Maren's mother had been a cook before her, and her grandmother before that. Her mother kept a notebook in a water-damaged cover, filled with recipes that no longer matched reality. "Sourdough starter," it read. "Use a little flour, a little water, time." Maren had kept the notebook too. She did not believe in its exact words anymore, but she believed in its shape. Food was the closest thing the Meridian had to a shared past.

The galley was not a kitchen from the old stories. Everything had a slot, a label, and a maintenance code. The ovens were automated, the mixers sealed, even the salt drawn from a lower-deck crystallizer. Yet Maren worked the station with an old cook's intuition: a pinch of this here, a splash of that there, a longer rest when the ship's gyros ran heavy, a cooler proof when the crew's moods ran thin.

That morning she was making a special loaf from the experimental wheat growing in the upper hydroponics bay. The plants were pale and thin, not the wheat of Earth, but they were real grain. The botanists had allowed her to take a small amount of harvested stalks, enough for a demonstration loaf. Maren measured the grain carefully, ground it in the stone mill she had built from a salvaged turbine housing, and mixed it with cultured flour and recycled water.

The smell reached the mess hall before the loaf did.

A line formed without ceremony. In the old colonies, a cook's reputation could be a matter of politics. Here it was simpler: people were tired, the ship hummed, and the walls were close. Bread was one of the few things that made the Meridian feel smaller than it was.

Maren cut the first loaf into thick rounds and placed them on warmed plates. The crust cracked as she lifted the first piece, releasing steam that caught the light. A child from the lower decks, no more than seven, stared at the bread as if it were a living thing.

"Is that real wheat?" the child asked.

Maren smiled. "A little. Mostly it's the Meridian."

The child ate anyway, chewing slowly, and for a moment looked less like a passenger and more like a human being.

The trouble began that afternoon.

Chief Osei called Maren into the engineering corridor. She was a tall woman with silver at her temples and the kind of flat, careful voice that could make an apology sound like a verdict.

"We found a discrepancy in the protein budget," Osei said.

Maren kept her face steady. "The demonstration wheat was approved."

"The harvest was approved. The mill, the extra proofing cycle, the nutrient adjustment—none of that was logged. You've exceeded your allocation by six percent."

It was not a large amount. It was not even a serious violation. But on the Meridian, six percent could tip a meal plan for an entire deck: softer soy patty, smaller fruit portion, a longer gap between the protein supplements that kept the young crews from feeling brittle.

"I was compensating for a faulty sensor in the hydroponic bay," Maren said. "The wheat yield was lower than expected, so I had to adjust the base recipe. I know I should have filed a note."

Osei looked at her for a long moment. "The note matters less than the precedent."

That was the line that stayed with her. Not the number. The precedent.

That evening, the mess hall felt heavier. The bread was gone, but its memory remained. People sat around trays of nutrient paste and algae rice, talking in low, careful voices. Maren served a final batch of smaller rolls and watched the crew eat them with a quiet reverence that almost made her angry.

They were not starving. They were always slightly underfed by design. The ship could carry only so much mass. Every gram had to earn its place. Maren had accepted that. She had even learned to love it, in a way. Scarcity had made her better at her work. It had forced her to listen to ingredients, to waste less, to make each bite mean something.

But that night, standing at the small sink in the galley, she opened her mother's notebook and turned to a page she had not read in years. A recipe for rye bread sat there, written in a pen that had faded to the color of weak tea. Beneath it, her mother had added a note: When the future gets too thin, feed it something thick.

Maren stared at the words until they blurred.

The next day, she went to the lower storage vault, a place reserved for sealed emergency supplies and the personal effects of the first crew. Access required a double key and a maintenance pass. Maren had the pass; the key had belonged to her grandmother. She told herself she was looking for a broken seal on a flour container. The truth was simpler: she wanted to know whether anything had been left behind.

The vault was cold and smelled of polymer. Racks of sealed canisters lined the walls, labeled in old notation. Medical supplies. Tools. Seeds. Water reagents. In one narrow alcove, beneath a row of damaged communication modules, she found a tin box no larger than her hand. Its label was corroded, but she recognized the script from her grandmother's stories.

She opened it.

Inside were three dried sprigs of something green and brittle, a small glass vial of dark powder, and a folded paper packet. Maren's hands trembled as she unfolded it. The paper was thin and soft, but the words inside were clear.

"To whoever finds this: these are the last known viable samples of common wheat, oats, and rye from the North American fields. Do not waste them. Do not forget. Bread is not only fuel. It is a promise that we were once on a world where the sky had no ceiling."

Below the note was a list of instructions, a recipe, and a small drawing of a loaf sliced in half. Maren sat on the vault floor for a long time, the tin in her lap, listening to the ship hum around her.

She did not take the sprigs. She knew what they were worth, what they could mean in the hands of a botanist or a council. Instead, she took only the vial and the packet. The vial contained a culture of starter—dried, dormant, waiting. She had no way to verify if it would still live. She had no approved reason to grow it. And yet, in the quiet of the vault, she understood that the Meridian had been running on logic for so long that it had begun to forget one essential ingredient: hope.

Back in the galley, she set the vial on a sterile tray and began the slow work of revival. There were protocols she would have to bend. There were risks she could not fully calculate. A failed culture would be discarded. A successful one would require space, water, and nutrients. It would take time. It might never produce enough grain to matter.

But it might.

For three cycles, Maren told no one. She used a small hidden compartment under the sink, a place where she stored broken tools and spare filters. The vial sat in a sealed chamber with a thin stream of filtered water and a light set to a warm amber. Each morning she checked it. Each evening she wrote a note in her mother's notebook.

On the first cycle, nothing.
On the second, a faint cloud appeared at the edge of the water.
On the third, a tiny bubble formed, then another, and the smell of yeast rose through the galley like a question.

Maren smiled so hard her jaw ached.

She did not make a loaf immediately. She waited until the starter was strong enough to be trusted. She waited until she could measure exactly what it needed. She waited until the ship's next review of the hydroponic bay, when the botanists were already arguing about the experimental wheat and the future of grain on the Meridian.

That day, Maren stood at the front of the small briefing room and placed the tin box on the table. She opened it to the sprigs, the vial, and the recipe. She told them what she had found. She told them what it meant. She did not ask for permission to bake. She asked for a single, small experiment: one loaf, one shared table, one proof that the ship could still carry something more than survival.

Chief Osei listened in silence. The botanists argued. The council's representative asked for data. In the end, they agreed to a monitored test: a single batch, no more, with full logging of every input and output. Maren accepted every clause. She would log the mill, the water, the nutrient additions, even the time of day she checked the dough.

The loaf she baked that night used a mixture of the revived starter, the experimental wheat, and a measured amount of rye flour from the emergency store. It was not the bread of Earth, not exactly. It was thinner, denser, more mineral, with a faint bitterness that reminded the crew of soil. But when Maren cut it open, the steam rose in a slow, golden cloud, and the smell filled the galley like a memory returning to someone who had thought it had been lost.

She carried the loaf to the central mess hall and placed it on the long table near the observation port. Outside, the stars remained exactly where they had always been. Inside, four thousand people turned to look.

She did not give speeches. She simply cut slices and set them out, one for each table, a small round of bread beside the standard meal. The crew ate it slowly. Some asked what it was. Some said nothing. Some closed their eyes.

A child from the lower decks, the same one who had stared at the first loaf, lifted a slice and held it up to the light.

"Does this mean we're almost there?" the child asked.

Maren thought about the long dark between stars. She thought about the vault, the tin box, the first bubble of yeast, and the note written by someone who had known that the future would need more than calculations.

She knelt so the child could hear her.

"It means we're still here," Maren said. "And we're still feeding each other."

In the months that followed, the starter grew into a small culture that the hydroponics team began to study. It was not a solution to the ship's resource problem. It did not end rationing. It did not change the fact that the Meridian still carried a fixed mass through an infinite dark. But it changed something else. It gave the crew a reason to gather around a table that was not only functional. It gave them a taste of a world they might never see, a reminder that the sky had once been open enough to hold a harvest.

Maren kept her mother's notebook by the sink. In a new page, she wrote a recipe in her own hand:

For the Meridian loaf, use water, grain, salt, and time. Add, if you have it, a little memory. If you do not, make room for it.

And each morning, when the ovens began to breathe, the whole ship seemed to lean a little closer, as if even the metal walls wanted to hear what came next.

# The Last Loaf

The ovens on the Meridian breathed in slow blue cycles, a low pulse the crew called the ship's heartbeat. Maren pressed her palm to the door of the main bread oven and listened. Somewhere beneath the heat, a loaf was swelling, its crust hardening, its interior softening into the warm, sour smell of home.

She had been ship's cook for nine years, translating algae, soy blocks, and fungal butter into something that felt like eating rather than fueling. The Meridian carried four thousand people and one way of life. Every meal had to be measured, recycled, and justified. Still, each morning Maren set aside a small amount of resources to make something slightly illegal in spirit: bread that tasted like memory.

The ship left Earth in 2132. Now it was 2196, and the Meridian was still more than half its journey to Tau Ceti. The stars outside the observation ports had not changed. When you were born on a ship and never knew a horizon, the sky became a ceiling. The ocean became a sealed tank. The idea of a world you could walk across without permission was less a fact than a rumor passed between engineers, teachers, and children.

Maren's mother had been a cook before her, and her grandmother before that. Her mother kept a water-damaged notebook filled with recipes that no longer matched reality. "Sourdough starter," it read. "Use a little flour, a little water, time." Maren had kept the notebook too. She did not believe in its exact words anymore, but she believed in its shape. Food was the closest thing the Meridian had to a shared past.

That morning she was making a special loaf from the experimental wheat growing in the upper hydroponics bay. The plants were pale and thin, not the wheat of Earth, but they were real grain. The botanists had allowed her to take a small amount of harvested stalks, enough for a demonstration loaf. Maren measured the grain carefully, ground it in the stone mill she had built from a salvaged turbine housing, and mixed it with cultured flour and recycled water.

The smell reached the mess hall before the loaf did. A line formed without ceremony. In the old colonies, a cook's reputation could be a matter of politics. Here it was simpler: people were tired, the ship hummed, and the walls were close. Bread was one of the few things that made the Meridian feel smaller than it was.

Maren cut the first loaf into thick rounds and placed them on warmed plates. The crust cracked as she lifted the first piece, releasing steam that caught the light. A child from the lower decks, no more than seven, stared at the bread as if it were a living thing.

"Is that real wheat?" the child asked.

Maren smiled. "A little. Mostly it's the Meridian."

The child ate anyway, chewing slowly, and for a moment looked less like a passenger and more like a human being.

The trouble began that afternoon. Chief Osei called Maren into the engineering corridor. She was a tall woman with silver at her temples and a voice that could make an apology sound like a verdict.

"We found a discrepancy in the protein budget," Osei said.

Maren kept her face steady. "The demonstration wheat was approved."

"The harvest was approved. The mill, the extra proofing cycle, the nutrient adjustment—none of that was logged. You've exceeded your allocation by six percent."

It was not a large amount, but on the Meridian, six percent could tip a meal plan for an entire deck: softer soy patty, smaller fruit portion, a longer gap between the protein supplements that kept the young crews from feeling brittle.

"I was compensating for a faulty sensor in the hydroponic bay," Maren said. "The wheat yield was lower than expected, so I had to adjust the base recipe. I know I should have filed a note."

Osei looked at her for a long moment. "The note matters less than the precedent."

That was the line that stayed with her. Not the number. The precedent.

That evening, the mess hall felt heavier. The bread was gone, but its memory remained. People sat around trays of nutrient paste and algae rice, talking in low, careful voices. Maren served a final batch of smaller rolls and watched the crew eat them with a quiet reverence that almost made her angry.

They were not starving. They were always slightly underfed by design. The ship could carry only so much mass. Every gram had to earn its place. Scarcity had made Maren better at her work. It had forced her to listen to ingredients, to waste less, to make each bite mean something.

But that night, standing at the small sink in the galley, she opened her mother's notebook and turned to a page she had not read in years. A recipe for rye bread sat there, written in a pen that had faded to the color of weak tea. Beneath it, her mother had added a note: When the future gets too thin, feed it something thick.

Maren stared at the words until they blurred.

The next day, she went to the lower storage vault, a place reserved for sealed emergency supplies and the personal effects of the first crew. Access required a double key and a maintenance pass. Maren had the pass; the key had belonged to her grandmother. She told herself she was looking for a broken seal on a flour container. The truth was simpler: she wanted to know whether anything had been left behind.

The vault was cold and smelled of polymer. Racks of sealed canisters lined the walls, labeled in old notation. Medical supplies. Tools. Seeds. Water reagents. In one narrow alcove, beneath a row of damaged communication modules, she found a tin box no larger than her hand. Its label was corroded, but she recognized the script from her grandmother's stories.

She opened it.

Inside were three dried sprigs of something green and brittle, a small glass vial of dark powder, and a folded paper packet. Maren's hands trembled as she unfolded it. The paper was thin and soft, but the words inside were clear.

"To whoever finds this: these are the last known viable samples of common wheat, oats, and rye from the North American fields. Do not waste them. Do not forget. Bread is not only fuel. It is a promise that we were once on a world where the sky had no ceiling."

Below the note was a list of instructions, a recipe, and a small drawing of a loaf sliced in half. Maren sat on the vault floor for a long time, the tin in her lap, listening to the ship hum around her.

She did not take the sprigs. She knew what they were worth, what they could mean in the hands of a botanist or a council. Instead, she took only the vial and the packet. The vial contained a culture of starter—dried, dormant, waiting. She had no way to verify if it would still live. She had no approved reason to grow it. And yet, in the quiet of the vault, she understood that the Meridian had been running on logic for so long that it had begun to forget one essential ingredient: hope.

Back in the galley, she set the vial on a sterile tray and began the slow work of revival. There were protocols she would have to bend. There were risks she could not fully calculate. A failed culture would be discarded. A successful one would require space, water, and nutrients. It would take time. It might never produce enough grain to matter.

But it might.

For three cycles, Maren told no one. She used a small hidden compartment under the sink, a place where she stored broken tools and spare filters. The vial sat in a sealed chamber with a thin stream of filtered water and a light set to a warm amber. Each morning she checked it. Each evening she wrote a note in her mother's notebook.

On the first cycle, nothing.

On the second, a faint cloud appeared at the edge of the water.

On the third, a tiny bubble formed, then another, and the smell of yeast rose through the galley like a question.

Maren smiled so hard her jaw ached.

She did not make a loaf immediately. She waited until the starter was strong enough to be trusted. She waited until she could measure exactly what it needed. She waited until the ship's next review of the hydroponic bay, when the botanists were already arguing about the experimental wheat and the future of grain on the Meridian.

That day, Maren stood at the front of the small briefing room and placed the tin box on the table. She opened it to the sprigs, the vial, and the recipe. She told them what she had found. She told them what it meant. She did not ask for permission to bake. She asked for a single, small experiment: one loaf, one shared table, one proof that the ship could still carry something more than survival.

Chief Osei listened in silence. The botanists argued. The council's representative asked for data. In the end, they agreed to a monitored test: a single batch, no more, with full logging of every input and output. Maren accepted every clause. She would log the mill, the water, the nutrient additions, even the time of day she checked the dough.

The loaf she baked that night used a mixture of the revived starter, the experimental wheat, and a measured amount of rye flour from the emergency store. It was not the bread of Earth, not exactly. It was thinner, denser, more mineral, with a faint bitterness that reminded the crew of soil. But when Maren cut it open, the steam rose in a slow, golden cloud, and the smell filled the galley like a memory returning to someone who had thought it had been lost.

She carried the loaf to the central mess hall and placed it on the long table near the observation port. Outside, the stars remained exactly where they had always been. Inside, four thousand people turned to look.

She did not give speeches. She simply cut slices and set them out, one for each table, a small round of bread beside the standard meal. The crew ate it slowly. Some asked what it was. Some said nothing. Some closed their eyes.

A child from the lower decks, the same one who had stared at the first loaf, lifted a slice and held it up to the light.

"Does this mean we're almost there?" the child asked.

Maren thought about the long dark between stars. She thought about the vault, the tin box, the first bubble of yeast, and the note written by someone who had known that the future would need more than calculations.

She knelt so the child could hear her.

"It means we're still here," Maren said. "And we're still feeding each other."

In the months that followed, the starter grew into a small culture that the hydroponics team began to study. It was not a solution to the ship's resource problem. It did not end rationing. It did not change the fact that the Meridian still carried a fixed mass through an infinite dark. But it changed something else. It gave the crew a reason to gather around a table that was not only functional. It gave them a taste of a world they might never see, a reminder that the sky had once been open enough to hold a harvest.

Maren kept her mother's notebook by the sink. In a new page, she wrote a recipe in her own hand:

For the Meridian loaf, use water, grain, salt, and time. Add, if you have it, a little memory. If you do not, make room for it.

And each morning, when the ovens began to breathe, the whole ship seemed to lean a little closer, as if even the metal walls wanted to hear what came next.
