## final text

# The Last Recipe

Mara had made the First Supper four hundred and twenty-seven times.

She said this out loud to the empty galley, the way her grandmother had, the way her grandmother's grandmother had, each of them standing behind the same steel counter in the same cramped kitchen at the stern of the *Arkadia*, stirring the same pot as if the numbers mattered. They didn't. The pot didn't care. The pot only cared about heat, and time, and whether the salt was still there.

Four hundred and twenty-eight years had passed since the *Arkadia* left the last orbit of Earth. Four hundred and twenty-eight years of recycled water, of hydroponic greens pale as paper, of protein blocks that tasted faintly of copper when the synthesis unit needed recalibration. Four hundred and twenty-eight years of the same slow arc through the void, of children born in corridors and never having felt true gravity, of a ship that was both a home and a coffin and a seed.

And now, for the first time in all four hundred and twenty-eight years, the First Supper was not a rehearsal.

The docking clamps would engage in nine days. After that, the *Arkadia* would sit on the soil of a world called Novara, its hull groaning into a shape it had been designed for before anyone alive had ever been born. The survey drones had come back three months ago, all green and wet and full of birds they couldn't name. The children in the upper decks were already learning to walk without the artificial gravity compensation, their steps clumsy and new. The adults had stopped saying "the long voyage." They just said *soon*.

Mara opened the sealed drawer under the counter. Inside, in a vacuum tube no bigger than a thumb, lay the saffron.

She had found the label in the ship's archive when she was nineteen, a handwritten card in a font she'd never seen: *Saffron, Kashmir. For the First Supper. Do not use. Preserve for the Arrival Meal. — E. Okafor, Chief Steward, 1242.*

One thousand two hundred and forty-two. That was the year the *Arkadia* launched. Her name on that card had been a smudge, but the handwriting was firm, the ink still dark after eight centuries. Someone had thought: *we will eat this meal when we arrive, and it will taste the same as it did the morning we left.*

The saffron had been sealed in a vacuum tube. The label on the tube read: *Check date: 2150. If degraded, substitute per protocol 7-C.*

Mara set the tube on the counter. The threads inside were no longer the deep, arterial red she'd seen in old holographic archives. They had faded to a dusty, bruised ochre, like a memory of blood. She held the tube up to the galley light and watched the color drift through the threads.

Protocol 7-C said: *If the primary agent is degraded, substitute with turmeric, ground, quantity equivalent. Note: color and aroma will differ. The meal is no longer authentic. Log the deviation.*

Mara read the note four times. Then she closed the drawer and went to the hydroponics bay.

The hydroponics bay was on Deck Nine, three levels up from the galley. She took the service elevator, the one with the flickering lights, and walked the corridor to the green rooms. The air there was different — heavier, wetter, smelling of wet stone and chlorophyll. Rows of racks held lettuce and kale and a few stubborn tomatoes that ripened in a perpetual amber light. Past them, deeper in, the spice racks.

Mara found what she needed in the back corner, behind a crate of dried chili that the botanists kept for "medicinal research" and that no one actually cooked with. A jar of sumac, harvested from a cutting they'd kept for three centuries because it kept trying to sprout. A small tin of whole black pepper, cracked open. A bundle of fresh thyme, still damp.

She stood there in the green quiet, holding the jar of sumac, and felt the old panic rise in her chest. Not because the ingredients were wrong. She knew how to cook. She'd cooked for two thousand souls every single day for thirty-one years. She knew how to take what you had and make it into something that made people close their eyes and remember a childhood they'd never had.

The panic was smaller than that. It was the feeling of being about to stop making a replica and start making something else. Every meal she'd ever cooked was a copy. A translation. A performance of a dish that had been real once, in a kitchen that was now a museum exhibit on Deck One, where a small placard said *Kitchen, Earth, approx. 2150, non-functional.* The First Supper was the one meal where the performance was supposed to be indistinguishable from the original. If she changed it, she wasn't preserving anything. She was, for the first time, *authoring.*

She thought of her mother, dead six years, who had said, one night, stirring the pot at two in the morning: "You know I'm terrified of it, Mara. Of the end. Because the meal is going to be the last one I ever cook that isn't a memory. After that, we cook what we find in the dirt. And I don't know how to do that. I don't know how to cook with the sun on a window."

She had thought her mother was talking about Novara. She hadn't meant it. Her mother was talking about herself, about the last moment she would stand in a kitchen and not be repeating something. About the terror of the next recipe being her own.

Mara set the sumac on the counter. She opened the saffron tube one more time, and let herself look. Then she put it back, closed the drawer, and went home to her small bunk in the galley's sleep quarters, where a framed photograph showed a woman at a stove on a blue planet, and she stood over it for a while before sleep came.

The morning of the Arrival Meal, the galley was full. Not with people — the *Arkadia* carried two thousand and forty souls, and not all of them ate in the stern galley — but with noise. The clatter of trays. The murmur of two thousand conversations compressed into a room that had been built for eighty. The smell of steam and rendered fat and something green and sharp that Mara had released into the air in small, deliberate bursts so that by the time people sat down, their noses would be telling them what was coming before their eyes confirmed it.

She had made it in the old shape — the rice, the slow-cooked legumes, the flatbread, the broth — but the center of the dish was no longer saffron. It was sumac, bloomed in warm oil with a splash of the ship's only wine, which was a fermented grape from Rack Seventeen and tasted like nothing anyone had ever tasted before. Black pepper, cracked at the table. Fresh thyme, torn rather than chopped. And the broth, which had been the thing she worried most about, which she had simmered for six hours in the big stockpot, fed with vegetable trimmings and a bone broth that the ship's livestock had been raised for exactly this purpose and had been waiting, patient and animal, in the holding pens for three hundred years.

The broth was not Earth broth. It had never been Earth broth. It was something new, built from the specific tastes of four centuries of ship-grown vegetables, from a particular strain of onion that the botanists had been trying to fix for two generations and had finally, accidentally, fixed, which now made everything taste a little like a green apple. It was the taste of the *Arkadia*, not the taste of the kitchen in 2150. It was the taste of where they had been.

People sat down. Mara walked the rows with the bread, pressing the warm flatbread into hands. An old man in the fourth row, a structural engineer who'd spent forty years in the hull, took a piece and broke it and put it in the broth and said, "Mm. Good." And then, because he was an engineer, he said, "It's a little acidic."

"Good," Mara said.

It was, she thought, the first time anyone had ever described the First Supper with a word that wasn't *the same* or *as it should be* or *like the old recordings show.*

The meal went on for an hour. Two hundred and forty people ate, because the galley could only hold so many at once, and the rest would eat in their quarters with trays brought up by the younger cooks who were learning. Mara stood behind the counter and watched. She watched a child in row three, no more than five, dip a piece of bread in the sumac oil and get a smear of red-brown across her lower lip, and not wipe it, and eat it like it was the most interesting thing she'd ever put in her mouth. She watched two people at the far end who she didn't know started talking, leaning toward each other, the way people do when food is good and the room is warm and you are not hungry in the way that matters.

She thought: this is not the meal they launched with. This is not the meal she is supposed to be serving. The saffron is in a drawer. Protocol 7-C has been logged. The meal is not authentic.

And it is the most real thing she has ever cooked.

At the end, the docking klaxon sounded. Not the old one, the one from the original launch, which had been retired and preserved, but the new one, a deeper tone that the acoustics team had designed. It rolled through the galley and through the ship and through the two thousand people still talking, still chewing, still finishing, and everyone looked up.

Mara looked at her hands. They were stained. The sumac had gotten under her nails and into the creases of her palms, and she would have to scrub them for an hour before she could go to her bunk. She thought about the first morning she'd stepped into this galley, at nineteen, and her mother's hands had been over hers on the cutting board, showing her how to break the flatbread. *You don't cut it,* her mother had said. *You tell it where to go.*

The klaxon faded. The ship, after four centuries of falling, was touching the ground.

Mara picked up a bowl of the leftover broth, still warm, and carried it to the window at the back of the galley. The viewport was small, a rectangle of glass set deep in the hull, and through it she could see, for the first time in her life, the sky of another world. It was not blue. It was a pale, strange grey-green, and in it hung a sun that was smaller than the one the children would inherit and a ring, thin as a thread, catching the light.

She drank the broth. It tasted like a green apple and thyme and four centuries of patience.

She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand and went to clean up. There were pots to wash and the sumac jar to re-seal and a new recipe to write in the logbook, because tomorrow would be the first morning on Novara, and she would need to figure out what to make with whatever grew in the dirt, in the sun on a window, in a kitchen that was no longer a kitchen but a doorway.

She set the saffron tube in the top drawer. She did not close the drawer.
