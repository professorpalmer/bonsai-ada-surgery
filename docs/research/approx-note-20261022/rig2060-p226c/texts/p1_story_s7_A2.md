# The Lighthouse at Vellmark

The sea had been speaking to Arthur Voss for forty-one years, and it always said the same things: *more, more, more.* The waves piled against the black basalt of the island, and he stood at the window of the keeper's quarters, watching the water work at the foundation the way a patient animal works at a bone.

The lighthouse was old. Older, in some way, than the island it stood on. The brass had gone the color of weak tea, and the great lens at the top of the tower had begun to fog in places that no amount of polishing could fix. Arthur was seventy-three. His knees protested each morning like old hinges, and the oil in the lamp needed a new gasket every three weeks or so. He was due for retirement in the autumn, when the mainland would send a machine to replace him. A machine with a timer and no soul.

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday in October, when the gales had been picking up for three days. He found it wedged under the door of the keeper's cottage, tucked between a note from the supply boat and a dead gull. The envelope was cream-colored, unmarked on the front except for his name in a careful, slanted hand: *Mr. Arthur Voss, Lighthouse Keeper, Vellmark Point.*

There was no return address. No postmark. The stamp, if it was a stamp, was a small blue circle with a design inside it that looked like a spiral, or maybe a fingerprint.

Inside, a single sheet of paper, thin as onion skin, held a few lines of text in that same slanted hand:

*Dear Mr. Voss, I'm sorry to write to you like this. I don't know how this letter got here, or whether you'll even open it, but I wanted to. I'm going to be very small when I'm born, and very alone in my first year, and I wanted you to know that I think about the lighthouse when I dream. I see you up top, turning the great glass, and I think you look like someone who has spent a long time listening to something, and that you are not lonely, though you are alone. I will be named Elara. I don't know if you'll know my name. I hope you do. — E.*

Arthur set the letter down on his desk. He read it twice. Then he went to the window and looked out at the water, and the gulls were gone, and the sea was just the sea, and nothing had changed.

He pinned the letter to the corkboard by his bed, next to a photograph of his wife, Margaret, from before the stroke took her speech and then the rest of her.

The second letter came three days later. This one described a beach two miles down the coast that Arthur had always thought was nothing but rocks and weed. The writer described it in exquisite detail: the way the tide carved a small pool of green glass in the crevice between two stones, the particular shade of rust on the rusted hinge of an old fishing net that had been caught there for years. Arthur knew every stone on that beach. He had walked that shore ten thousand times. He read the description, and his skin went cold, because the net had rusted in a pattern she described that he had only ever thought to himself, a private thought, never spoken aloud.

The third letter came in November. She was, she wrote, eight months old. She was learning to pull up to the edge of a cot. She was afraid of a sound in the wall that she would later learn was a water pipe. She wrote, *I don't understand why I'm writing to a man I've never met and who I don't believe exists yet, but I have the feeling that the distance between us is a kind of door, and that I'm on the other side of it, and I want to press my hand to it.*

Arthur began to leave a letter back. He wrote about the foghorn. He wrote about the way the light threw its beam across the water at two in the morning and made the whole bay look like a road made of silver. He wrote about the year the storm of 1997 took the western breakwater, and he had stood on the tower all night with a cup of bad coffee, watching the sea chew on the stones. He wrote about Margaret, briefly, the way you mention a name that has been dead for so long it has become furniture.

She wrote back. And back. And back.

By February, the letters had become a conversation. She was, she said, now six. She was learning to read. She could spell her own name but not the word "lighthouse." She wrote, *You sound like a man who is going to stop doing the thing you do soon, and when you stop, something in the world goes quiet that I don't have a word for yet.*

Arthur wrote, *I'm afraid that's true.*

*What will you do?*

*I don't know. I think the letters will stop.*

The silence between the letters grew. It was months, then a year. Then a winter with no letters at all. Arthur went about his rounds, climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, cleaned the lens, trimmed the wick, and wrote no letters. He told himself he had moved on. He told himself the whole thing had been a fever dream, a trick of the gulls and the fog and his own loneliness talking.

Then, in the spring, a letter arrived. She was nineteen now. She was studying astrophysics in a city he did not know. She wrote that she had found a photograph of the lighthouse in a library archive, a grainy black-and-white shot from the 1950s, and in it the keeper was a young man with a cigarette at his lip, and she had thought, *There he is. There he actually is.* She described the ache of seeing a face you have never met and knowing it with your whole body.

Arthur wept. He sat on the kitchen floor with his back against the stove, and he wept like a man who has been holding a breath for a year.

She wrote, *I keep thinking about the last thing you told me. You said, "When you're older, I hope you remember the sound the foghorn makes at dawn, because I'm going to stop making it, and you won't have heard it, and I'm sorry." I remember. I remember the low, broken groan of it, like a whale that's almost given up. I remember it the way I remember a lullaby. I don't know who sang it to me. I just know it.*

Arthur wrote back, *That's all I wanted. For someone to remember.*

The letters slowed again. She was busy now, in a lab, in a relationship, in the ordinary grinding life of a young person building a future. But every few months, a letter came. A postcard from the coast where she lived. A note about a book she was reading. A single line: *I saw your light last night. I was on a train, and the window was dark, and I looked up, and there you were. Still turning.*

Arthur would take up the light. He would climb the one hundred and twelve steps. He would polish the glass. He would feel, in a way he could not name, that he was not alone in the act.

The last letter came in the autumn, the same autumn as his retirement. It was only three lines.

*Dear Mr. Voss. I am twenty-nine now. I am married. I am happy, though I will not be happy for the rest of my life in the way you need to be. I just wanted you to know that I think about the door. I think about the hand pressed to it. I think about how you were on the other side, and you pressed back, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough. Thank you for the foghorn. Thank you for the light. I will remember.*

*— E*

Arthur read it, and set it down. He went to the window. The sea was doing what the sea always did, working at the bones.

He would leave the island in three days. The mainland men were coming with their timer and their machine. He would go back to a flat in the city, and a smaller chair, and a silence that was only his.

But that night, and the night after, and the night after that, he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps, and he lit the lamp, and he turned the great glass, and he let the beam go out across the water, and across the dark, and into the year that would come, and the year that would come after that, where a woman who did not exist yet was learning to read the name of the star that was, or would be, or would have been, his.

And the sea said *more, more, more,* and Arthur thought, *Yes. More. I'll keep turning.*

And he did.

*The lighthouse at Vellmark was automated in 1987. The island was uninhabited after that. In 2031, a woman named Elara Voss—no relation, though she liked the coincidence—began a doctoral thesis on maritime folklore and found, in a water-stained box in the keeper's cottage, a corkboard with forty-three letters pinned to it, the last addressed to no one, the first from someone who had not yet been born. She read them all in one evening. She did not sleep. In the morning, she walked down the two miles of rocks and weed to the beach, and she found the small pool of green glass in the crevice between the two stones, and she understood, finally, what a door could be.*
