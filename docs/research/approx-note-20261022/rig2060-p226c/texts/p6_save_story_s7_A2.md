# Salt and Light

Mara woke to the smell of yeast.

It was always the yeast first—the sour, warm breath of three hundred kilograms of dough rising in the great vats, drifting through the ring of the *Meridian* on the ventilation's exhalation before the lights woke. Nineteen years, and she had never slept through it.

The *Meridian* carried nine thousand people, of whom eight hundred and ninety-nine knew her only as "the galley chief." The tenth knew her by name, and by her hands. Ido was sixteen, small, and already burning with a curiosity that would, in ten years, eat her alive. She had shadowed Mara through two full harvests; this was her first winter aboard.

Their galley stood in the ship's heart, three floors of copper and steel between the hydroponic bays and the residential rings. Through its floor windows, one could watch the lights of the growing rings pulse in the dark. The bays were the ship's lungs and its kitchen; the galley was its throat.

This was a duty older than the ship's name. Every generation, a cook was chosen from among the bakers, the gardeners, the stew-menders—and every generation, the cook's daughter or son followed, because a ship's memory lives in the mouth of its cook. Mara's mother, Anouk, had been the cook for thirty-one years. Anouk's mother, Sera, had been the cook from launch day.

Between them, they had written forty-seven recipes into one leather book, kept in a case of lacquered oak. Forty-seven. The first recipe was bread. The second was bread again, made better.

In eleven days, the *Meridian* would land on a world the survey crews had called Eridanu. And in eleven days, by the oldest rule of the galley, Mara was to cook the arrival meal.

Her mother had left her the name in a small card tucked inside the book: *Dawn Garden Stew.* Nine ingredients, written in a hand that trembled toward the end. And only four ingredients fully described. Anouk had died in the middle of a winter, in the middle of a sentence.

For weeks, Mara had stood over the half-finished recipe. *Tomato. Onion. Garlic. A leafy green that grows toward the light.* After that, only: *the sweet pepper, the root that bites back, the fruit that ripens in the cold... and whatever the garden keeps.*

The crop database of the *Meridian* held eight hundred years... no—87 years of growing logs. Mara pulled up the bays, row by row, leaf by leaf. And what she found made her stomach drop.

The tomato that Sera had grown in 2129 was red, smooth-skinned, acid-bright. The tomato that grew now was deep purple, round, sweet, with a flavor the database had a long, technical word for that Mara did not like. The "leafy greens that grow toward the light" had become a dark violet leaf, with a peppery bite that made her water run. The sweet pepper was now so sweet it tasted like honey. The root that bit back—the old name was horseradish—was a thick, white bulb that burned in the back of the throat like smoke.

The garden had changed. It had to, in order to survive: the light was a star, the water was recirculated, and the seed had drifted like a ship's drift. The garden of the *Meridian* was not the garden of Earth. It was, by now, a garden of its own.

Mara could have frozen the rest. In the deep stock at the bow of the ship there were four hundred grams of real tomato—sun-ripened in a valley she had read about in a museum's audio file, never tasted—and a small jar of the original seed onion, dried, and sealed. Enough for one pot, maybe two. Not enough for nine thousand mouths. The old rule said the arrival meal fed everyone.

Sera's first book. Bound in canvas, written in launch ink, its pages swollen with a century of steam.

The first page was not bread. It was dated three days after launch, when the first bay had just put out its first crop—thin, pale, angry leaves. And there, in a hand that was still steady, the first cook had written:

*"First meal. Whatever the bay gave, I gave them. They were hungry for home, and home had not yet learned to grow. If the garden forgets what it was, the pot must remember it for us. Feed them home."*

That was the thought that struck her, and it stayed: this garden was not Earth's garden failing. It was Earth's garden arriving. Eighty-seven years of adaptation, of slow negotiation with artificial light and recycled water. It was a world with a ship in it, and a ship was a world.

She picked through six bays. She bit, tasted, and noted. She found, in the fourth bay, a small squash the database had almost forgotten—a cousin of a pumpkin, pale gold, butter-sweet. The "root that bit back," she pulled, and it bit back exactly as the recipe had promised, in the old, honest way.

The arrival feast was a Tuesday, and the galley was at war with itself by dawn.

Mara called her people to her. "We make it true," she said. "Not from a book. From the garden. Ido, the onions."

She worked the half-recipe like a conversation. The stew began in a pot too large to hold the whole of it—no, she was making it in the great vats, four of them, the size of swimming pools. No wait. Nine thousand people: the stews went into the great vats, the bread into the ovens, the greens into the trays. The galley became a river of steam and light.

She put the four hundred grams of real tomato into the heart of the sauce. Just a handful. Enough to give the whole thing a note of something remembered. Enough that anyone who tasted it and wept would not have to be told what they were missing.

When it was done, she tasted it herself. And stood still for a long time, holding a spoonful of her mother's stew in a bowl, with the ship turning in the dark above her head, and said: "That's it."

At the arrival meal, no one was served a plate. The *Meridian* had no plates; people ate in the dining rings from the tables, with the stew in the center. Nine thousand people ate.

Mara sat at the far end of the galley's floor window, watching. An elderly woman at table 40—old enough to remember the launch ceremony, with a child's first name tattooed on her forearm—put down her spoon.

"It tastes like the food at the ceremony," the woman said. "The first day."

Mara nodded. She had no answer for that, and no answer was necessary.

A child—Ido's cousin, about eight years old—raised a hand, as at school. "What's it supposed to taste like, cook?"

Mara looked at the stew, at the purple skins of the tomato, at the pale gold squash, at the smoke of the root.

"Home," she said.

At dawn, the viewport opened, and Eridanu filled the glass: a blue-green eye, slow and enormous, turning. The *Meridian* cut the last of the dark, and the galley went quiet around her.

Ido stood beside her, holding the leather book, a fresh page already torn out and waiting.

"It's not finished," Mara said.

"No," Ido said. "It's just started."

Mara put down her spoon. Outside, the new world rose to meet the ship, and in the galley at the heart of the *Meridian*, the bread began to rise.
