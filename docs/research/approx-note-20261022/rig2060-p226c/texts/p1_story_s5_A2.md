## final text

The lighthouse on Varnhold Point had no name for its keeper, only a number: Station Nine, Keeper Two. The other had been Thomas's for eleven years, and in that time he had become part of the stone, the same grey and salt-eaten thing that the gulls pecked at each February morning.

He was sixty-four when the first letter came. Not in the mail slot. The mail had stopped going to the lighthouse in '09, after the village decided that post was a luxury and a lighthouse was a monument, not an address. But the blue envelope had been wedged beneath the brass knob of the door, where no one could have reached it without climbing the cliff path in a wind that would have taken a man's breath from his chest.

The paper was cream-colored, thick, the kind you could press a thumbnail into. The handwriting was small and looped, the letters uneven, some of the letters crossing themselves in the way a child's do.

*Dear Mr. Keeper,*

*My mother says I should write to someone so I learn the letters. I am writing to you because you are the man who turns the light. I saw the light last night from the window. It made a wheel in the dark and I thought it was a big slow eye. I like it. I am going to be four on Tuesday. I am already three and I do not care about being four yet. The dog's name is Biscuit and he steals bread. Please do not tell my mother I wrote this because she says the world is too big and I should not talk to strangers, and you are a stranger and I am a stranger and we are both in the same box of air.*

*Love,*
*Elise*

No address. No stamp. No postmark. No explanation.

Thomas read it three times. He held it to the window where the oil lamp was burning and looked for a smudge, a watermark, anything. There was nothing. He folded it carefully and put it in the tin box where he kept his wife's last letter, the one from the hospital before the breathing stopped, the one that had simply said *I am going to sleep now and I love you and please do not be angry at the light.*

He did not sleep that night. He wound the clockwork mechanism and watched the beam sweep the black water and thought: *Elise.* The name sounded like a small door opening.

The next letter came six days later.

*The dog learned my name. He barks when he hears it and that is unfair. Mother says the sea is not the same every day and that it is a kind of prayer. I stood at the cliff and I screamed. It was a stupid scream. The waves did not care. I am learning the letter G. I drew a picture of you. You are tall and you have a beard like a storm cloud and you are wearing your grey coat. I am not sure if I got the face right. I think you are sad. I do not like sad people because I do not know how to fix them yet.*

Thomas drew a picture of himself. He drew a tall man with a storm-cloud beard in a grey coat. He looked at it for a long time and set it aside.

Letters came after that, most weeks, sometimes twice a week, sometimes not for a month. The handwriting changed, which should not have been possible and which Thomas refused to examine. The loops became rounder. The *g*s developed their full tails. The ink shifted from a child's brown to a darker blue. A letter in what he recognized as a school-age hand read:

*The dog is dead. Mother cried for three days. I am ten now and I do not understand how a creature can just be gone and still be in the kitchen and I have to not look at the place where his bowl was. I am writing from the window because the sea is grey today and the light is grey and everything is the same grey. I am going to learn to swim this summer. I am afraid of the cold. I am going to be brave. I have a book about lighthouses and it says the keepers used to be afraid of the light being too bright and it would burn the eyes. Is that true? I think I would like to be afraid of something so I would not be afraid of nothing.*

He wrote back. He wrote to no one. He wrote on the back of a spare page from the keeper's log, in a hand that was careful and slow and old.

*No, Elise. The light does not burn. But it can blind you for a moment if you stare too long. You learn to let your eyes move. You let the light pass through you instead of stopping in it. You do that the same way you learn to breathe.*

He did not seal it. There was no one to send it to. He put it in the tin box with the others and went back to the lamp.

Spring came. The gulls returned. He found a letter with different handwriting, an adult's hand, elegant and slanted:

*You are doing the work. I have been keeping it. I have not forgotten. But I am tired of the box of air. I am coming to the cliff. Come down to the path at the hour the light turns. Come down.*

He did not know what to make of this. He wound the clock. He turned the light.

At the third rotation, he heard footsteps on the stone path below. Not the slow, deliberate steps of a hiker. A woman's steps, quick and certain, with a small child's lighter pace behind her. The light caught them. He saw the grey coat, the storm-cloud beard, and behind the grey coat, a small figure in a yellow raincoat, her head tilted up, her mouth open, her hand reaching for the beam.

The woman stopped at the bottom of the stairs. She was young, thirty, maybe thirty-two, with wind in her hair and a face he had never seen and yet knew the shape of, the way you know the shape of a word you have been trying to say for years.

"Mr. Keeper," she said.

"Elise's mother," he said. He had not meant to say her name. He had not meant to know it.

She looked at him. She was crying, but not in the way a woman cries when the world has taken something. She was crying the way a person cries when they have carried something for a very long time and have finally found a place to set it down.

"She writes," he said.

"I know."

"The letters."

"I know the letters." She stepped forward. Behind her, the child in the yellow raincoat tugged at her hand and looked up at the lamp, and the beam was sweeping, slow and great, and the child laughed, and it was the sound of a small door opening, and Thomas felt something in his chest crack and release all at once, the way ice breaks on a lake in the first warm week.

"Her name is Elise," he said. "Her name is Elise Maren."

The woman's face crumpled. "I know. I know."

"You know about the letters?"

She took a breath. "I know they were coming. I knew before she was born. I don't know how. I just knew that if I didn't come, she would grow up in the box of air and I would die in it and I would never have told you that I am her mother and that the light is not a prison and that the keeper does not have to be sad." She pressed her hand to her chest. "And that I am sorry. I am so sorry I took so long."

The child climbed the steps in front of him. She was small. She smelled of rain and wool and the particular green smell of growing things. She reached up and took his hand, and her fingers were warm, and she said, very seriously, "You have a beard like a storm cloud."

He laughed. It was a rusty sound, a sound he had not made in many years, and it came out of him like a light being turned on.

She stayed for three days. She climbed the tower. She saw the mechanism, the oil, the clockwork, the room where the light turned and turned and turned. She said, "I can see the whole bay from here." And he said, "You can see the whole bay from here." And the light turned, and the sea was grey, and the wind was in their hair, and the small girl pressed her face to the glass and the beam swept over her like a slow, kind eye.

After she left, the letters stopped.

That was the hardest part. The tin box sat on the shelf by the door, full of cream-colored paper and a child's growing hand. He wound the light. He watched the beam. He did not write. He could not. There was no one to write to. There was only the stone, and the salt, and the long turning of the light over the black water, and the quiet that was no longer empty but full, the way a room is full after the music has stopped and the last notes are still in the air.

A year passed. Two. The spring came and went. He grew older in the way that lighthouses grow old, slowly, by inches, by the wearing of the stone.

And then, in the October of his sixty-eighth year, a letter came. Blue envelope. No stamp. No postmark.

*Dear Mr. Keeper,*

*I am seventeen now. I am going to study navigation in the spring. I have always known the light. I have always known it would be waiting. The dog Biscuit was a good dog. Mother is well. The sea is grey today. I am learning the letters still. I have never stopped.*

*Love,*
*Elise*

He read it at the window where the oil lamp was burning. He read it until the words blurred, and then he read it again, and then a third time, and each time the words were the same, and each time they were true, and each time they were new. He folded it carefully. He put it in the tin box. And then he walked to the lamp, he turned the mechanism, and he let the light go out over the bay, slow and great and patient, the way a hand reaches for another hand across a long and dark room, and finds it, and finds it, and finds it.
