# Two Fires

In the early morning, before the fishermen were out on the water, the harbor square of Saltmere belonged to steam.

Elena Voss knew it by the hour and by the feel of the stones beneath her boots. She crossed the cobbles with a basket of empty tins and two wooden trays under each arm, and the square was still all hers: the grey-blue of the sea, the black wet of the rocks, the first pale gold of dawn sliding down the side of the lighthouse at the head of the town. The salt was always there in Saltmere, and it was never exactly a smell. It was a condition, a weather of the body. It worked into the thread of a shirt, the grain of old wood, the crumb of a loaf. Even the waves sounded different here than in any other harbor you could name—lower, more patient, as if the sea had been breathing for a very long time and had no intention of changing its mind.

Voss Bakehouse occupied the corner house on the north side of the fountain. Its neighbor—the door painted the color of butter on the south side of the square—was Theo Marsh's. Between the two ovens stood a fountain older than either bakery, its stone basin filled each morning with water so still it looked like a coin laid flat on the ground. For three generations bread had come out of the north oven. For twelve years, out of the one on the south.

That was the arrangement, and for a long time it had been enough.

Elena's days began with Brin. The starter lived in a stoneware crock on the high shelf above the hearth, a sourdough culture that her grandmother Rosa had carried to Saltmere in a biscuit tin the year Rosa first opened the bakehouse with a loan and a woodstove. Brin was fed at dawn with flour and water—never tap water, never. Rosa had mixed it with rain caught in a tin on the roof, and Elena still did, and the difference was a difference she could only point to, never prove. She fed it, she turned it over the lip of the crock, she listened to the small wet sigh it made, and only then did she pull on the heavy canvas apron and begin.

The hearth in the north bakery was the oldest thing in the square. Its stones had been laid before the lighthouse, and they held the heat of forty winters. Elena worked her dough in a wooden bowl the size of a bread bin, folding by hand because Rosa had written, in a slanting hand at the end of a recipe card, that *hands remember what hands are told*—a sentence Elena had not understood at nineteen and was beginning to understand at forty-eight.

In Saltmere, salt was the third ingredient in everything. The fishermen left their nets to dry on the rocks and the loaves that came home with them tasted faintly of the harbor in the way only a local tongue could tell. A city loaf could be copied in an afternoon. A Saltmere loaf, the fishermen said, had to be *known*.

That is what made Theo Marsh dangerous, and that is what made him beloved.

Theo came to Saltmere twelve years before Elena's hair went first grey and her shoulders learned the shape of a long workday. He had been born in Kettleford, a city two valleys over where his father ran a bakery on a high street that had slowly, year by year, been swallowed by chain stores. The marsh came with the city in its bones: loud ovens, loud furnaces, bread baked in trays and sold in paper bags before the crust had fully set. He came to Saltmere to work the fish market, where he slept above a chandler's shop and learned, out of boredom and hunger, to watch the old women knead their morning loaves.

His first loaf came out of a borrowed oven on a Tuesday. He left it at the market stall of Old Reyes, who sold mussels and had never been seen to raise a sourdough in his life. The loaf was a sea loaf—salted, dark-crust, baked slow with a handful of the dried dulse the fishermen hauled each week. Reyes ate half of it standing up over his bucket, and the other half he did not finish because it had turned into something he could not name. By Thursday there were six people in line at the fish stall, not for the mussels, and Reyes told Elena the truth of it over the counter while she was buying her flour.

"You have a problem now, love," Reyes said. "A man has come in with a city in his boots and a sea in his bread."

Elena took it as a challenge in the way Rosa would have taken it. She made her own sea loaf that week, and hers was the old way: low, salted, a long and slow bake in the stone hearth that ended in a crust like wet stone and a crumb that went open and honeyed at the break. She set it on the counter at the fountain's edge and said nothing. Theo saw it. Theo never said nothing.

He put his loaf next to it, and the next morning the two were still there, and the next, and the square divided itself on the spot. The people who lived north of the fountain bought from the north oven. The people who lived south bought from the south. It was not an argument so much as a tide, and tides do not ask permission.

For a few years the division held its own, and the rivalry stayed mostly at the level of chalk on a window and a raised eyebrow. Elena's window chalked, in Rosa's slanting hand, *The city's butter is a lie. Come home.* Theo's chalked, in a clean hand that had never learned to slant, *The home has no recipe. Come in.* The children of Saltmere bet pennies on which loaf would be first out of the oven at the first of the month, and the fishwives at the market sorted their opinions by which door they crossed to buy bread. It was the town's private weather, and everyone enjoyed it.

Then the mayor came out of the town hall with a folder and a speech.

The council had finished restoring the lighthouse bakery. For sixty years the old keeper's cottage, perched at the head of the headland with its stone oven and its door facing the sea, had stood empty, its oven cold and its name gone out of the books. Now it was done, and the council wanted the Founding Tide—the first calm morning after the winter storms, when the whole town climbed the headland with its coats and its lanterns—to have a purpose again. They would hand a key, a single iron key with the lighthouse cut into its bow, to the best baker in Saltmere, and that baker would run the lighthouse bakery for the year and bake the Founding Tide loaf, the loaf that every household in the town was expected to buy and every household was expected to break over the table that night.

The town hall went quiet in the way towns do when they are being handed a mirror.

The north side of the square looked at the south. The south looked back. The fountain, which had been the truce, became the battleground.

The weeks before the festival ran hot and clean, the way a kiln runs. Elena worked eighteen-hour days. She made a loaf for every hour of the week and ate only the crusts, telling herself that tasting was part of the work and that Rosa would have laughed at her for the lie. Theo worked the same. He ran his ovens in shifts, a long low heat on the first rise, a high fast burn for the second, and he posted the times on the door so Elena would know exactly how he was doing it. It was the first thing he had done in years that felt like a taunt with the door closed, and he knew it the moment he nailed the paper up.

At the market, the whispers changed shape. There was talk that Elena's starter had been failing, that Brin had gone quiet, that the old woman was trying to save something that was already lost. There was talk, too, that Theo's bread was too perfect, too regular, that a city loaf could be measured and a sea loaf could not, that the lighthouse would be a funeral for the north side of the square. Elena heard it all the way to her door. She did not argue with it, which made it worse, and the children bet more pennies because the adults had begun to mean it.

Theo, for his part, was not thinking about the north side of the square at all. He was thinking about his father's hands, which had never been steady, and about the last year of the bakery on the high street, when the fire took the ovens on a Monday and the lease went up in smoke with them, and about the drive to the coast in the dark because the city had no room left in him that he could find. He had told himself, on the first morning in Saltmere, that he was coming to learn. He had been wrong about that, and he had been right about the rest. He had come because he could not stop comparing. He could not stop taking a loaf out of a stranger's oven and thinking, *not yet. Not yet, not yet, not yet.* Saltmere had given him a sea, and the sea had given him something he had not been able to name, and he had spent twelve years trying to keep up with it.

Elena was trying to keep up with the same thing, only from the other side. Her grandmother's bread had been the town's bread. It was in the wedding cakes and the funeral trays. It was the thing that had been in the baskets when the storm came and the boats came in and the doors were closed and the world was just bread and water and people. And now the sea itself was changing—saltier, colder, the fish coming up different—and her bread was changing with it, and she could not say where the line was between a loaf and a memory. She was forty-eight. She was not a relic. But the stone hearth held the heat of a woman she had been for three years, and some mornings, when the steam rose off the counter and the square was empty, she felt less like a baker and more like a witness.

The gale came three days before the Founding Tide.

It came off the headland at dusk with a low sound that the old fishermen called a *warning*, and it did not break. It ran across the water all the way to the square and into the lanes and up the headland path, and the lighthouse beam swept the storm with its long slow arm as if trying to find it. Elena closed the shutters of Voss Bakehouse with the iron bars and the rope and the bar of her body behind the wood, and she stood in the kitchen with the lights off, listening to the wind argue with the roof.

It was the roof that went.

She knew it because the sound of the storm changed, went from a wall of noise to a noise with edges, the way a room sounds when something in it breaks. She stood in the dark with the candle and the matchbox in her hand and the candle was shaking in a way the wind could not be shaking it. She put on her coat, and she went out, and the wind took half her voice for the crossing, and the north corner of the bakehouse was leaning. The old timber above the hearth had let go of its iron brackets and had come down across the hearth mouth in a broken heap, and the stones were cracked, and the hearth—Rosa's hearth, the stone that had held the heat of forty winters—had a seam running through it that ran the length of the face.

Brin's crock was still on the shelf, unbroken, and the crock was the only thing in the house that had not moved.

Elena stood in her own kitchen and looked at the cracked hearth, and she did not cry. She had a festival in three days and a lighthouse key and a loaf to bake, and she had a starter on a shelf and a door that would not lock and a square in the cold and a neighbor on the south side of a fountain. She pulled on the canvas apron anyway. It was the only thing she could do that her hands would let her do.

At two in the morning, a knock came at the door that would not close, and the wind was pushing the frame in a way that made it sound like a fist.

Theo Marsh stood in the dark in a yellow oilskin that was soaked through at the shoulders, and he was holding a cup of tea in one hand and a lantern in the other, and he had a look on his face that Elena had never seen on it, which was not anger and not triumph and was, in fact, worry.

"I heard it go," he said. "The beam above the hearth. I thought it was the whole wall." He set the tea on the counter. "I brought tea. I know you don't drink it. I brought it anyway."

Elena did not speak. She looked at the crack in the hearth stone, and then at Theo, and she said, "My festival loaf is in the proof."

"I know." He set the lantern on the worktable. "You don't have to tell me what you need. I know what you need. You need three days and a hearth that can take the heat. You can have mine."

The words came out of her before she could catch them: "You'll have to bake mine."

"And you'll have to bake mine, if you're going to be in my kitchen." He looked at the broken hearth. "I'll move the crock to the lighthouse shelf. Brin's going to have to come with you, or the lighthouse loaf is going to taste like a city loaf."

She looked at him for a long time. The storm was still out there, still arguing with the roof, and the tea was already going cold in its cup, and the lantern made a small steady circle of light on the worktable, and she thought of the line in the square that the children used to draw in chalk and erase when it rained, and she thought of the lighthouse at the head of the town with its beam sweeping the dark, and she said, "Come in. Your feet are wet."

They baked for three days in the south oven.

It was not easy. It was not the kind of easy that comes from friendship or from tiredness, but the other kind, the kind that comes from two people who have been at the same table for a long time and have finally put their elbows on it and are going to sit there. Their techniques argued at every step. Elena's dough went slow and low, turned by hand, folded in the way Rosa had folded it, and it rose in the dark like a breath. Theo's went fast and hot, scored deep, baked in a high burst that gave it a crust that cracked and split at the edges like a wave breaking on the rocks. They argued over the salt. He put it in before the knead. She put it in after, and it mattered, and they both knew it mattered, and they argued over the water, and the rain, and the dulse, and the length of the second rise, and the angle of the blade, and the sound the crust made when it was done.

And then, on the second night, with the storm still out there and the tea going cold and the oven ticking as it cooled, they stopped arguing.

It happened the way it usually happens: one of them reached for something the other had already put down, and their hands met, and they both stepped back, and one of them laughed. It was Elena who laughed. She laughed at the way his hands were moving when she wasn't looking at them, the way he was testing the dough the way she did, with the heel of her palm and the side of her fingers, and she said, "You've been doing it the long way."

He looked at her. "For six months," he said. "I've been doing it the long way. I just didn't know the name for it."

They stood in the south kitchen with the lantern on the table and the rain on the window and two loaves on the bench, and they talked. Not about the bakery, not about the key, not about the north side or the south side. They talked about the sea, and the way the salt had changed, and the way the bread had changed with it, and the way they had both been trying to hold on to the old loaf and the new one at the same time and could not say which one was the lie. Elena told him about the morning her grandmother had come home from the sea with a jar of dulse and a loaf under her arm and put both of them on the table without a word. Theo told her about the fire, and about the drive to the coast in the dark, and about the first loaf he had ever baked in a city, which had been a disaster, and how he had eaten it standing up over the sink because he could not bring himself to throw it out.

They were done at four in the morning, and there were two loaves on the worktable, one with a dark crust and an open honeyed crumb, the other with a cracked edge and a crust that sounded like the sea, and they looked at each other and said nothing for a long time, and then Elena said, "Bring your apron. We're going up to the lighthouse."

The Founding Tide came in with the first calm of the season.

The headland was full by nine, and the lighthouse door was open, and the stone oven was warm, and the square below was quiet in the way a town is quiet when it is holding something in its hands and not ready to say what it is. Elena stood at the door with the two loaves on a board, and Theo stood beside her, and the mayor stood behind them with the iron key in his hand, and the three judges—the pilot, Old Reyes, and Mrs. Idris from the fish stall—stood in front of the board with their knives and their tea and their long quiet faces.

They broke the north loaf first. Elena broke it, because it was her bread, and the crumb came apart clean and open, and the smell went up out of the square and out to the sea. Then Theo broke his, and the crust split with a sound that the whole crowd heard, and the smell came up out of the square and out to the sea in the same way, and the two smells mixed, and no one could say which one was the old one and which one was the new one, and that, Elena thought, was the point.

They ate. They talked. They broke again, and again, and the pilot put his knife down and said, in the way he said everything, that he had eaten better bread in a city and a country and a storm, and that he could not say which one of these two loaves he preferred, and that he did not want to, and Mrs. Idris said, in the way she said everything, which was with a piece of bread in her mouth and the rest coming after, "I'm not saying this is a tie, I'm saying I'm not saying which one, and I'm saying I'm going to eat the rest."

The mayor put the key on the board between the two loaves, and he looked at them, and he looked at the square, and he looked at the lighthouse door, and he said, "The key was made for a single hand. The oven was made for two. The council will have to decide what to do about that, and I would like to suggest that we let the bread decide."

The square went quiet in the way a town goes quiet when it has been handed something it did not expect, and the lighthouse beam turned out in the grey morning light, and the sea at the head of the town was flat and grey and full of the first gulls, and Elena reached for the key, and Theo reached for the board, and the two of them stood in the door of the lighthouse with the key between them and the square below and the sea out there, and she thought of Brin on the shelf in the south kitchen, and of the stone hearth in the north kitchen with its crack running through it, and of the fountain in the square with its coin of water, and she thought that the line had been there a long time, and that it had been the wrong line all along, and that the real line was the one that ran from the north oven to the south oven, and that it was made of salt, and that it was the only line that mattered.

On the last day of the festival, a child came to the fountain in the square with a piece of chalk and drew a line in the stone, and then another child came with another piece of chalk, and the first line went on and on, and the second line went over it, and then a third, and then the rain came, and the lines went, and the square was clean, and the water in the fountain was still, and the two ovens were warm, and the lighthouse door was open, and the bread was breaking, and the sea was out there, and it was enough.

It was enough, and it was the same as enough, and it was the same as the sea, and it was the same as the bread, and it was the same as the two fires in the square, and it was the same as the line that was not a line, and it was the same as the two loaves on the board, and it was the same as the key, and it was the same as the hand that reached for the key, and it was the same as the hand that reached for the board, and it was the same as the two of them standing in the door of the lighthouse with the key between them and the square below and the sea out there, and it was the same as enough, and it was the same as the same, and it was the same as the same, and it was the same as the same.
