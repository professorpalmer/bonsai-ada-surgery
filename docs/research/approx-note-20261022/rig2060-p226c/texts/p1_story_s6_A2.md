## final text

# The Letters at Kettle Point

The first letter arrived in October, folded into a shape the wind had not touched, set upon the brass bell-rung of the lighthouse door in the blue hour before dawn. Elias Finch had kept Kettle Point for thirty-one years. He knew every creak of the iron stair, every salt-crust bloom on the gallery rail, every way the gale folded the fog around the lantern room. He knew the mail, too. Two a month, the government reports on lamp oil and the quarterly check from the Maritime Authority, both in dry, official handwriting.

This letter was in a hand that seemed to be learning how to hold a pen. The ink wobbled. It said:

*Dear Mr. Finch, I am sorry to write to you. I don't think you will believe this, or even that I am real, but I have been trying for a long time to reach you and the letters keep coming back to me in the water. I am going to be a girl soon. I am going to be very small and I am going to have brown hair and I am going to live in the house on the cliff path with my mother, and one day she will tell me that there is a lighthouse keeper on Kettle Point and I will want to come and see him, and I hope by then you will still be here. I hope you will still be keeping the light. I am so sorry I am writing from a time that hasn't happened yet. Please don't throw it away.*

Elias read it three times. Then he sat on the kitchen chair, the letter in his lap, and listened to the sea throw itself against the rocks below with the patience of something that had been doing it since before the first lighthouse was built.

He kept the letter in his coat pocket for two weeks.

The second came in November. Same slanted, uncertain hand. The ink was blue this time. *I am six now, though I don't feel it yet. I have a dog named Sable. I am afraid of thunderstorms and I pretend I am not afraid of them. My mother is painting something on the wall in the kitchen and it looks like a map of somewhere that doesn't exist. I keep thinking about you. I don't know how I know your name. I think it's because you keep the light on, and I can see it from the cliff path in the evenings, and it looks like someone is saying please don't go out there. I am beginning to understand why I am writing to you. I think I have been alone for a very long time in a way I can't explain.*

Elias folded it into his breast pocket, over his heart, as if the letter might fall out with the rain.

Winter came. The letters continued, one every two weeks or so, appearing on the doorstep with no postmark, no stamp, no sign of having crossed any distance at all. They described a life in slow, tender detail. A girl's life. A small life. The smell of toast on a cold morning. The way her mother's paint got into the creases of her fingers. The way the dog barked at the lighthouse beam as if it were a ship coming in. Each letter ended the same: *I am not here yet. I am so sorry. I hope the light is still on for me when I arrive.*

Elias began to leave a lamp burning in the kitchen window. He didn't know who was watching it. He didn't need to know.

The letters changed as the year turned. The penmanship steadied. The sentences grew longer, more tangled, more adult. The girl was twelve in February. She wrote about school, about a boy she liked and didn't like, about her mother's illness, about the way Kettle Point looked in spring when the ice went out and the rocks were bare and the whole coastline seemed to breathe. *I'm coming up the cliff path on a Saturday,* she wrote in March. *I'll look for you in the lamp room. If you're not there, I'll leave this letter. If I am there and I'm not, that's also fine.*

Elias went out to the gallery on that Saturday. The sun was low and the sea was a flat sheet of pewter. He saw her on the path below, small and brown-haired, pulling her coat tighter against the wind. She looked up. He stood very still in the iron railing, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

Then she raised one hand, a small wave, and turned back toward the village.

She never came up. She had left the letter in the bell-rung as she'd promised. *I came as far as the path gate. I don't think I can go higher. I'm sorry. But I'm glad. I'm glad I can see you. That's enough for now.*

Elias read it on the stairs, one step at a time.

The letters continued. Fourteen, fifteen, eighteen. She was eighteen when the last one came, written in a hand so sure it barely needed to be there at all. *I'm going to leave for a long time. I'm going to see all of it. I'm going to be angry at some of it and fall in love with other parts of it and I don't know who I will be when I come back. But I want you to know that I was always writing to you. Every letter. Every one of them. I wanted someone to know that I existed, even before I did. I wanted someone to know the shape of my life before it had a body. I think that's strange, I know it is. But you made it not strange. You kept the light on.*

The final line: *Thank you, Mr. Finch. I'll be on the cliff path next year. I'll be thirty. I won't be afraid anymore.*

Elias read it in the lamp room, the great Fresnel lens turning above him, the beam sweeping out into the dark like a hand reaching for something it could not hold. He set the letter on the railing. He turned the key in the lamp. The light went on.

The next letter never came.

But on the cliff path, in the evenings, he could see a small figure walking the gate, standing still, looking up. She would stay until the wind picked up, then turn and go home. She would not wave. She didn't need to.

Elias Finch kept Kettle Point for two more years, and then the Maritime Authority sent their people with their cameras and their digital timers and their polite, final forms. He packed his coat and his few books and his small brass compass. He packed the letters, all of them, into the leather satchel he'd carried since the day he first took the post.

On the last night, he stood on the gallery and turned off the great lamp by hand, the last manual act in a long, slow history of light. The beam died. The dark came in.

In the morning, there was a letter on the bell-rung.

It was in a steady hand. Brown hair, she'd written once. A small life. A long life. *I'm thirty-two now and I have a daughter. She has brown hair too. She asks me about the lighthouse. I tell her about a man who kept a light for a stranger. I tell her that the light doesn't need a reason to burn. I tell her that she is not writing from a time that hasn't happened. I tell her the light is still on.*

Elias read it, folded it, and put it in his coat.

He took the boat down to the village at first light. He walked the cliff path once more, slowly, the way the wind made him walk, the way the years had taught him. The gate stood open. The path led down to the village and out to the road.

He did not look back.

But he left the light on.

Not the lamp. The other one. The one in the kitchen window. The one that said *you are not alone, you are not not here, you are not a letter from a time that hasn't happened.* The one that said *the light is still on. The light is still on. The light is still on.*

And in the morning, in the house on the cliff path, a woman set a cup of tea in a small girl's hand and said, *There. There's your light.* And the girl looked out the window, out across the dark water to Kettle Point, where the old tower stood against the sky, and the lens was gone and the lamp was gone, but the window was lit, and the light went out and out and out, and the girl said, *It's still on,* and the woman said, *It's still on.*
