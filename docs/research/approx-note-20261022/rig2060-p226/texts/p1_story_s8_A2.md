# The Unwritten Year

The lighthouse at Carrow Point had no name, only a number stenciled in peeling white paint on the iron door: 7. For thirty-one years, Elias Wren had been its keeper, and in that time the lighthouse had become less a building and more a body—its brass gears a heartbeat, its windows a pair of eyes that never blinked in the fog.

It was October when the first letter came.

He found it at the base of the lamp room stairs, pressed under the iron door, the envelope sealed with a thumbprint of red wax the color of a wound. No postmark. No return address. Only his name, written in a hand so small and slanted it looked like a child trying to imitate cursive.

*Dear Mr. Wren,* it read. *My name is Mara. I am nine years old. I want to tell you about the winter I am still waiting for.*

Elias read it three times. Then he read it four. He set it on the kitchen table beside his cold tea and stared at it until the words lost their shape and became only ink.

He was seventy-three. He had not received a letter of any kind since his wife, Helen, had passed in the winter of 2016, and even then only the formal card from the parish office. Carrow Point was not a place that received mail, really. The postal van came once a fortnight and left what it could, and most weeks the bundle was nothing but a catalog for fishing nets and a government pamphlet on energy conservation.

But the letter had arrived.

He read the rest that evening by the lamp, after he had wound the great clock and checked the wick and climbed the one hundred and forty-three steps for the final time. The hand was indeed small. The sentences were short.

*I saw a bird today. A puffin, but not on the rocks—on the cliff above the lighthouse where the grass goes soft. It had a chip in its beak like a little broken tooth. I held my breath so you would hear it.*

Elias set the letter down. Above him, through the glass of the lantern room, the sea was a flat grey sheet, calm as poured lead. There were no puffins on the upper cliff. There had not been puffins on that stretch of headland for at least a decade; the weather had made them too unpredictable to return.

He should have tossed the letter. He should have called the harbor master and said, *Someone left a strange letter, I don't know who.* And he would have, in another year, in another life, when the world was still a place that made sense in its own ordinary way. But he did not toss it. He put it in his coat pocket and slept with it against his chest, and he did not know why.

---

The second letter came on a Thursday. The third on a Friday. They came one after another, like small, patient birds landing on the windowsill of a house no one lived in.

Mara wrote about nothing. Or rather, she wrote about everything, but everything in the small way a child sees everything.

*Today I counted the cracks in the kitchen ceiling. There are seventeen if you don't count the one shaped like a rabbit, and eighteen if you do. I think the rabbit one is the real one because it looks like it's running.*

*Mr. Wren, do you know if stars are just fire or if they're also something else? Like a memory the sky can't quite forget?*

*My mother says the sea is always the same but it isn't. She says that's what makes it the sea. I don't understand. I want to ask you because you live next to it.*

Elias began to notice things in his own life that matched. The cracks in the ceiling of his kitchen—there were seventeen. The eighteenth was shaped, if you squinted, like a rabbit in mid-leap. He stood on a step ladder in the blue early light and stared at it for a long time, his hands still holding the rail, his breath shallow.

The letters continued for six weeks. Mara wrote about rain and about the color of her mother's scarf in November and about a boy at school who stole her pencil and how she had not cried, which was the important part. She wrote about a dream in which she stood in a lighthouse and the light was not light but a sound, a low hum like a cello string left loose.

On the twenty-third day, she wrote:

*My mother says you are the last keeper. That there will be no more keepers at Carrow Point because the light is automatic now. She says I might be the last person who remembers you by name. I don't like that. I want to say your name out loud and hear it in my own mouth so it can't disappear.*

Elias held the letter with both hands. He had been told, months before his last contract ended, that the automated system would be installed by spring. The Ministry had sent a surveyor in August who had measured the door and the stairs and the lamp room and said, in the flat way of government men, that no further human maintenance was anticipated.

He had not grieved about it. He had grieved for Helen. He had grieved for the years. He had grieved, in the small, private way of a man who has done one thing for his whole life and is being asked to stop, for the fact that the work would simply... end. Not in a dramatic way. The light would keep turning. The ship would still be warned. But he would not be there to feel the weight of it, the slow grinding of the great lens, the particular cold of the stone at four in the morning when the fog came in thick as a wool blanket.

And now, in a letter from a girl who did not yet exist, someone was saying: *I don't want you to disappear.*

---

By December, the letters had become a routine as fixed as winding the clock. He kept them in a tin box on the shelf beside the cookstove, stacked in the order they arrived. He did not send anything back. He did not know where to send it to. There was no address. There was only *Mara*, and the red wax, and the small slanted hand.

But he began to answer, in his head, in the quiet hours between the light's long arcs. He told Mara about the gulls. He told her about the year a ship called the *Cormorant* had run aground in a squall and how the crew had been rescued in a boat the size of a breadbin, and how they had sat on the rocks in their wet oilskins drinking tea out of the lighthouse kettles, and how they had never come back to thank him, which was all right. He told her that the light was not just fire or a memory. It was both, and it was also a kind of promise. *I am here. You are not alone. Turn your bow. I will not go dark.*

He did not know if the letters were real. He did not know what he should believe. He knew only that on the coldest nights, when the wind came off the North Channel with the force of a fist and the lighthouse groaned and the whole structure hummed like a struck bell, he felt less alone. Not because anyone was with him. Because someone, somewhere in the not-yet, was counting the cracks in a ceiling and wondering about stars.

---

In February, the letters stopped.

It was a Tuesday. The morning fog sat in the valley like milk poured over a plate, and he climbed the steps, wound the clock, lit the lamp, and came back down to find nothing on the stairs. No red wax. No small hand.

He waited. He waited through the afternoon and the evening and the night. He waited through the next day and the next.

On the ninth morning, there was a letter on the iron door. It was shorter than the others.

*I think I finished. I think the winter you were waiting for is over, or I've finished writing about it. I don't know. My mother says I have to start a new notebook. I don't know what I'll say in the next one. I'm going to try.*

Then, after a space, in letters even smaller and shakier:

*Thank you for the name. I said it out loud today. I said it in my mouth and it was real.*

Elias set the letter on the table. He made tea. He stood at the window and looked out over the water, which was, as ever, always the same and never the same.

He did not write back. He did not need to. The tin box on the shelf was full, and the letters were his, and they would outlast him, and that was all right. That was the point. The light turned, and the ship passed, and the fog lifted, and the name he had kept safe in the only way a man could keep a name—by saying it to a girl in a notebook who had not yet been born, or who would not yet be remembered by anyone else—was real. It had weight. It had been spoken.

---

He left Carrow Point in April, as the automated system went in. The surveyor's men came with their boxes and their wires and their flat-faced machines that would keep the lens turning without hands, without eyes, without a man who had counted one hundred and forty-three steps for thirty-one years and learned the particular groan each iron rung made in the cold.

On his last night, Elias stood in the lamp room. The light turned above him, steady as a slow breath. He placed his palm on the brass housing and felt the vibration travel up his wrist, into the bones, into the old ache of his shoulder. It was not a fire. It was not a memory. It was the sound of a cello string, low and long, and it went on and on.

Below him, on the kitchen table, the tin box sat in the dark. And in the tin box, stacked in the order of a small slanted hand, were the letters of a girl who had not yet been born, who was still waiting, in her way, for the winter to end.
