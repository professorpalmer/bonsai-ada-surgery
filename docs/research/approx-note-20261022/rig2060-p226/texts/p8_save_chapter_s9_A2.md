# Chapter One: The Shell-Cities

The tide had been rising for three days, and by the third morning, the children of the lower Warrens had started to sing the Drowning Hymn again.

Kael pressed her ear against the barnacle-crusted plating of the walkway railing and listened. Not for the hymn—she'd heard it a hundred times, the wailing notes that climbed the spiral stairs and faded into the salt-heavy air above. She was listening for the *thump*. The deep, slow pulse that rose from somewhere beneath everything, from the great living body that carried the entire city of Vael-Orun above the world's oceans.

It came, as it always did. A tremor that started in her bones and traveled upward through the stone soles of her boots. The turtle's heartbeat. Slow as a glacier. Ancient. Unchanged.

"Stop that."

Kael turned. Old Maren stood at the railing beside her, her weathered face a topography of wrinkles, her hands wrapped around a cup of bitter kelp tea. The old woman's eyes were fixed on the horizon where the turtle's vast neck disappeared into a bank of morning fog.

"I'm listening," Kael said. "The rhythm's off by a half-beat. I've counted it."

Maren set down her cup. "You've been counting since you were old enough to climb. And every time you've noticed an off-beat, you've come to me with it, and every time I've told you the same thing."

"And?"

"That the giant does not keep time, child. It keeps *being*."

Kael rolled her eyes, but the tension in her shoulders eased. It always did. Maren's words were a small anchor, a way to stop the spiraling thoughts that had been eating at her since the tide started climbing.

Three hundred feet below them, the city spread across the broad, domed carapace of the creature in a patchwork of terraced districts. The Shell-Cities, the scholars called them—though the people who lived there just said *here*. The lower Warrens, where Kael grew up and now worked as a tide-runner, clung to the forward edge of the shell near where the turtle's eyes would have been if it were a lesser beast. Above that, in the broadest and flattest stretch of the carapace, rose the Crown Warrens: the spires of the Shell-Council, the great observatories with their brass lenses, the temple of the Deep Patience where the High Tetherers sang to the turtle's mind and kept it calm, kept it *moving*.

And beyond the Crown, the rear Warrens. The quieter districts. The families who had lived there so long they'd forgotten what the ocean looked like from the inside, if the turtle ever dove.

Kael was twenty-two, lean as a cutlass, with a scar across her left knuckle from a rope burn gone bad and a braid of dark hair that she'd been meaning to cut for six months. She was a tide-runner, which meant she climbed the rigging lines at the base of the shell to check the barnacle growth, the coral encrustations, the slow creep of the ocean's patience against the creature's skin. It was honest work. It was the only work that didn't ask you to believe that a hundred-million-year-old turtle could think and feel and *choose* to carry four thousand people across the ocean.

She'd chosen to believe.

"You're still doing the tide checks tomorrow," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"I am."

"Then you'll be low when the tide hits the knee-plate. You'll see the water line climb. You'll feel it press against your shins like a hand."

"I know what it's going to look like."

"No. You won't." Maren's voice dropped. "You'll see the water and you'll remember your mother. She used to stand right there, on the first step of the Warren, with her hands in her lap, and she'd say, *Watch the water, Kael. The water tells you things before the turtle does.* She told you that every morning for twenty years. And now the water's coming up and you can't even bring yourself to look at it."

Kael's jaw tightened. She couldn't argue. She'd told herself she could. She'd told herself for three days. But the hymn in the lower Warrens had a different sound now, a lower register, and she'd stopped sleeping properly after the second night.

Her mother had been dead for eleven years. She'd fallen during a surge—a sudden, violent lurch of the turtle that no one could explain—and the sea had taken her before the tide-ropes caught. Kael had been twelve. She'd been on the rigging above her, checking for barnacles, and she'd looked down and seen the water rise up the steps like a slow, dark wave and she'd seen her mother's hands slip from the railing.

She'd not slept well since. She'd not looked at the water since.

"The High Tetherers are holding a service tomorrow," Maren said softly. "They'll be singing the Stillness Hymn in the Crown. If the tide keeps climbing, the water will reach the first steps of the lower Warrens by dusk. The children will be singing the Drowning Hymn all night, and the adults will pretend they're not listening."

"I know," Kael said again. But this time she didn't have an answer.

Maren put a hand on her shoulder. Her fingers were rough as sandpaper, warm from the tea. "Go back to your room. Put on your coat. And when you're in the rigging tomorrow, look at the water. Just once. Just to see what she saw."

Kael stood at the railing and watched the fog swallow the turtle's neck, and the fog swallow everything after it. Somewhere far below, a bell rang. The Shell-Council bell. The long, low note that rolled across the carapace and made the whole city hum.

She didn't go back to her room.

She started climbing.
