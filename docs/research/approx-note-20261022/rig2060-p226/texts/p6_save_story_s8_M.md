# The Salt of Forgetting

The hydroponic bay hummed at three in the morning, the way it always did—a low, green pulse, like the ship breathing. Elara pressed her palm against the glass panel of Bay Four and watched the rows of pale stalks lean toward their artificial sun, the light tuned to a frequency that no human eye had ever seen the sky deliver.

She was forty-three years old, the fourth in a line of cooks on the *Meridian*, and she had never once tasted rain.

"Morning, Elara." The voice came from behind her, slurred and warm. Joren, the botanist who'd been her friend since they were children hugging the same bunk in the family quarters, stood in the doorway with dirt on his knees and a grin that hadn't quite dried from yesterday. "They came up early. All of them. The whole row."

She turned. He was right. The bay was a riot of green—taller than last cycle, fuller, their leaves catching the blue-white glow in a way that made them look like little windows. "You've been feeding them," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Some root extract. Experimental. I figured if we're going to eat them, they might as well taste like *something*." He said the last word with a kind of tenderness that made her chest ache. "Like something from before."

"Before there isn't a before, Joren. You said it yourself. The Archive is incomplete. There's no recipe. No memory."

He shrugged, but his eyes were careful. "Maybe not. But there's a shape to it. The way a face is shaped. You can't see the whole thing, but the jaw's still there."

Elara nodded, and she did not argue. She never argued about it anymore. It had become a kind of weather, something that passed over her every few days and then settled into the background noise.

She pulled off her gloves and went to the galley.

The galley was a small room on Deck Nine, no wider than a bedroom, all stainless steel and the ghost-smell of a hundred meals. The walls were lined with spice racks—salt, pepper, turmeric, cumin, paprika, a dozen others whose names she had learned from the Archive's fragmentary text files, from a woman in the first generation who had been a chemist and kept a notebook that said *this is what my mother used to smell like when she cooked*. Elara kept that woman's notebook in a drawer beside the stove, the leather cracked and soft as skin.

She opened the drawer. The notebook smelled of nothing now. Of course. Nothing that isn't there.

She began to cook.

The recipe was not a recipe, not really. It was an accumulation. A stew made of whatever the bays gave that week—protein from the algae vats, starch from the tubers that grew in the dark soil of Bay Seven, a thin broth built from bones that the ship's medical team collected and returned, "for use," they'd said, with the same flat courtesy they used for everything. She had been cooking the same stew for eleven years. The same ratio. The same heat. The same small, almost inaudible sigh she made when the broth finally turned the color of weak tea.

But tonight was different. Tonight there was the new crop.

She put a handful of the leaves in the pot. They hissed. The smell that came up was not nothing, but it was not *anything*, not in the way she meant. It was green in the way that a hospital room was green. Clean. Uncommitted. It didn't pull at her. It didn't remind her of anything because there was nothing to remind her of, and she had stopped trying to make it remind her.

Joren came in at some point and sat at the small counter, where the cook always ate, and watched. He did not ask her to make the new leaves taste like something. He just sat. He put a piece of the bread she'd baked that morning—a dense, sour thing, the only bread that worked with what they had—and tore it into his mouth and chewed slowly.

"Does it taste like home?" he asked.

Elara considered the question. Home was a word she had stopped using, but not for the reason people thought. It wasn't that she'd forgotten. She'd never known home. It was that the word had become too heavy, too loaded, like a stone you carried in your pocket for no reason. If you asked her where home was, she would have to tell him the ship, and the ship was not a place. It was a duration. A way of being that ended without her knowing, probably, when one of the younger crew looked up at the ceiling and said, *is it time yet*, and she would have to say *not yet*, and it would be true, and it would not be true, and she would not know which one she'd meant.

"It tastes like the fourth year of the long winter," she said. "When the protein yield dropped and we had to stretch the salt. You remember."

"I remember being six. I remember being hungry and not knowing why."

"You were hungry because the bays were tired. Like all the bays get tired. Every few years. They need rest. They need us to stop pulling from them and let them just... be. Be green in the dark. Be nothing for a while." She stirred the stew. "This crop is different because it's the first time in a generation they've been resting. You've been feeding them for three cycles. They've been *growing*, not *producing*. There's a difference."

Joren was quiet for a moment. Outside, the hum of the bay shifted a half-tone lower, the way it did when the lights cycled for the overnight dim. "Do you think they'll make it?" he asked. "The ship. To the place in the maps."

"Maps are not promises."

"They're something we walk by. That's all they are. We walk by them and we say, *there, I'm going there*, and we keep walking, and if we stop, we don't have to be somewhere else."

Elara looked at the stew. The broth was the color of weak tea now, as it should be. She added the last pinch of salt.

"I think we'll make it," she said, and she was surprised to find that she believed it. Not because of any map or any destination, but because of this: because the bays were resting and coming back, because the leaves tasted green in a way that was not nothing, because she had stood here in the dark and made the same soup for eleven years and it had, every single time, been enough.

She ladled a bowl for him. He took it. They sat in the small galley and ate, and the stew was warm and salty and not much else, and it was exactly right.

When they were finished, Elara scraped the pot clean and rinsed the leaves that had fallen into the broth into the waste recycler, watching the green dissolve into a thin stream and disappear. She opened the drawer and took out the old notebook. The woman who had kept it was dead now. A long time dead. Her handwriting had faded to a brownish ghost, but the last entry was still legible, in a small, precise hand:

*This is what I can still remember. The smell of tomatoes after rain. Not the taste. The smell. The green, wet, almost sour smell. If you ever get it, if the bays ever give you something that smells like that, you'll know. You'll know without knowing. It'll just be there, in your chest, and you'll cry a little, and you'll know you're not the first to be here.*

Elara closed the drawer. She put her hands in the basin of hot water. They smelled of salt and green and the faint, clean nothingness of the new crop. She dried them on her apron and walked out into the corridor, where the lights were dim and the ship was humming its long, low note, the sound of ten thousand people sleeping, of ten thousand lives being continued, of a journey with no end that had become, somehow, a kind of home.

She did not cry. Not yet. But she thought about tomatoes. About rain. About a smell that had never existed and that, in a way, had.

She went back to the galley and began to clean the counters, because there was always more to do, and the ship was still moving, and somewhere in the bays a row of pale green stalks was leaning, slowly, toward a light that was not the sun and was not, yet, anything else.

It was enough.

For now, it was enough.
