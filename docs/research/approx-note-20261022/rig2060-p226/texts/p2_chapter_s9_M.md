## final text

# CHAPTER ONE: DEPARTURES AND ARRIVALS

The coffee in Ray Dunmore's percolator screamed at 5:12 in the morning, the same way it had screamed for eleven years, and the same way it would scream every morning until the day it gave out entirely. He stood at the window of his one-story house on the edge of Marrow Creek, watching the fog peel itself off the valley floor like wet newspaper, and he watched it the way he used to watch the switchbacks out of Kettle Junction. Methodically. Looking for the thing that wasn't supposed to be there.

The old rail line was gone. Had been gone since the highway swallowed it, section by section, over the course of two unglamorous years in the late nineties. The ties were still in the ground in the places the road crews hadn't dug them up, and in the spring the grass grew through the gaps between them like something trying to remember. Ray walked that stretch every evening after supper, mostly for the exercise, mostly for the silence. The town had no silence. Not anymore. Not with the new logging operation at the bottom of the ridge and the Friday-night crowds at the Rye & Barrow. But the rail bed had silence. It had the particular silence of a place that had been walked by a thousand boots and was now walked by only one.

Ray was sixty-three. He was five-foot-ten, and he carried himself the way he'd carried himself on the A-2 line for thirty-four years: slightly forward, shoulders loose, weight on the balls of his feet, ready to move. His hands were the wrong hands for a man his age. They were too big, too rough, the knuckles swollen into permanent knots from decades of gripping brass handles and pulling levers. The town doctor had said, more than once, that he should be doing less. Ray had said, more than once, that he'd do what the doctor said the moment the doctor told him to take his pension money and move to a place where the air didn't taste like pine sap and diesel.

He poured the coffee. He did not drink it. He set the cup on the counter, where it would go cold, and put on his boots.

The house belonged to the town. Had always belonged to the town, technically. His wife, June, had left it to him in her will, along with a list of instructions so specific it read like a train manifest: *The good fork is the second one, not the one you think. The dog food is in the blue bin, not the green. Call Margaret on Sundays. Do not open the door for the men in the gray trucks. Do not trust the man who sells apples from the van.* He'd followed every instruction, because that's what he'd been trained to do, and because June had been dead nine years and there was no one left to argue with her about which fork.

The air was wet on his face by the time he reached the mailbox. There was nothing in it. A folded newspaper from the *Marrow Creek Gazette*, and a postcard from a sister-in-law in Tucson. He set both on the kitchen counter.

He did not go to the diner. He did not stop at the post office to check the mail a second time, though he'd been doing that every day for three years, ever since June died, ever since the quiet had gotten into his house and started to eat at the walls. He walked the rail bed. He always walked the rail bed.

The tunnel was at the two-mile mark. The old Kettle Creek Tunnel, half a mile long, bored through the hillside on the old line. The railroad company, long since bankrupt and long since sold off to a shell company in Asheville, still technically owned the right-of-way. The tunnel was sealed with a steel plate and a padlock, and the plate had rusted through in the place where the mountain seeped water in the winter, so that the lock hung on one hinge, more suggestion than security.

Ray stopped at the tree line. He stood there for a moment, breathing in. The smell of the place was the smell of everything: wet stone, iron, the black mold that lived in the cracks of the concrete. He'd worked this section in his first decade on the line. He'd walked this tunnel every night for twenty-two years. He knew the exact spot where the ceiling drooped low enough to scrape the top of a man's head. He knew the exact spot where a rat had once chewed through the wire on the emergency lamp and set the whole tunnel dark for a night and a day.

He pulled the plate off the hinges. It came away with a shriek of rust and metal that would have set his teeth on edge if he'd still been a conductor, still had been listening for the whistle.

The dark inside was not the dark he remembered. It was thicker. It had a weight to it, like the air had been compressed in a space that had been closed for too long. He switched on his headlamp. The beam cut a pale cone into the black and stopped at the far wall, about two hundred and forty feet from the entrance. He'd never been able to see the far wall from the entrance. The tunnel was a little longer than he'd ever told anyone it was. That was the thing about the rail bed: it had secrets it kept from the men who worked it, and it kept them with the patience of stone.

Ray walked in.

He walked the way he'd walked the line. Slow, steady, counting his steps. One-two-three-four. He counted because counting kept the mind from wandering into the parts of the mind that asked questions. He counted to thirty. To sixty. He was somewhere around the hundred-and-thirties when the beam of his headlamp caught something that was not a rock.

It was a boot. A woman's boot. Size eight, he judged, though it could have been a nine. It was brown leather, scuffed at the toe, and it was sitting in a puddle of water that had no business being there. The tunnel floor was dry. He'd walked this tunnel in every season of every year for two decades. It was dry.

Ray stopped. He stood still. He let the silence do its work.

The boot was not the only thing. There was another shape in the beam of his light, a few feet past the boot, and it took him another three seconds to understand what it was, because the mind is a lazy thing and it will build you a story that is easier to look at than the truth. A shape in a puddle. A thing that was not a thing. A thing that was not what it looked like.

It was a body.

Ray Dunmore, retired train conductor, who had walked this tunnel ten thousand times and never once seen this, stood very still and counted to thirty again.

One-two-three-four. Five-six-seven-eight.

His hands were shaking. He did not think about this. He did not think about it the way a man thinks about a thing that is breaking inside him, because that was a feeling he did not have the time for. He thought about it the way he'd thought about the track: *Is it clear? Is the gauge right? Is the signal set for the next train?*

The body was a woman. She was on her back, and she was wearing a dark coat, and her head was turned away from the entrance, so that Ray could not see her face. The water around her had the color of rust, and it was spreading in a slow circle, and the smell of it was not the smell of the tunnel. It was the smell of something fresh and wrong, like a cut apple left too long in a warm room.

Ray Dunmore reached into his jacket pocket and took out his phone. He did not know why he had the phone in that particular pocket. He had carried it there since the winter of '98, when his left hand had gone numb on the A-2 and he'd needed to reach the corded radio without feeling the metal of it. It had never mattered. It had never mattered for twenty-six years.

He dialed 911.

He told them his name. He told them the address: the old Kettle Creek Tunnel, two miles north of Marrow Creek, off the rail bed, south of the logging road. He told them the coordinates because the phone asked for them and he knew them, because he'd memorized them the first year, the way you memorize a schedule, because the schedule was the whole point of the thing, because a man who does not know where he is going has no business being a conductor, and he had been a conductor for a very long time and he knew where he was.

He told them there was a woman in the tunnel. He told them he did not know if she was alive. He did not tell them that he was kneeling on the cold concrete and that the water was moving toward him and that it was very, very cold, and that he could not see the face of the woman in the dark, and that his hands would not stop shaking, and that he was thinking about the fork, the good fork, the second one, not the one you think.

He told them he was coming to the entrance. He told them to bring a light. He told them that the plate on the door would be open, and that the lock was on the inside, and that he had left the plate where it fell.

He hung up.

The phone was cold in his hand. The tunnel was cold. The water was spreading.

Ray Dunmore stood up. He walked toward the body. He walked the way he'd walked the line. Slow, steady, counting.

One-two-three-four.

He got to her. She was wearing a dark coat, and her boots were brown leather, and her head was turned away from the entrance, and the water was coming up to her knees, and the light from his headlamp was going soft at the edges because the battery was dying, and he could hear the fog pressing against the mouth of the tunnel, and he could hear the water, and he could not hear his own breathing, and he thought, very clearly, for the first time in eleven years, that he was going to have to go back to work.

One-two-three-four.

He reached the far side of the body, where the beam of the lamp was still strong, and he saw her face.

It was a face he knew.

It was the face of the woman who had been standing in the doorway of the Rye & Barrow on a Tuesday night in November, holding a bottle of rye whiskey in one hand and a photograph in the other, and looking at him with the particular, terrible calm of a woman who had already made her decision and was waiting for him to make hers.

It was the face of Claire Marchetti.

And Claire Marchetti had been dead for six years.

Ray Dunmore stood in the tunnel of the dead railway, in the water, in the cold, in the dark that was not quite dark anymore because the headlamp was still on and the fog was still pressing in, and he understood, with the absolute and terrible clarity of a man who has been reading the tracks for thirty-four years and has never once been wrong, that he had not found a body in a tunnel.

He had found a departure.

And the train was coming.

By the time the first patrol car turned onto the logging road, its lights sweeping through the pines and catching the steel plate hanging open on one hinge, Ray Dunmore was sitting on the edge of the old rail bed, his boots off, his boots soaking on the grass. His coat was off. His shirt was damp with sweat and something else, something he would not name. His phone was on the ground beside him, screen cracked, the 911 call still playing in a loop if you held it up to the speaker.

The patrol car stopped. The lights stopped. A door opened.

"Ray?"

It was Dale. Dale Kowalski, the only other cop in Marrow Creek after the county pulled the second one to Boone two years ago and the town hadn't been strong enough to argue. Dale was fifty-five, red-faced, and he had the face of a man who had been drinking since the shift started and intended to finish the bottle before the body did.

"You all right, Ray?" Dale asked. He got out of the car. His boots were on the wet ground. He looked at the open tunnel. He looked at Ray.

"You went in," Dale said. It was not a question.

"I went in."

"Jesus, Ray." Dale was at the tunnel mouth before he finished the sentence. He shone his flashlight in. He stood there, still, and the beam went in and came back out and went in again, and the water at the bottom was very still, and the light did not reach the far wall, and Dale Kowalski, who had been a cop for twenty-one years, made a sound in his throat that was not quite a sound, and then he called in the state police and the medical examiner and the fire department, and he did not look at Ray.

Ray looked at him.

"Who is it?" Dale said.

"I know who it is," Ray said.

Dale looked at him. "You don't know who it is. You don't know anything about this. You're not a cop. You're a man with a phone and a bad knee who lives at the edge of town and walks in the woods."

Ray stood up. His knees made a sound. He put his hands on his thighs. He looked at the tunnel.

"It's Claire Marchetti," he said.

Dale stared at him. "That's not possible. Claire's been dead since '18. I was the one who closed the file. I was at the funeral. I knew her. She was—"

"I know."

"She was *who?*"

Ray did not answer. He was already walking away from the tunnel. He was walking back along the rail bed, toward the tree line, toward the house, toward the good fork and the blue bin and the Sundays and the men in the gray trucks, and he was counting, and he was counting, and he was counting, and the fog was coming up out of the valley and pressing against the trees and pressing against the road and pressing against the town, and he could feel it the way he had always felt the tracks: not with his feet, but with the part of the mind that had been trained to listen to the sound of the rails and to know, before the train was even there, that something was wrong, that something was coming, that the schedule was broken and there was no one to fix it but him.

And he was sixty-three years old, and he was alone, and the train was coming.

One-two-three-four.

One-two-three-four.

One-two-three-four.
