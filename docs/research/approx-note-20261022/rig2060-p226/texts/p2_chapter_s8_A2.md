# Chapter One: The Dead Mile

The fog came in off the Bluewater River every morning at the same time it had for the last thirty-two years, curling up the eastern slope of Brenner's Hollow like something alive deciding whether to stay. Walt Grady watched it from his kitchen window, coffee going cold in a mug that said *World's Okayest Conductor* in peeling blue lettering, and felt the familiar tightening in his chest that came with the hour. Three-one-five. He could tell it from the way the fog lay thick enough to swallow the far ridge, even though no clock on the wall had ticked past the actual time. His body kept the schedule. His hands did. His back, if you pressed the right spot, did.

Forty-two years on the 11:15 to Davenport and back. Forty-two years of counting the same thirty-one stations, of hearing the clack-clack-clack of the couplings settling, of knowing which sleepers had the bad spots that would make a man's teeth ache. He'd taken the pension in November, three months after the company sold the line to the logging operation and the last passenger train rolled into the yard. Now the only train he walked was the one around the neighborhood: the loop to the general store, the post office, the old railyard past the creek, and back. A four-mile walk. Forty minutes if he took his time.

He'd been taking his time. For three days, he'd been taking it a little too slowly, which meant he was at the water tower bridge at quarter to four and the sun had already cleared the west peaks. His knees made a sound like gravel shifting, and his wife, Margot, had started leaving a plate of toast on the counter with the note *Eat something, you stubborn mule* in her handwriting, which still had that slight leftward slant she'd developed after the cataract surgery.

He set the mug down on the counter. It made a small sound against the wood, the only sound in the kitchen. Outside, the fog was thinning where the sun pushed through, turning the trees along the creek into gray silhouettes.

He'd been going to the railyard every morning for two weeks. Not really *to* it, exactly. More of a... checking. His hands wanted it. The old habit of making sure the switch stands were set right, the gates were latched, the tie-rod on the north turntable wasn't snapped. The railyard had been closed since 2003, but the town had never quite figured out what to do with it. Not a demolition. Not a park. Just a place where the weeds grew up between the rails and the old signal tower stood like a crooked finger pointing at the sky.

He'd told himself it was the knees. That walking the loop was the same as walking the yard.

He'd been lying to himself for two weeks.

The yard was the same as always. Or rather, it was the same as always except for the fact that the weeds had grown another two inches in the six days since he'd last been there. The smell was the same: rust, wet wood, and the faint green tang of grass trying to grow through the gravel. The north gate was latched. The signal tower door hung off one hinge.

Walt walked the length of the dead spur, his good shoe finding the rhythm of the ties the way it always had. One-two. One-two. The sound of his walking against the gravel, the creak of the gate behind him. He counted the carriages of his mind, the way he used to count the cars in a consist. One, two, three, four, five—

He stopped.

There was a shape among the weeds. Not a person, not exactly. A shape that the mind wanted to call a person. He stood still for a long moment, the way he'd once stood on a platform and watched a train pull away, knowing something was leaving him behind. The fog was almost gone now, the trees along the creek just trees, and the shape was a shape. A body.

A woman.

She was slumped against the north wall of the old freight house, half in the weeds, half on the gravel. Her coat was a dark blue, too clean for the damp. Her hair was pulled back, or had been, a few strands loose. One arm hung across the gravel at an angle that made Walt's stomach do a small, quiet turning. The other was bent up, the hand near her chest.

She was not, he thought, *riding* anything.

But the way she was laid out, the precise geometry of it, the angle of the leg that was bent at the knee like a seat had been, like a body had been set down and arranged with a particular attention—

Walt crouched, his knees protesting in a language older than English. He looked at her face. The eyes were open. Not wide, not surprised. Just... open, the way a window is open after the storm has passed. Her lips were a slightly darker color than the rest of her face, not purple, not blue, but a deepening.

He looked at the gravel. The weeds. The wall.

And then he saw the token.

It was lying in a patch of flattened grass about three feet from her hand. Brass, oval, with a number stamped into the face. He didn't need to read the number. He knew what it was. A conductor's token, the kind they'd issued from 1962 to 2003. The kind he'd carried in his breast pocket for twenty-six years, from the time the company gave him his first one to the morning of his last walk.

There were a hundred of them out there. In drawers. In attics. In the hands of collectors at yard sales in Davenport. It could mean nothing. It could mean anything.

He picked it up. It was warm. Warmer than it should have been in that gray morning air.

"Grady."

The voice came from behind him. Walt turned. Dale Ferrick, the Brenner's Hollow sheriff, was standing at the yard gate, his cap pulled low against the sun. He was a big man, slow on the feet, and he had a way of walking into a room like he owned the floorboards.

"Walt." Dale set his hat on the back of a post. "What are you doing in there?"

"Checking the gate latch," Walt said.

"The gate latch is latched. I can see it from here." Dale walked closer, hands in his coat pockets. He stopped at the edge of the weeds. He looked at the body. He did not flinch, but his jaw tightened, a small muscle working under the stubble.

"Who's that?" he said. Not a question. An assessment.

"Walt didn't say. He stood. He wiped his hands on his trousers. "I was walking the loop. I passed the yard. I saw her."

Dale pulled out his phone. "I'm calling it in. You go back to your house, Grady. You don't touch nothing. You don't go closer."

"I already—"

"You don't go *closer*." The tone was not unkind, but it was the tone of a man closing a door. "You go home."

Walt looked at the body. The woman's coat. The brass token still in his hand, still warm, still holding the heat of whatever hand had dropped it. Or whatever hand had placed it.

"You don't know who this is," Walt said.

"That's for the state police. This is a closed property, no one's been in the yard in—" He stopped himself. He looked at Walt. "You don't know who it is either, or I would have told you when I got the call."

"I don't know who it is."

"No. You don't."

The sheriff made a phone call that took ninety seconds. The fog was gone. The sun was climbing the west slope and putting a hard edge on the railyard, turning the rust into a kind of fire.

Walt stood at the gate. His hand was closed around the token. He could feel the edge of the number stamped into the brass against his thumb. He could feel, or think he could feel, the particular weight of a thing that had been carried in a coat pocket for twenty-six years.

He put the token back in his coat pocket.

"Grady." Dale turned back to him. "You're coming with me to the station house. Fingerprint the token, get a description of the victim,—"

"She's wearing a ring," Walt said.

Dale blinked. "What?"

"Left hand. Under the nails, on the ring finger. A gold band. And there's a dent in it, a little dent, on the outside."

Dale looked at the body. He looked at Walt. Something shifted behind his eyes, a small recalculation. "Where did you see that?"

"I saw her."

Dale was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded once, slow, like a man turning a valve. "Okay. You come with me."

They walked out of the yard. The sun was fully up now, and the railyard behind them was a place of iron and shadow and the smell of old metal. Walt's knees ached. His back ached. His left shoulder had a spot of pain that he'd been ignoring since Tuesday, which meant it was probably Wednesday now.

In the car, Dale drove slow. The road to the town center was a single lane cut through the mountain, and the morning traffic was a thin thing: one pickup truck, a woman walking a dog, a mail van with its door open.

"You've walked this track for forty-two years," Dale said.

"Thirty-seven. I started in '87. I was twenty-nine."

"Close enough." Dale's eyes were on the road. "You ever see this woman before? She's not from around here. She's not *local*."

Walt looked out the window. The trees were moving past, the same trees he'd walked past every morning for three weeks. The creek, low and brown. The general store with its crooked sign. The life of Brenner's Hollow, small and steady as a track.

"Everyone in this town is local," he said. "That's the whole point of it."

Dale didn't answer.

They drove into town. The station house was a squat brick building with a porch that sagged on the east side. Two officers were already there, talking to a woman who stood by the car with her arms crossed, her face doing something complicated.

Walt's stomach went cold.

He knew that face. He knew the particular way the jaw set, the way the hands were folded and then unfolded and folded again. He knew that face from the platform at Davenport, from the lunch counter at station four, from the thousand small interactions of a life spent counting heads on a train.

The woman turned.

Margaret Ellison. The pharmacist. Forty-six years old. Widow of the postmaster, two daughters who went to college in Davenport. She had a way of tilting her head when she listened, as if the sound were coming from a specific angle and she needed to adjust for it.

She was looking at Walt.

Walt looked back.

"Mr. Grady," Dale said. "You don't know her?"

Walt did not say yes. He did not say no. He looked at the token in his coat pocket. He looked at Margaret Ellison's face, which was doing that thing, the complicated thing, the thing that said *I know where I am and I do not know why I am here and something is very wrong.*

The ring on her left hand. He could have seen it from here if the light hit right. But he'd already seen it. The gold band. The little dent on the outside.

He knew the dent.

He'd made the dent.

He'd made it in his kitchen, on a Tuesday, three weeks ago, while his hands shook and the fog was coming in off the river and Margot was in the next room washing a pan. He'd been holding the token. He'd been holding it for three weeks.

And now it was in his pocket, and the woman it had been near for three weeks was dead in the railyard, and the dent was still there, and the sun was coming up over a town that had never once stopped running on its schedule.

"Take me to her," Walt said.
