# Chapter 1: The Slow Earth

The city woke the way all cities woke—late, grumbling, with the smell of burning wood in its nostrils. But the waking here was never just a waking. It was a *heave*, a great subterter tremor that rolled up through the cobblestones of Kethara, through the foundations of every merchant's stall and fishmonger's crate and cobbled lane, and out through the soles of ten thousand bare feet. The elders called it the Breath. The children called it the Stomper, because it always came with the sound of something enormous shifting its weight.

Mara Voss felt the Stomper first, the way she always did, three heartbeats before the sound reached her window. She was lying on her back on a mat of dried reeds in her mother's back room, one hand tucked beneath her ear, the other tracing the spiral of a crack in the ceiling that ran from the rafters to the floor like a river on a map of a place no one had ever been.

"Grandmother says the shell's tired today," she murmured to no one.

The crack in the ceiling widened by a grain. A fine dust of limestone sifted down through her hair.

Below, in the long cobbled avenue that ran the length of what the scholars called the Palate—the broad, gently sloping expanse of the turtle's upper shell that formed the city's central plateau—vendors were already pulling their wares onto the streets. Fish from the Salt Pans, where the thermal vents beneath the shell's underside sent up columns of green-tinged steam that the city's alchemists harvested for ink and medicine. Dried kelp from the Cliffs, where the shell's edges dropped away two hundred feet to the grass sea far below. Bread from the ovens in the Underbelly, the cavernous spaces between the shell's ridges where the heat never quite died and the bakers worked in a perpetual warm gloom.

The city was old. No one could say how old. The Founders' tablets, carved into the keel-bone at the city's northern terminus, spoke of the First Walking and the Settlement, but the language on those stones had worn smooth to nothing but grooves, and the scholars' arguments over what the grooves had once meant went on for generations, ending always in a shrug. What was certain was that Kethara had been on the turtle's back long enough for the shell's ridges to become street names, for the slow grooves where the shell's scutes met to become the city's most expensive real estate, and for the people of Kethara to forget that the ground beneath them was, in the strictest sense, alive.

Mara sat up. The dust had stopped. She padded to the window, which looked out over the eastern quarter, where the houses stepped up the shell's natural curve toward the Rim—the outermost edge where the city's buildings thinned into watchtowers and the sky opened up, vast and unmediated, and the grass sea stretched to every horizon and back.

Today the horizon was doing something it rarely did.

Something was moving out there. Not a ship—too low, too slow. Not a herd of the great bison that ranged the plains between the shell's tracks. It was a shape, a dark smudge against the green, and as the morning light sharpened, Mara could see it was a herd of bison, but *wrong*. They were walking in a line, single file, stretching from the visible distance all the way to where the sea of grass met the sky. And they were walking *up*, toward the base of the Cliffs, toward Kethara.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass.

The bison were walking up the shell.

That was not possible. The Cliffs were two hundred feet of smooth, sloping calcium, and the bison could not—

A crash. Not far off. Then another. And then the ground beneath her feet gave a lurch so sudden she stumbled back from the window and caught her shoulder on the doorframe.

Down in the avenue, the Stomper had come again, but this one was different. It wasn't the slow heave of the turtle shifting its weight. It was a *knock*. A series of them, rising, climbing the shell's slope in a rhythm that had nothing to do with any gait Mara had ever learned to read.

Her mother's hand was on her shoulder. Old. Trembling in a way it hadn't in years.

"Go to your mother's door," her mother said, and her voice was the voice she used when the shell's thermal vents flared too hot and the heat-shimmer made the streets look like they were dissolving. "Go now."

Mara looked at her mother. Looked back through the window.

The line of bison was closer now. At the base of the Cliffs, where the grass met the shell's first low ridge, something was happening that made her mother's hand tighten on her shoulder. The bison were not walking up. They were *gathering*, pressing against the shell's curve in a dark, shifting mass, and the ground was answering them, the whole great body beneath Kethara giving a sound that was not the Breath and not the Stomper but something deeper, something that rose from somewhere far below the shell and far below the grass sea and made the windows in the eastern quarter rattle in their frames.

The turtle was answering the bison.

And for the first time in as long as anyone in Kethara could remember, the city was not the only thing that was moving.
