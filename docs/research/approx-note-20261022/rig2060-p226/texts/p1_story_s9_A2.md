# The Correspondence at Point Solace

The lighthouse at Point Solace had stood for one hundred and nineteen years before Elias Thorn first saw the letter on the step.

He was seventy-four that autumn, and he had kept the light since he was thirty-one, which meant the lamp had outlasted his mother, his first marriage, and the particular gray of the November sea that made him feel the world had been scrubbed clean of color. The light turned its slow, faithful beam across the black water. Below, the sea threw itself at the rocks in its eternal complaint. Above, the sky was nothing but a ceiling.

He found the letter in the brass slot at the base of the door, where the postman dropped things. Except there had been no postman since 1983. There had been no post at all since the village of Carrow had thinned to four souls and a church with the door locked. The letter was cream-colored, thick as a banknote, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. There was no return address. There was no postmark. There was only a single word in ink the color of the sea: *Maren.*

He did not open it for three days.

On the fourth day, while the fog rolled in so thick he could no longer see the lamp he'd lit himself, he broke the seal. The handwriting was small and slanted, the letters hunched together as if the writer were trying to fit an ocean into a sentence.

*Dear Mr. Thorn,*

*You must be wondering how I know your name. I have a photograph of you in the lower gallery of the museum in what is now called the Northern Reclamation. The curator told me the photograph was from 1998, though the image shows you with a beard three times your current width and a coat the color of the sea in February. She said you looked, in that picture, like a man who had been arguing with the weather his entire life.*

*I don't know how to explain this. I have tried. My mother tried, before me. But I think the only thing that is true is that I am writing to you from a Tuesday in the year 2149, and the sea here is warmer than the one you see, and I have come to the Reclamation because I wanted to stand in the room where they kept your logbooks, and I wanted to know if you ever, in all the years of watching the horizon, felt the particular loneliness of being a lighthouse. Not the machine. The keeper.*

*The sea is the same, though. That I can tell. The rocks are the same. And I think, standing in the museum with the glass case in front of me, that I was not reading your logbooks. I was reading a letter that you wrote to no one and that somehow, after all these years, reached me.*

*I am Maren. I am forty-one. I am a marine biologist by trade, though the word means something slightly different where I am. I live in a house with a window that faces the old lighthouse and I watch the light turn every night because my mother watched it before me, and her mother before that. We have a tradition. A strange one. A family that has been receiving letters from people who are not there yet, for longer than my grandmother will say.*

*But you are the first one I have answered. Do not write back if you cannot. The letters travel forward. I will simply sit with what you give me.*

*— Maren*

Elias read it six times. He then read it one more time in the dark, after the lamp had clicked through the fourth hour and the sea had quieted to a kind of breathing.

He wrote back that same night.

He wrote about the fog. He wrote about the particular way the light made a shadow on the east wall that looked like a hand reaching for something. He wrote that he had not spoken to a human voice in eleven days and that the gull on the headland had been rude. He wrote that his name was Elias, that he was old, that the letters were nonsense, but that he was grateful they had come.

He put the letter in the slot.

It arrived three days later. He could not have explained the mechanism. The cream paper, the red wax, the slanted handwriting. The next letter told him about a storm in 2153 that had reshaped the coastline and turned the headland into a series of islands. It told him that the lighthouse had been converted into a climate archive, and that the museum in the Northern Reclamation had been built in the year of the flood. It told him that she was married, and that her husband was a sound engineer who recorded the ocean so that future generations who might lose the coast entirely could still hear it.

It told him that her daughter was seven and had a gap in her front teeth and asked every morning whether the light was still on.

Elias wrote back every week. He wrote about the salt in his teeth and the way the winter wind made the tower hum a single, sustained note that sounded like a ship's bell. He told her about the night in 1991 when the generator failed for nine hours and he had kept the light by hand, feeding the wick, turning the lens with the crank, until his fingers bled and the dawn came gray and forgiving. He told her about the man from Carrow who used to bring him tobacco and argue about whether the tide was "rude" or "determined." He told her about the silence, which was not the same as quiet.

She wrote back about her work, about the way the new ocean measured differently than the old one, about the strange tenderness of her daughter's gap-toothed smile. She wrote about a dream she'd had of a tall man in a wool coat, standing at the top of a tower, looking at a horizon that stretched forever in one direction and stopped at a wall of fog in the other. *I woke up and I could not sleep,* she wrote. *I sat at the window and I watched the light turn and I thought, I know you, Elias. I have known you since the first letter. I don't understand it. I just know.*

He told her he understood that he would not live to see the year 2149. He told her that the distance between them was not a problem because distance was what he had spent forty-three years measuring, and he knew its shape well.

She wrote, *Then let it be the shape of a light. Turning. Not reaching, exactly. Just… arriving, over and over, at the same point.*

That was the line he kept. He did not put it in a book. He did not pin it to the wall. He simply carried it, the way he carried the lamp, the way he carried the salt, the way he carried the years.

The correspondence continued for eleven months. In the spring of his seventy-fifth year, the letters slowed. Not because Maren had stopped writing, but because the mechanism, whatever it was, grew thin. The cream paper became translucent. The red wax came back as a faint smear, the color of a bruise. She wrote shorter letters. One said only that she was in a hospital, that she had a tumor, that the sound engineer had not stopped recording the sea. Another said that her daughter had brought her to the window and that she was pointing at the light and saying, *Look, Papa, the old one is still turning.*

Elias wrote back a letter that was mostly the sea. The sound of it. The way it filled the whole world and asked nothing. He told her that the lighthouse would still be there, though not him, though perhaps not even the light in the form he knew it, and that this was not an ending but a continuation, the way a river is not less a river when it reaches the sea.

The last letter from Maren was two lines long. *Thank you for the light. I am going to sleep now. I will meet her on the other side. Tell your daughter I said hello.* He did not have a daughter. He wrote back: *Your mother would have liked you. I am sorry.*

The slot received no more letters.

He kept the light for three more years. He grew old in the way the sea grows old—not suddenly, but all at once, as if the tide had decided a new shape for the coast. He spoke to the gull on the headland. He spoke to the wind. He spoke, sometimes, to the east wall where the shadow of the lamp made a hand that was almost, almost reaching for something.

On the last night he kept the light, in February of the year the fog would not lift, he climbed the one hundred and nineteen steps to the lamp room. He lit it. He turned the lens. He watched the beam sweep out over the water and come back, and sweep out again, and come back, and he thought of a woman in a hospital bed with a gap-toothed daughter at the window, pointing.

*Still turning.*

In the morning, he put on his coat. He walked to the brass slot at the base of the door and placed a letter inside. It was not cream-colored. It was the gray of the November sea. He wrote on it, in the small, hunched handwriting he had learned from a stranger in a museum four hundred miles and a thousand years away:

*Dear Maren,*

*I am Elias. I am not there yet. But I have your letter. I have always had your letter. The light is still turning. The sea is the same. Tell your daughter I said hello.*

*— E.T.*

He walked back down the spiral stairs. He set the logbook on the table. He sat in the chair where he had sat for forty-three years, in the particular gray of the November sea, and he watched the lamp turn, and turn, and turn, until the fog lifted and the day came in, gray and forgiving, and the beam found the water and came back.
