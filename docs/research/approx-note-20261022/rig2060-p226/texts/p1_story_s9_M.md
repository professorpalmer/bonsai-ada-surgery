## final text

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday in late November, folded into the hollow of a weathered gull egg that had been carried up the cliff path by the wind. Arthur Finch, seventy-four years old and keeper of the Blackpoint Light for thirty-eight consecutive years, held the paper to his nose. It smelled of salt and something faintly sweet, like the foxglove he grew in the patch of garden behind the keeper's cottage.

The handwriting was small, slanted, pressed hard into the page. *Dear Mr. Finch,* it read. *I hope you don't mind the egg. I don't know where else to send it. I know you keep the light turning. I know you talk to the fog. I know you've had a dog named Wren for six years and that he sleeps under the stair because the wind finds him up on the landing. I know you've been writing back and forth with the Coast Authority about the replacement for the lamp mechanism, and that they'll never approve it because the budget committee meets in March and you've already asked twice.*

Arthur set the paper on the kitchen table and stood very still.

He did have a dog. The dog was named Wren. The dog was a terrier mix with one cloudy eye, and yes, he slept beneath the stairs. And yes, he had written three letters to the Coast Authority. Two had gone unanswered. The third was in a drawer, ink still slightly damp.

He turned the page.

*I don't know why I'm writing this. I know it makes no sense. I've written to you before, I think, though I can't prove it. I only know that the words were already there when I sat down to write, and they kept coming, and I couldn't stop. I'm Elara Voss. I am twenty-nine years old. I have not met you. I don't think you've met me. But I will. Or I have. I can't sort out the tense of my own life.*

Arthur made tea. He drank it without tasting it.

The second letter came nine days later. This one had no egg. It simply lay on the step, a plain envelope with no stamp and no address, only the words *BLACKPOINT LIGHT* in careful block capitals. Inside, Elara wrote about the storm in February, the one that took the outer railing on the gallery and sent a wave through the lens housing. *I remember the sound of the glass singing,* she wrote. *Not breaking. Singing. You held the shutter open for forty minutes because you said the light had to keep its word.*

He had held the shutter open. He remembered. He'd told no one.

The third letter came in January. The fourth in February. Each one more intimate, more specific. Elara described the way the fog horn sounded on a Tuesday night, which it did every Tuesday night because the wind shifted that direction and pressed the air through the horn's throat. She described the particular gray of the sea when a squall was two days out, a gray that was almost white. She described the creak of the gallery stairs, which had a different note on the third step, a flat and lonely note that no amount of oil could fix.

There was no postmark. No return address. No trace of paper that suggested a commercial printer. The ink was the same color every time — a blue that was almost violet in the low light of the cottage. And the handwriting grew more confident with each letter, as if Elara were not learning to write but rather learning to *be* someone.

Arthur began to leave a place set at the kitchen table. A mug. A small dish of shortbread. The letters did not eat the shortbread, but he could not stop.

In March, the Coast Authority approved his request. The new mechanism was installed on a cold Saturday. Arthur watched the old lamp's last rotation and felt something loosen in his chest that he had carried for so long he'd mistaken it for bone. That evening, a letter arrived with no envelope at all, just the page, damp at the edges.

*They're putting in the new lamp. I know. I've written to you about it and you've written back, or I will. I'm scared, Mr. Finch. Not of you. Of the time after. The time when you stop keeping the light and start keeping yourself. You've been keeping it for so long I don't know if you'll remember how to do the other thing. I'm writing to tell you that you will remember. That the garden will come back. That Wren will die in the summer and you will plant foxglove in his place because the foxglove is what you always say you'll plant and never do. I'm writing to tell you that it's all right to set the tea down and walk to the water and just stand there for an hour and not turn anything. I'm writing to tell you that I'm not a stranger. I'm the part of you that still believes the light was for the ships and not for the man who holds the shutter open.*

Arthur read the letter three times. He looked at Wren, who was asleep under the stairs, one ear twitching at the sound of the sea. He looked at the garden window where the foxglove stalks stood tall and purple and waiting. He set the letter down very carefully, as if it were a live thing.

The letters continued through spring and into June. Elara wrote about a woman she knew, a fisherwoman in the harbor town at the base of the cliff, a woman with a scar across her jaw from a rope burn and a laugh that could be heard across the water. *Her name is Margot,* Elara wrote. *She mends nets. She brings me coffee when I'm on the headland and I tell her about the light and she laughs and says it's a pretty light for a light. I don't know her. I'm writing to you so I can write to her. I'm writing to her through you. I don't know how that works. I don't know how any of this works. I only know the words come.*

In July, Margot appeared at the lighthouse door on a Thursday afternoon, carrying two mugs of coffee and a plate of fish cakes. She was in her fifties, sun-darkened, with a scar across her jaw from a rope burn and a laugh that could be heard across the water. She looked at Arthur and said, "She told me you'd be here."

"Who?" Arthur said.

"You know who."

He did not. He did not, and yet the words settled into him with the ease of a key into a lock he'd forgotten was there. He took the coffee. He gave her the fish cakes to the table and Wren to the floor. Margot stayed until the light turned gold over the water. She did not speak of Elara again, but she came back every Thursday after that, and she mended nets in the kitchen while Arthur swept, and the cottage filled with a sound that was not quite conversation but was close enough.

The last letter arrived in September. It was short.

*I'm not coming. I'm not going. I'm not a boat or a shore or a name you'll look up. I'm the space between the shutter and the glass, Mr. Finch. I'm the reason you held it open. I'm the part of you that writes at 2 a.m. in the little notebook you keep in the drawer. I'm the part that talks to the fog and means it. I'm writing this because the words stopped coming last week and I need you to know that stopping is also a kind of writing. The light will turn. Wren will die in August. You will plant the foxglove. Margot will stay. You will be old in the way that is not an ending but a season. And when it comes, you will look up and you will see me in the lens, in the turning, in the long slow arc over the black water. You will see me and you will not be afraid. That is all. That is the whole of it.*

There was no signature.

Arthur read the letter once. He folded it along its creases and placed it in the drawer with the other letters, all of them, all of them, all of them. He closed the drawer. He poured a cup of tea and set it on the table, though no one would drink it.

Outside, the fog was in. The fog horn was about to sound. Arthur walked to the gallery door, pushed it open, and stepped out into the gray. The wind found the third stair and made its flat note. He stood on the landing, small and old and alone, and he listened.

The horn sounded. The light turned.

And in the long slow arc of the beam over the black water, in the glass and the glass and the turning, there was something that was not a reflection and was not a thing and was not a person, but was a presence, a small bright pressure in the dark, like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck.

Arthur stood there for an hour. He did not turn anything. He did not speak to the fog, though it seemed to be listening.

When he went inside, Margot was there, mending a net at the kitchen table. She looked up.

"It's coming in," she said.

"Yes," he said.

She nodded, as if that were the most natural thing in the world. She cut a slice of shortbread and set it on the plate in front of him. He ate it. It tasted, for the first time, like tea.

Wren died in August, under the stairs, with the foxglove stems reaching through the window above him. Arthur planted them where the dog had slept, in the cold patch of earth where the wind could not reach. They bloomed in September, all tall and purple and stubborn.

And the light turned. And turned. And turned.

In the morning, when the fog lifted and the sea went silver, Arthur would stand on the gallery and look out at the headland where a young woman sometimes walked, with a scarf pulled up and her hands in her pockets. He did not know her. She did not know him. She walked the length of the cliff and back, and every week she got a little slower, a little more herself.

He never wrote to her. She never wrote to him.

But the words were always there, in the small notebook in the drawer, in the way the light caught the water at three in the afternoon and made it look, briefly, like ink.
