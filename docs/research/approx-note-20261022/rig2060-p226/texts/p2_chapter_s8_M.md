# CHAPTER ONE: The Schedule

The 6:17 never came again.

That was the first thing Edmund "Ed" Calloway told people when he came to the post office to return his mail subscription. *The 6:17 never came again.* As if the train could have been expected to continue arriving into a town of four hundred and twelve souls for another forty years. As if the rails, laid in 1923 by men who believed the mountain would yield its ore to anyone patient enough, weren't already rusting into the earth like a skeleton in a meadow.

The postmistress, Darlene, had said, "Well, Ed, I'm sure it's a new beginning."

A new beginning. Ed had nodded. He had nodded the way one nods at weather. Not because he agreed, but because the alternative was to explain, in his careful, flat voice, that the beginning of something does not require a beginning at all. Things simply stop. The schedule ends. You put your cap in the drawer. You walk home through the hemlock and the wet stone and the grey November morning, and the world does not rearrange itself around your absence.

But the world had, in fact, rearranged itself. And this morning it had rearranged itself again.

Ed stood in the doorway of the old Ashford Station, which had been a hardware store for six years and a community center for three of those, and watched the road. His boots were muddy. The gravel drive was slick with dew. Somewhere past the ridge, the fog was lifting off the river like a hand pulling a sheet from a table.

He was seventy-one years old. His knees made a small, specific sound when he bent, a dry crack, like a match struck in a dark room. He had retired from the Ashford Line eleven years ago, the same winter the last ore train ran through town and the last crew was sent north to the new depot. He had not, after the retirement, taken up fishing. He had not started a garden. He had not, as his daughter in Burlington had gently suggested, "found some new purpose." He had continued, simply, to live. To walk. To watch the road. To keep the small, private timetable of his days as exact as he once kept the timetable of the trains.

The 6:17 never came again.

But the 7:03 did.

It was not a train. It was a black sedan, low to the ground, with no plates he could read in the fog. It came up the hill from the south road, the way the old freighters used to come when they were coming in heavy and the whole town would feel the vibration in the floorboards. It pulled up in front of the station. The door opened. A man got out.

Ed recognized the man's walk before he recognized his face. The slight drag of the left heel. The way the shoulders carried the weight of something invisible. The way the head turned, first to the right, then to the left, scanning. Ed had seen that walk a thousand times. Not on the tracks. On the platform. The man who was looking for something he couldn't name but was certain of.

The man stood by the sedan. He did not get out. He looked toward the station doorway. Toward Ed.

Ed did not look back. He knew, with the particular certainty that belonged to a man who had spent forty years reading a room the way he read a board of departures, that the man in the sedan was waiting to see if he was seen. And if he was seen, the transaction would not happen. The man in the sedan was a passenger who had arrived at the wrong stop and needed, before the next one, to be made to believe he was in the right place.

The man got back in the car. The engine turned over. The sedan pulled back down the hill and was gone. The fog swallowed the taillights. The 7:03 was done.

Ed stood in the doorway for another minute. Then he went inside.

The station was cold in the way old buildings get cold, not a sudden bite but a slow seeping, the kind of cold that lives in the walls and the beams and the plaster. The community center meetings had moved to the school gym after last winter. The old office on the far end of the room still held the ledger of the rail company's final year. Ed had asked to keep it. No one had said no.

He made his coffee in the small kitchen at the back. He read the Ashford Gazette, which had been a weekly for sixty years and a monthly since the second printer's back gave out. Today the Gazette was four pages and two of them were the school football game and one of them was a notice that the town council would be discussing the drainage of the lower meadow in April. The fourth page was a brief, factual notice:

**DEATH OF LOCAL RESIDENT**

The body of Victor Soren, 58, was discovered this morning by a hiker on the old rail corridor, approximately a quarter mile south of the Ashford Station. Soren, who had operated the Soren Hardware Supply on Main Street since 1996, is believed to have suffered a fall. The Ashford Police Department is treating the matter as an accidental death.

Ed read it twice. Then he set the paper down and went to the window.

The old rail corridor. The spur that ran behind the station, south, through the hemlock stand, where the ties had long since rotted and the rails were nothing more than two dark lines in the dirt, swallowed by grass and bramble. No one walked out that way anymore. The trail that followed the old track was used, in summer, by hikers who were looking for the river. In November, the light came on at four and the trail was a dark, narrow throat of mud and fallen timber.

A hiker. In November. On the old rail corridor.

Ed knew the old rail corridor. He knew it the way a man knows the back of his own hand. He knew where the ground dropped and where it rose. He knew where the creek crossed on the east side and where the old switch stand stood, half-buried in leaf-litter, its two iron levers rusted into a single, unmovable shape. He knew the exact distance from the station to the place where the line had been cut in '83, the place where the last crew had welded the final joint and the foreman had stood back, hands on his hips, and said, *That's the last one, boys.*

A fall. On the old rail corridor. In November.

Ed thought about the walk of the man in the sedan. The left heel. The shoulders. The scanning.

He thought about Victor Soren. He knew Victor. They had nodded at each other for eleven years. Victor had been the only person left in town who had ever set foot on the Ashford Line as a worker. He'd been a brakeman on the ore trains. Young. Angry. The kind of young a man can be when the work is hard and the pay is bad and the mountain is always closing in. He had told Ed, once, over a beer in the station kitchen, that the line had been the best and worst thing of his life, and that he still dreamed of the sound of the wheels on the iron, and he was sorry.

*You don't get to be sorry for something that's gone,* Ed had said. *You get to be over it.*

Victor had smiled at him then. He had not been over it.

The phone rang.

Ed let it ring. He knew who it would be. He knew, with the same certainty he had used to read the distance between trains on a dark track, that it would be the Ashford Police, and that they would be calling because something was wrong with a story they had filed too quickly.

He picked up on the fourth ring.

"Mr. Calloway? This is Officer Briggs. I'm calling about the death of Victor Soren."

"I saw it in the paper," Ed said.

"Right. Well. The thing is. I was going to drop by the station after the council meeting. I wanted to talk to you." A pause. "Victor was a friend. You know that. He'd mention you in his stories. The old line. He said you were the conductor who'd come through the last time the 6:17 ran in '12. He said you never once checked your watch."

Ed did not correct him. The 6:17 had been his last run, yes, but in '12 he had been a station agent. He had not checked his watch. He had not needed to. He had been looking at the door.

"Mr. Calloway? I'm sorry to bother you."

"I'm not a bother."

"The coroner's report hasn't come in yet. Briggs is... I mean, I'm just saying. There are a few things that don't add up. The hiker found him near the switch stand. He's lying on his side, and his hands are—well. They're not in a natural position."

"Natural how?"

A pause. Long enough that Ed heard Briggs breathe.

"They're raised. Both of them. Above his head. Like he was... holding up the sky. Or making a shape. The coroner said it was a fall. But the position. It's not the position of a man who fell."

Ed set the phone down on the counter. He looked at the ledger on the far shelf. The final year. The numbers in neat, small columns. The last entry: *November 14. 6:17. Ashford to Kettle. All clear.*

He looked at the window. The fog was almost gone now. The mountains stood out of it like teeth.

He picked up his hat. He went to the closet where he kept his coat, the one with the brass buttons, the one he'd worn the day the line closed and the day after and the day after that. He pulled it on.

"Officer Briggs," he said, picking the phone back up. "What was the exact time the hiker found him?"

"About six-fifteen. Give or take ten minutes."

"And where exactly? I mean, relative to the track. The old track."

"A little ways south. Past the creek crossing. Near the switch."

"Which switch?"

Briggs hesitated.

There were two switches on the old spur. One at the station, where the branch to the ore field had once split off. One further south, where the line had been cut in '83. The first was in front of anyone's house. The second was alone in the hemlock.

"The one in the woods," Briggs said. "The one nobody goes to anymore."

Ed hung up. He stood in the kitchen for a long moment. The coffee was going cold. The clock on the wall, the one he had wound every day for thirty years, was ticking its small, metronomic tick.

The 6:17 never came again.

But someone had left a ticket.

He went outside. The morning air hit him like the inside of a freezer, the kind of cold that made your teeth ache and your lungs burn. He walked down the gravel drive, across the station yard where the old platform was now just a slab of concrete jutting out of the grass like a broken plate, and he went onto the rail corridor.

The trail was a narrow path, barely a trail, more a suggestion. The hemlock closed on both sides, thick and black, the needles on the ground a deep, soft carpet. The rails were barely visible now, two dark lines in the dirt, half-swallowed by bramble. Ed walked them the way he had walked them ten thousand times. He counted the steps. He knew the distance to the creek crossing. He knew the exact point where the ground dropped and the old switch stood.

It stood as he had remembered. Two iron levers, rusted to a single shape. The base of the switch housing was in the mud.

And there, a few paces south, on his side was Victor Soren.

He was lying on his side. The position Briggs had described. Both arms raised above his head, the fingers spread. The face was turned toward the ground. The coat was the grey one, the one with the dark thread showing at the cuffs. The left heel, the one that dragged, was at a strange angle.

Ed stood over the body for a long time. He did not touch it. He did not call for anyone. He simply looked.

The raised hands. The spread fingers. The angle of the body. The way the left heel was turned out, as if the man had been looking to the right at the moment he died.

It was a signal.

Not a random shape. Not the posture of a man who fell and broke. It was a shape with intent. A shape with a grammar. A shape that Ed had spent forty years learning the language of.

It was the signal for *stop*.

Both hands raised. Fingers spread. The classic, the oldest, the one that meant: *The track ahead is clear of the engine but not clear of the man. The man is here. You do not go. You do not go.*

Ed looked south. Down the old rail line. The direction the 6:17 used to go. The direction the ore trains had gone for forty years.

No one walked that trail. No one hiked that trail. No one had any reason to be at the switch in November, in the dark, at six in the morning.

But someone was there. Someone had been there. And whoever it was had put a man in the shape of a signal and left the body for a hiker to find.

A ticket left on the seat. A boarding passed to the wrong person.

Ed Calloway stood in the hemlock, in the grey November light, and made his note. He would be seen. He would be late. He would walk back to the station and the phone would be ringing by the time he got there. He would give them what they needed. The shape. The signal. The grammar.

And then he would do what a conductor did when the platform was empty and the board showed nothing and the train was not due for another hour.

He would wait.

He would watch the track.

And he would not miss the next one.
