# Chapter One: The Trestle

The fog came up out of the valley the way it always did in October — slow and gray, swallowing the pines one row at a time until the whole mountain seemed to dissolve into a watercolor no one had bothered to finish. Miles Harrow watched it from the window of his kitchen, a mug of black coffee growing cold in his hands, and thought about how the world could be this small.

Three rows of pines. The ridge above the ridge. The road that looped in and out of town like a tongue tasting the air. And the rail line, gone silent for three years, cutting through the middle of it all like a scar someone had never bothered to stitch up.

Miles set the mug down. Seventy-one years old. His hands ached in the damp the way a good clock's pendulum ached — a steady, familiar complaint. He'd been a conductor on the Cascade Mountain Line for forty-one years, had counted the same tunnel, the same trestle, the same stubborn stretch of switchback near Bear Hollow until the tracks were worn shallow in the center from the wheels. The line shut down in February. No announcement, no ceremony. Just a memo, a pile of boxes, and a final whistle that sounded more like a sigh.

He'd never left town. There was nothing to go to. The train had been the whole shape of his life, the only road he'd ever truly walked, and now the road was gone and he was just a man in a house with too many quiet rooms.

The phone on the hook by the door rang at 10:14.

He let it ring four times. Five. The sixth, he picked it up.

"Voss."

"Mr. Harrow. Sheriff Dane. You're still around, I trust?"

"Mr. Harrow," he repeated, because a man's name is a thing you hold onto, and the way he'd said it — flat, tired, the way you say a word you'd been dreading all night — made Miles set his jaw.

"Found a body. On the old line, past the trestle."

The fog pressed its palms against the glass. Miles felt it in his chest before he felt it in his hands, a low throb, like a rail warming under a summer sun.

"Who?"

"That's the trouble, Mr. Harrow. That's the trouble."

A pause. Miles could hear the wind outside, the way it found the gaps in his eaves and whistled through them.

"She's a young woman. Twenty-nine, maybe thirty. No phone, no bag, no shoes. She was just... lying on the ballast. Face up. Her eyes were open."

"Did she have a name?"

"We got a name, eventually. Claire Delacroix. She came to town about three months ago, said she was doing research on the old rail history. Was renovating the station building into a little museum. You'd know her, probably."

Miles knew her. He knew her because she'd come to his porch every Thursday for the past ten weeks, sitting in the crooked chair by the woodstove, asking him questions about the schedule of 1962, about the flood that had washed out the approach bridge at Kettle Creek, about why the conductor's caboose on the old line was painted a color that wasn't on any official palette. He'd answered, mostly out of politeness, but she'd listened like the words were something precious, and his answers had grown a little longer each week, a little less guarded.

She'd called him "Miles" by the fifth visit, and something in his chest had unclenched by the seventh.

"I know her," he said.

"Come out, Mr. Harrow. Bring your coat. The sheriff's got the scene taped off, but he needs someone who knows the grade."

"I'm seventy-one."

"You're seventy-one and you've walked that line more times than I've been alive. We don't have anyone else who knows where the switchbacks start and where the embankment gives out. Please."

He hung up. Stood in the kitchen for a long moment, watching the fog eat the yard. Then he went to the closet and pulled out the green flannel coat with the brass buttons, the one he'd worn on every winter call for twenty years. He'd never washed it. It smelled of cold air and diesel and the particular dust that only settles in old rail yards.

The town of Pine Hollow was small enough that a body on the rail line meant a body for everyone. He could feel it in the way people in the hardware store looked at each other across the counter, in the way the bakery woman had already wiped her counter three times since the 9 AM call. He drove his truck up the grade slowly, the wipers chopping through a drizzle that felt more like the mountain exhaling.

Sheriff Dane met him at the edge of the tape. He was a big man, red-faced from the cold, with the kind of heavy silence in his shoulders that suggested he hadn't slept since last night.

"Mr. Harrow."

"Mr. Harrow," Miles repeated again. It was becoming a ritual.

They walked the last stretch together. The old rail line rose out of the fog in a gray ribbon, the ties pale as bones, the rails catching the weak October light in thin bright lines. Miles's boots found the grade the way they always had — a left lean at the curve, a right step-up where the ballast shifted. His body remembered the geometry of the line even after the trains had stopped.

They passed the trestle at a slow walk. It was a wooden thing, long and low, spanning a ravine where a creek ran so fast in spring it looked like hammered silver. The boards were green with moss. Miles had walked across this trestle ten thousand times. He could tell from the angle of his shoulders that the section of it where the crossbeam had splintered in 1987 was still there, still dangerous, still a thing you leaned your weight toward the center to avoid.

They went past the trestle and up the short climb toward the old passing siding where the freight cars had once waited.

Claire Delacroix was lying face-up in the center of the rails, about two hundred yards from the trestle.

Miles stopped at the edge of the tape and looked.

The fog was thinner here, but it still hung low in the ravine below, and the light coming through it gave everything the look of a photograph left too long in a darkroom. Claire's coat was a dark green wool, too warm for the October air. Her hair, black and wet, spread out in a dark halo around her face. Her eyes were open, and they were the pale gray-green of the mountain lake below town, and they were looking at nothing, and yet they were looking at *something*, and Miles found, to his surprise, that it was like looking at a train window at night when the passenger inside has fallen asleep and the landscape is passing by in streaks of gold and black and you can't quite tell if it's moving or you are.

Her hands were in her lap. One hand held nothing. The other held a small, folded piece of paper.

Sheriff Dane knelt at her side. "No sign of struggle. No blood. Well, not much. A little at the mouth. She'd been dead a while, maybe six hours. Maybe eight."

"Six hours ago, the fog was already up." Miles didn't look at the sheriff. He was looking at the paper in her hand.

Dane nodded. "Yeah."

They crouched at the edge of the tape, and the paper came up between them on a pair of tweezers. Miles unfolded it with his fingers.

It was a conductor's timetable. Old. The print was faded, the ink a rusty brown. And in the margin, in handwriting he knew as well as his own, someone had written: *The 11:42 didn't stop. It never stops.*

Miles's hand went still. He'd written that. He'd written it forty years ago, in the margin of the same timetable, because the 11:42 eastbound had skipped the stop at Kettle Creek for the last time on a Tuesday in November, and a woman with a stroller had stood in the snow for four minutes before the engineer realized, and she'd called it a miracle she'd made it down the hill to the next town. He'd kept the timetable in a drawer in the depot office until the office was gone, until it was in his coat pocket, until it was in his kitchen drawer where he found it that morning, tucked between a letter from his daughter and a bill he couldn't remember getting.

He looked up at the paper in his hand and felt the fog pressing against him, or maybe he was pressing against it.

"Who was the last person to see her?" Dane asked.

Miles didn't answer. Not yet. He looked at Claire's face again, at the open eyes, and he thought: *The 11:42 didn't stop.* And he thought: *It never stops.* And the two sentences went together the way rail joints went together, with a small, clean click, and something behind his ribs turned over.

He put the timetable in his coat pocket.

"Who found her?" he asked.

"The ranger. He was walking his dog up the service road. Found the coat first. Green wool, by the trestle."

"Did she have her phone on her?"

"She didn't have a phone. The museum folks said she'd left it at the station. Said she was walking the line on Thursday afternoons. You were one of the ones she talked to, I think."

"I was."

"Did she ever seem... worried?"

Miles considered this. He thought of the fourth Thursday, when she'd asked him about the color of the caboose. He'd told her it was a mistake. A painter in 1953 had mixed the blue too dark and no one had cared. She'd looked at him for a long time and said, *Like someone's trying to hide something behind the pretty part.* And he'd laughed, and she'd smiled, and the fog had been coming up that morning, too, and the whole world had smelled like wet stone and cold iron.

"She was thorough," he said. "But not worried."

"Maybe not. Or maybe worried in a quiet way."

Miles looked at Claire. At the green wool coat. At the wet black hair spread across the rails like ink dropped on a page. He thought about the timetable in his pocket, about the words in the margin, about the fact that he'd found it in his kitchen drawer this morning and not remembered putting it there.

He didn't ask the sheriff if that was a problem. He asked him instead: "Did she take the 11:42?"

Dane blinked. "The what?"

"The eastbound. The 11:42. Last Tuesday."

The fog thinned. The sun, barely there, broke through a gap in the trees and lit the rails in a thin gold line that ran, in Miles's mind, from Claire's outstretched hands all the way down the line and past the trestle and into the ravine, where the creek was already moving, already forgetting.

"The 11:42," Miles said. "When it ran. It stopped at every station for three minutes and forty seconds. Kettle Creek was the only one it ever skipped."

He looked at the sheriff. The sheriff looked at him.

"Nowhere," Miles said quietly. "It never skipped Kettle Creek again."

Above them, the fog began to lift.

Miles Harrow stood on the old rail line, in the cold, with a dead woman's timetable in his pocket, and felt, for the first time in three years, that the train was running.
