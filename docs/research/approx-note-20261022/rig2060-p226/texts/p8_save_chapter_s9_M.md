# Chapter One: The Tremor

The first thing a child learns in Vaelthyr is that the ground will move.

Not shake. Not quake. *Move.* Slowly, impossibly, the way a mountain might shift if you held your breath long enough to watch it. The cobblestones of the Lower Market ground against each other with a sound like teeth grinding, and the old women selling smoked eels pull their carts sideways with practiced ease, muttering apologies to the sky. Below the city's outer rim, the legs—*the* legs, the great pillar-legs that the elders called Pillars One through Four—press against the riverbed of the Thornwaste, and the whole city tilts, just a fraction, toward the east.

Kael pressed his back against the warm, ridged wall of the Shellhouse and watched the morning light crawl across the scute patterns overhead. The shell of Vaelthyr's guardian—Vaelthyr the Great, though everyone in the city simply called him *Old One*—curved above them in a dome of bone-white and amber, each scute the size of a merchant's barn. Between the great plates ran the Gullyways, where the wind howled and the salt air from the open ocean laced the breath of everyone who lived in the city's outer ring.

He pressed his fingertips to the wall and felt the warmth. That was the first thing the old women never told you about living on a living thing: it *breathed*. Not in a way you'd expect. No great tidal rise and fall of the whole city. Just a deep, subsonic pulse that traveled through the shell and into the floorboards, into the soles of your feet, into the marrow. A heartbeat so slow you could only feel it if you stopped moving and pressed your ear to the wall and waited four minutes. Four minutes, and there it was: *thrum-thrum-thrum.* The Old One's heart, four hundred and thirty-two years old, beating under a city of twelve thousand souls.

Kael was sixteen, and he was late.

He was late for the Tethering.

He knew the shape of it from the window: the long rope of men and women strung across the city's eastern edge, the Great Tether that connected Vaelthyr's foreleg to the anchor-stones of the Thornwaste. Without it, the Old One would simply keep walking. East. And east. And the city would march into the Khorran Expanse, a flat and merciless sea of white salt where no water grew and no fire could be lit. The Tether wasn't meant to stop the Old One. The elders said the Old One didn't *want* to stop. The Tether was a request, made in the old language that predated any of Vaelthyr's current tongues, a plea whispered into the shell each dawn and each dusk: *Wait. Stay. Let the children sleep.*

The Old One always waited. That was the deal.

The question was what the Old One would do when it stopped waiting.

Kael yanked his leather satchel over his shoulder and stepped out into the Gullyway. The morning wind was sharp and tasted of brine and something else—something copper and old. He'd noticed it for three days now, a faint metallic tang that the wind carried up from the Pillars' feet. The other children called it "turtle-rot." The Tetherers called it nothing, which was worse.

He ran. The cobblestones tilted under his boots, and he corrected his balance without thinking, the way you learn to walk on a ship after the first week at sea. His satchel thudded against his hip. Ahead, the Gullyway opened into the Tethering Yard, where the morning crew was already in place: thirty men and women in the gray oilskins of the Anchor Guild, their ropes coiled like sleeping snakes, their hooks forged from the black iron they mined from the Old One's shed scutes.

Tess was there. Tess, with her braid of red hair and her voice like a hammer on a nail. She looked up as he came, and her smile was the kind that meant *you're late and I'm not mad, but you'd better have a good excuse.*

"You feel it?" she asked, not a question, her eyes on the eastern rim of the shell where the light hit the scutes at a low angle and made the whole surface glow like a struck match.

Kael felt it. He'd felt it since he woke up. That wrongness. That the Old One's pulse had been *off* for three days, a half-beat early each cycle, as if the heart were skipping, or rushing, or trying to break a rhythm it had kept for four centuries.

"The Gullyway wind's carrying salt from the Khorran," he said, because that's what the answer was supposed to be.

Tess studied him for a moment. Then she unslung her hook over her shoulder and nodded, once.

"Then come on," she said. "The Tether goes at the first bell."

Above them, the Old One's eye opened.

It was the size of a carriage and the color of deep amber, and it did not look at the city. It did not look at the Tether, or the Pillars, or the Gullyways where twelve thousand people went about their morning. It looked at the sky. Somewhere beyond the cloud layer, somewhere so far east that the Tether's ropes might not have reached, something was out there that the Old One had not seen in three hundred and twelve years.

The Old One blinked.

The whole city tilted.

And Kael, standing in the Gullyway with Tess's hammer-voice fading behind him and the Tether crew reaching for their ropes, felt the heartbeat change.

Not a skip. Not a rush.

A stop.

Then, slow and vast and absolute, a second beat.

*Thrum.*

The cobblestones shuddered. The eel vendors' carts toppled. And from somewhere deep below the Pillars, below the riverbed, below the salt and the stone and the dark, something answered back.
