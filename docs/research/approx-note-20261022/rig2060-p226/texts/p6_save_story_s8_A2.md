## final text

# The Last Meal

The galley of the *Meridian* was never quiet. It was a cathedral of heat and noise, a cathedral built from recycled air and the collective appetite of forty-three thousand souls, and Sera Voss had spent every day of her forty-one years inside its walls.

She stood at the long steel counter, her forearms bare above the elbow where the burn scars of the third generation's spice riots showed pale and puckered. Around her, the evening shift hummed. The hydroponic bay's automated lift dropped its daily shipment of leafy greens into the receiving bin with a soft mechanical thud. The fermentation tanks in the corner burped their slow, sour breath, each one labeled in the script of a different homeland: Sumerian cuneiform, Devanagari, Hangul, a script that had no name and that the archivists called merely *Language Seven*.

Forty-one years. She was a cook for the *Meridian* the way her mother had been a cook, the way her mother's mother had been a cook, the way their namesake, the first Sera Voss, had been a cook the day the ship's engines first caught in the Lagrange point of Jupiter and humanity looked back at Earth for what many would be the last time.

"Batch fourteen's ready," said Dael, her sous, tapping the lid of a massive ceramic pot. "The soy-fermentation cycle hit its mark."

"Good. Taste it."

He did. His face did the small, private thing it did when the flavor was right: a fractional nod, a softening of the eyes. "It's right."

"It's always right," she told him, because it was not always right, and because the small ritual of tasting, of correction, of the *no, the garlic needs another minute*—that was the thread she pulled to keep from unraveling.

The *Meridian* was seven hundred meters long and had been moving for one hundred and nine years. She had been aboard for all of them. The ship's artificial gravity held at 0.94, just enough to make the floors feel like floors and not like a suggestion. The windows—there were windows, she had insisted on windows when she was a junior line cook and the design team was still arguing about whether the outer hull could accommodate them—showed only stars that no one had ever seen before. Not the constellations of the old Earth. Not Orion. Not the Pleiades. She could not name a single one. She had never needed to.

But she could name every recipe.

She thought about this as she set out the day's menu. Breakfast for the lower decks: grain porridge with the cultured dairy that the protein synthesizers made, sweetened with the syrup they called *amber*, which was really a crystallized blend of three different fruit cultivars and a mineral seasoning that the chemists argued about every quarter. Midday: the rotating cultural lunch. Today it would be a version of the dish they called *haru*, a rice-and-miso broth that originated in a region of Earth that had been flooded two centuries before the *Meridian* launched. Dinner: the large communal meal. The one she had been planning for three weeks.

The final communal meal.

Because in nine days, the *Meridian* would dock.

She knew this the way she knew the position of every burner, every valve, every drip of oil in the fryer. Nine days. The *Meridian* had been traveling for one hundred and nine years, and it would travel for eleven more before the docking clamps engaged with the habitat ring of Veritas, the station orbiting the third planet of a system that had no proper name yet and whose sun shone the color of weak honey.

Nine days. After that, the galley would stop. Not forever, perhaps. There would be kitchens on Veritas, on the colonists' land, in the long open fields where the soil was real and the air tasted of something she could not yet imagine. But *this* galley, with its burn scars and its labeled tanks and its forty-one years of accumulated smell, would stop.

She thought about what it would be like to cook somewhere that was not a ship.

She thought about her grandmother's hands. Not her own. Her grandmother, the first Sera Voss, had stood in a galley that smelled of burning and of the first real tomatoes the ship had grown in its own soil, and she had cooked the meal that had fed the founding families the night the engines were lit. Her grandmother had been the cook because the cook was the person who remembered. Who remembered that the *haru* had a particular ratio of dashi to water. Who remembered that the children on the upper decks liked their porridge with a little extra sweetness, the way the children of the old world had liked their porridge. Who remembered, because someone had to, because the ship would otherwise become a machine, and a machine did not feed people; it only kept them alive.

Sera tasted the dashi from Dael's pot. She closed her eyes. The flavor was a sentence in a language she had spoken since birth. *This is what we are. This is what we carried. This is not the ship. This is the people in the ship, which is not the same thing as the ship.*

She wrote it into the menu.

By the fourth day of the nine, the announcement had gone out. Every screen in every deck showed the date. Every person in the *Meridian* was preparing in the way they could: some packed bags for the landing shuttles, some wrote letters to the archivists, some stood at their windows and stared at the new stars as though they might finally learn their names.

Sera did none of these things. She cooked.

On the seventh day, the captain asked to come by.

It was not unusual for officers to visit the galley. It was, she thought, rather unusual for the captain of a ship to sit at the cook's counter and not speak for a while, watching the steam rise from the soup pot with the expression of a child.

"Are you ready for this?" she asked. Not the captain. The pot. The soup. The whole improbable, grinding, magnificent thing of making food inside a metal tube between two stars.

The captain—Maren, she was called, a woman in her fifties who had been born the night the *Meridian* turned its nose toward Veritas—looked at her for a long moment.

"I don't know what I'm going to do," the captain said, and her voice was very small. "I've been the captain of the *Meridian* for nineteen years. I've done the job. I don't know what it means to not be doing the job. To be just... a person on a planet."

Sera stirred the soup. The broth was clear, the rice had just begun to go translucent, the daikon floated in pale, perfect rounds.

"You'll cook a meal," Sera said. "You'll stand in a kitchen that isn't a ship, and you'll put something in a pot, and it will be the first thing you cook that isn't for forty-three thousand people who have to be on a specific schedule to not die. And it will be the easiest thing you've ever done, and the hardest thing you'll ever do."

The captain looked at the pot.

"Will it taste different?" she asked.

"I don't know," Sera said. "I don't know what it'll taste like on that world. I don't know what the soil will do to the tomatoes. I don't know if the wind will carry the smoke the way it does through the ventilation here. I don't know." She ladled some into a bowl and set it in front of the captain. "But you'll know when you taste it."

Maren ate the soup in silence. When she set the bowl down, she said, "Thank you, Sera. For the last one."

Sera nodded. She did not say *you're welcome*. That would have been a lie, because the thank-you was for the whole thing. For the ninety days of planning, the three weeks of rehearsal with the dining hall staff, the argument with the protein synthesizers about the final adjustment to the cultured dairy. For the last meal. For the fact that in nine days, she would walk out of this galley and into a field, and the field would be a kind of answer to a question she had been cooking toward her entire life without knowing it.

On the final day, the galley was full of people. Not in the usual sense. The dining hall was packed, as it always was for the large communal meal, but the cooks were in the back, moving in the quick, wordless choreography of a team that had cooked ten thousand meals and would cook one more. Dael had tears in his eyes, which he blamed on the steam, which he was not entirely wrong about.

Sera plated the final course herself. A simple thing: roasted vegetables from the last harvest in the hydroponic bay, a grain bread she had baked for four hours, a sauce that was the same recipe her grandmother had made the night the engines lit, adjusted only for the fact that the tomatoes here grew in a recirculated water column instead of a sun.

She carried the first plate to the front of the dining hall. The people were quiet. Not the quiet of a held breath, but the quiet of a room that has stopped arguing. Forty-three thousand people, sitting, waiting for the meal that would taste like the end of something.

Sera set the plate on the table for the captain. Then the next table. Then the next.

She moved down the rows, and the rows, and the rows, and the ship hummed around her, and the stars outside were still stars she could not name, and the food in her hands was warm and alive with the specific, irreplaceable flavor of being cooked by someone who had no other place to be.

She did not think about the field. She thought about the pot. The broth. The ratio. The small, private nod.

It was right.

And that was enough. For now. For nine more days. For the first meal on a world with a name she would have to learn.

But that was later. Right now, she was the cook, and the galley was quiet, and the plates were warm, and the meal was ready.
