# Chapter One: The First Morning

The city of Velthar sat on the back of the great turtle Orunna, and every morning began the same way—with the grinding of its spine.

Not the grinding of stone on stone, but of a living body stretching in the dark. The elders called it the Tremor, that first slow shudder that traveled up from the shell plates beneath the cobbles, rattling window frames and sending a plume of dust through every unsealed door. Young children learned to sleep on the north side of their beds so that, when the city rolled three degrees toward its own gullet-rim, they'd be tumbled toward the footboard rather than the wall.

Kael woke to the Tremor with a full mouth of blood.

She spat it onto the floor of the cramped room she shared with her mother, watched the dark drops catch the pale light seeping under the door, and felt the old fear settle behind her ribs. *Again.* The shell plates were shifting. They'd been shifting for six nights in a row, longer than anyone could remember, and the city's geomancers had run their instruments dry trying to find the cause.

"Up," her mother said from the narrow cot across the room. Ma's voice was the flat, careful tone she used when she didn't want to startle anyone. "The tide-lines are already moving. Go help at the market before the plates settle."

Kael swung her legs over the side of the bed and pressed her bare feet to the cold tile. The tile was warm. Not hot—warm, the way skin is warm after a long walk, and that was the wrong temperature for a morning in October on Orunna's back. She traced a line along the grout with one toe. The pattern of the tile had changed. It always changed, the old tiles migrated with the shell's slow growth, but this morning the lines ran at an angle she didn't recognize, slanting toward the eastern ridge where the great scarring ran.

The Scarring. That was what the children called it. The elders had a longer name—*the Wound of the First Crossing*—which referred to the night, three hundred years ago, when Orunna had dragged her belly across the Saltflats and the earth had split open in her wake. The scar stretched the length of the city's eastern edge, a raw gash in the shell-plate where the rock beneath had been torn away and the wet, black muscle of the turtle's own flesh showed through, oozing a slow black brine that smelled of iron and deep water.

The market was already stirring when she reached it. Kael's mother worked a stall selling pressed salt and smoked kelp to the fish-folk, who lived in the lower warrens beneath the shell's belly. The fish-folk spoke in a language of clicks and half-swallowing consonants that Kael had learned only fragments of, and they always smelled of brine and lamp-oil, and their scales caught the dawn in small, shifting flashes.

"Kael." The words from the fish-folk woman named Oru were clipped, half-choked. "The eastern line is wet. Wetter than wet. You feel it?"

Kael felt it. A thin film of that black brine had started to bead on the tiles near the stall's edge, pooling in the grooves. She crouched, pressed her fingers to the stone, and felt the faint, almost imperceptible pulse beneath. A heartbeat. Not the Tremor. Something deeper. Something underneath the turtle's own rhythm, as if something in the dark beneath Orunna's flesh had begun to knock.

"Ma," she said, standing, "the plates moved more than usual. And the eastern line—"

"Go," her mother said, not looking up from the kelp. Her hands were too steady for a mother who'd lost their father to the Scarring ten years ago. "Go to the Geomancers' Tower. Tell them the tiles are singing. Tell them the tiles are *singing*, Kael, because that is what they sound like, not the grinding. And bring me the boy. Bring him before the bell."

Kael didn't ask which boy. She already knew.

She climbed. The Tower was the only structure in Velthar that rose above the highest ridge of the shell, and it was built not of stone but of the turtle's own shed bones, the great curved ribs that Orunna exfoliated every few centuries and which the city's masons carved into arches and spires. The walls were warm. They were always warm, and in October the cold from the open sky above made the contrast a kind of small violence against the skin.

The Geomancers were already gathered in the lower chamber, a circle of them around a great instrument of brass and bone, its long needles trembling and weeping. The head, an old woman with eyes like cracked glass, met Kael's at the door.

"Go on," she said. "Speak. What do the tiles say?"

And Kael opened her mouth, and what came out was not a report. It was a name. A name she had never been given, a name that tasted of iron and deep water and the long, slow dark beneath the shell.

She spoke it once. And the brass needles stopped trembling.

All of them. At the same moment.

And in the silence they made, every person in the lower chamber heard it too: a sound from far below, rising through the bone-walls, through the warm living rock of Orunna's back, a sound like a door opening in a house that had been closed for a very long time.

The Geomancer head closed her eyes.

"Seal the eastern line," she said quietly. "Seal it all the way down to the marrow. And send riders to the south. Send riders *now*."

Kael looked at the old woman and said, "Who?"

The Geomancer didn't answer. She was already moving, gathering her instruments, her hands shaking in a way Kael had never seen them shake before.

Outside, the morning light climbed the eastern ridge and found the Scarring. The black brine that had been pooling in the grooves all night was now rising in a slow, steady line, cutting across the tile floor like a drawn blade. And where it passed, the tiles went dark. Not stained. Dark, as if something beneath them had turned its face up and was looking through.

Kael pressed her palm flat against the warm bone-wall of the tower and felt, beneath the familiar pulse of the great turtle, something new. A second rhythm. Slower. Patient. Waiting.

And then, very softly, like a whisper in the space between heartbeats:

*Thank you for the door.*
