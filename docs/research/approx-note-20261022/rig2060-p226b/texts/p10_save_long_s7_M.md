By the time it finally lifted, the whole town carried the smell of bread with it — warm dough, burnt sugar, a faint salt tang that clung to your coat and would not let go until the next evening. Elias Thorne's bakery was the older of the two, the one with the chimney that had seen three storms of his father's and six of his grandfather's. He rose at four each morning, long before the gulls began arguing over the harbor, and walked down to the cellar where Old Brin waited. Old Brin was a sourdough starter — a thick, pinkish paste, alive with a smell like wet earth and warm bread — and it had been fed, day after day, for sixty-one years. It had outlived every baker who had ever tended it, and it would outlive them all. Behind the counter stood Elias's grandfather's stone oven, a black-bellied beast built of river stone, and Elias knew its moods the way a fisherman knows the wind: a low drum of the fire meant patience, a high crackle meant hurry.

The Thorne family's bread was the town's memory. Sea bread was their signature — a dense, chewy loaf with a salt crust that crackled as it cooled, baked until the surface turned the color of driftwood on Gull Cove. For three generations, no matter what storm or hunger the town had known, the sea bread was there. Old Peter's widow still asked for a heel on Sundays. The captain's wife still called the bakery by name when she ordered. Elias did not do anything fancy, and he never would. Flour, water, salt, time — and the stone, which held the heat the way the sea held the light: all the way down.

A blue-painted brick oven with a French-trained baker behind the glass — all linen apron, flour-dusted wrist, and the kind of confidence that made small towns feel smaller than they were. That night, on Gull Street, there was no argument over the steam. The fog came in from the sea, and it filled the square and swallowed the lanterns, and the two bakeries stood side by side in the dark, their chimneys breathing the same slow, blue breath. If you went out into the street at midnight, you could still smell the bread, and you could smell the salt, and you could smell something that was neither one nor the other and was both, and it was the smell of a town that had decided, for the first time, to keep both things.

A year later, the street had changed. There was a shared shelf at the corner — not a shared counter, not a shared oven, just a shelf where the two bakers put a loaf each morning for whoever came by at seven, and no one was ever quite sure who had put it there first. The rivalry was still there. It was always there. Danny Hartwell still bit into the croissants with the same slow, gap-toothed awe. Old Peter's widow Margaret still asked for a heel on Sundays. And every morning, when the steam rose off the two bakeries at once, the town looked up, and the fog lifted a little, and the gulls came back, and Marrow's Landing had its two kinds of bread, and it was enough.
