## final text

# The Keeper at Blackrock Point

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, folded into a square the size of a postcard, with no stamp, no return address, and no name at the bottom of the envelope. Just a small hand-drawn lighthouse in blue ink on the front, and inside, in handwriting that was too small and too slanted, a single sentence: *The light is still turning, I can see it from the valley. Tell me the wind is west today.*

Elias set it on his kitchen table beside the salt-stained cup of tea he'd already finished. Outside, the wind was howling east, ripping at the cliff grass like something trying to climb the rocks. He read the sentence three times. He looked out the window at the beam cutting its slow arc through the November dark. He read the sentence a fourth time, then tucked it into the drawer where he kept his ship's logbooks.

He told no one. There was no one to tell. The supply boat came every fifteen days, and the postman on the mainland had stopped coming since the postal service rationalized its rural routes. Elias was sixty-eight, alone, and he had not expected to be surprised. The lighthouse had been in his family for four generations, and a lighthouse keeper's job, he had been told by his father, was to make sure the sea had no new tricks. But a letter with no return address was not a trick the sea played.

The second letter came nine days later. The handwriting was the same—slanted, hurried, as though the writer's hand was always a fraction behind her thoughts. *I think you'd be proud of my father. He's learning the ropes. He fell in the harbor on Tuesday and it's the third time this month. I told him to stop swimming in the cove like a man who's never seen water before. He did not stop.*

There was no signature. Elias read it twice, then sat in the kitchen for a long while with his hands on the table's edge. He knew the man. He knew everyone in the village of Harrow's Hollow, four miles down the coast. The harbor-master, Davy Crane, was a thick-necked man who swam in the cove in July and called it "a little exercise." He had not, to Elias's knowledge, attempted to swim in the harbor in November. And there was no daughter. Davy Crane's only child had died when it was three, taken by a fever in a hospital in the city.

Elias put the letter back in the drawer.

The third letter. The fourth. The fifth came within a month, always unposted, always with the small lighthouse sketch on the front, always in that same slanted script. They grew longer. They described a girl who could not yet be born—she called herself, in the second of these letters, simply *M*, and by the seventh she had given herself the name Clara. She described her mother as "tall and quiet and always looking at the ceiling as if waiting for it to fall." She described the summer they were going to take the old fishing boat out to the point where the light was, "just to see it up close," and how the boat leaked at the hull and they had to bail with buckets and she'd been crying but not from the water, "just because it was beautiful and I was so small I couldn't hold all of it."

Elias began to leave the drawer open. He kept the letters spread across the table, a small constellation of paper, and at night he read them by the lamp while the beam turned above him. He read them in the order they arrived, because they were not chronological, and that made them worse and better than chronological. The letter in which Clara wrote that she had broken her wrist in a fall from the hayloft came before the letter in which she mentioned, casually, that the hayloft was on their second summer in the house on the road. The letter in which she said *I don't know if you're real* came before the letter in which she said *I think you are the only person who has ever really listened to the sea.*

He did not write back. He could not. There was no name to address him to, no address, no way to say *I am here, I am Elias Voss, I have been here for thirty-one years, I am here.* The letters kept coming. One per week, roughly. The handwriting stayed the same, though the slant gradually lessened, as though whatever had been rushing her thoughts was slowing, settling.

In March, Elias's wife's sister's daughter, the only other woman who had ever lived within ten miles of him, came up to the lighthouse to check the oil supply and stood in the kitchen looking at the drawer of letters spread across the table.

"You don't know who this is," she said. It was not a question.

"I think I might," Elias said.

"That's not what you said six months ago."

"I might think it differently."

She looked at the lighthouse sketches on the envelopes. She looked at the beam outside. She did not look at him, which was, Elias thought, the kindest thing a person could do.

In June, the letter said: *The doctor says I'll be born in September. I'm scared. I thought you'd know what to say. I don't suppose you can. I don't suppose you'll even know I'm writing this. I don't know who you are, really. I know you turn the light. I know you keep the log. I know you have a cat named Bess and she sleeps on the second shelf and you feed her sardines and you talk to her in a low voice like you're being polite. I know these things and I don't know how I know them and it frightens me because I think it means you're the only other person in the world who is also not supposed to be here yet.*

Elias wrote Bess's name aloud to the empty kitchen, and the cat looked up from her shelf, as though the words were meant for her, and he fed her two sardines that evening instead of one.

The last letter from Clara arrived on a Saturday in August. It was longer than the others. The lighthouse sketch on the front was done in pencil this time, and there was a small addition: a child's handprint in red crayon pressed next to the beam.

*Dear Elias,* it began. She had learned his name. He did not know how.

*You'll be gone before I get old. I've thought about that a lot. Not in a sad way, not most of the time. Just... I think you've been keeping the light for a very long time and it's tired now and the light needs to be kept by someone who isn't you. I think that's all. I think that's the whole letter. I think I just needed you to know that I was here, for a little while, in the only way I knew how, before I was in the only way I actually am. You don't have to reply. You don't have to do anything. You can just keep the light turning. That's the whole job. That's all I ever needed you to do.*

*—C*

The drawer was full. Forty-three letters. The kitchen table was a small archive of a life that had not yet happened, a biography written by a woman who would not be born for another month, who would not know him, who would grow into a woman who mended nets for a living and hated the color yellow and once, in a letter she'd never write, described the exact shade of the sea on the night the lighthouse was automated.

He was sixty-nine now. He put the letters in a tin box. He locked the drawer. He climbed the two hundred and thirteen steps to the lantern room on the evening the light was turned over to the automatic system, the final ceremony, the last manual turn. He turned it one more time, by hand, and stood in the gallery as the beam swept out over the water, and he thought: *I see you from the valley.* And then: *The wind is west today.*

Clara was born on the first of October, in Harrow's Hollow, in a small room with a tall woman looking up at the ceiling. The father, Davy Crane, held her and said nothing for a long time. He had fallen in the harbor one last time that summer, in September, and it was the fourth time, and he did not get up. Clara was held by her mother, who was quiet and tall and looked at the ceiling as if it might fall.

The tin box sat on the second shelf in the lighthouse, next to Bess's favorite spot, for eleven more years. Then it did not sit there anymore. The lighthouse was closed. The town council sold the property. The box was in a moving van, in a storage unit in the city, in the attic of a house on a road in a different county.

Clara was nineteen when she found it. She had inherited it through a letter from a solicitor, a formal letter, a polite letter, with no lighthouse sketch on the front and no slanted handwriting inside. She opened it because she was curious, because she had always been curious, and because the lighthouse had been in her family's story since before she could remember, a detail her grandmother had mentioned once, passing, like a window left open in a hot house.

She read the letters in the garden, in the afternoon, with the tea going cold in her hand. She read them in the order they were stacked, because she was careful that way. She read them and she did not cry, which she had expected. She read them and the wind was west, and the light was turning, and she thought, for a very long time, that the whole world was just a lighthouse that someone, somewhere, was still keeping the log for.

Then she put the tin box on the shelf. She put a small cat on top of it, the way he had done. She went inside, and the house was quiet, and the light outside was still turning.
