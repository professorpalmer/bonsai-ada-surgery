# The First Meal

The hydroponics bay smelled of green things dying and green things beginning, and on the *Meridian* that was the smell of breakfast.

Ilya Voss stood at the pass with a ladle in his hand and listened to the ship. The hull hummed at a frequency that had, in his family's accounting, been steady for five hundred and forty-seven years. He was the sixty-second cook of the *Meridian* — not that any of them had a family, not really. The cook's line was the only family on the ship that anyone had ever been able to trace back. Okafor, who kept the first freeze-dryer going after the ice storm. Tanaka, who taught four thousand people to eat algae like a soup and the soup like a gift. His mother, Sable, who learned to make bread in a world that had never seen a field.

Ilya had never seen a field either. He had seen a green rectangle, thirty meters of leafy glass, and he had eaten well all his life in the belief that this was what Earth had always looked like.

That was the lie he carried. That was the lie every cook carried, and it was written at the top of the Ledger, the leather book of recipes bound in the ship's original hull plating: *We do not remember. Therefore we cook.*

On the morning the announcement came, the ship's voice was doing what voices do on the *Meridian*: speaking in a calm so level it was almost cruel.

"All hands, this is the vessel's command. The Kestrel has entered the final approach corridor. Expected orbital insertion in three years, two hundred and six days. Protocol Seven-Nine is now in effect."

Ilya kept the pot going. "What's Protocol Seven-Nine?" he asked the empty pass.

"Arrival Meal," the voice said.

The word fell through the galley the way a stone falls through a well. By lunch, every table in every deck had gone quiet. The Arrival Meal. One dish, served to four thousand people, identical to what the original crew ate in the hold the night the *Meridian* had left Earth behind. According to the Ledger's last entry, a recipe by the first cook, it had been "a feast for a whole new world, with a cake that made everyone cry."

Ilya had spent six years trying to make that cake. He had made it in the galley, in the hydroponics bay, in his quarters, on the deck, and he had come up with nothing that made anyone cry. He had come up with chocolate, and almond, and a dark syrup he called grief because it tasted like a Tuesday.

The vessel's voice spoke again, softer this time, and this was how the *Meridian* announced things it knew were heavy.

"First Cook Voss, the Vault Seven hold will be opened at 0900. Please report."

He was walking the corridor with his apron still on when the child on Deck Nine was crying, and a man on Deck Twelve had put down his ration tray and was staring at the wall like he was trying to read something there. The whole ship knew what was coming. The whole ship had been preparing for it for five hundred years, the way you prepare for a door you've always been told is at the end of the hall.

Ilya didn't knock. He put his palm to the Vault door. The door opened because it was his work, because the handprint on the plate had been his mother's, because his mother's handprint had been the first cook's.

Inside, the stasis light was blue and slow, and the first meal sat on a steel table under it: a row of tins, a packet of salt, a crumpled cloth, and a data card so old it looked like a chip of bone.

There was no cake.

There was no feast.

There were tins of beans, black and brown and gray, with labels that had faded to smudges. A packet of salt with the words *do not run out* written in pencil across the side, in handwriting so old it looked like a scar. A crumpled cloth, clean and folded, the kind you used to wipe your face when there was no towel and no mirror and no one to look at you with.

And the card.

The card played on the table, its image flickering in the blue light, a dozen faces, faces no one had ever seen, faces the ship had carried across five hundred years of dark. A woman with flour on her jaw. A man with a child's hand on his shoulder. A face looking directly at the camera, with something in her eyes that Ilya had spent his whole life trying to put in the food, and finally understood he had been trying to put in the wrong dish.

"We are forty thousand," the woman said. "And we are going to eat beans. Do not make this a story about the food. Make it a story about the going." The pause. "Tell them we were afraid. Tell them we were hungry. Tell them we ate, and the going started, and the going is what makes us the kind of people who can land."

The stasis light held. The words finished. Ilya stood in the blue dark with the tins and the salt and the cloth, and the only sound was the ship, the same hum, the same steady sound, and he understood what every cook before him had understood in some small, frightened way and could not name:

That the Arrival Meal was not a meal.

It was an apology.

It was the first thing they had eaten after leaving everything that had ever fed them, and it had been a tin of beans, and no one had said it out loud, and every cook since had been trying to make the ship forget, with cake and with bread and with a green rectangle that was supposed to be a field, the thing they were not ready to say: *We started with nothing, and we ate, and that was enough, and that is why we are here.*

The vessel's voice asked if he was all right. It asked in the same level voice it used for weather and for hull pressure and for the morning announcement of the algae harvest.

"I'm all right," Ilya said. "Tell the decks I want a pot. A big one. And I want the salt."

The salt had not run out. It had lasted five hundred and forty-seven years, a packet of it, and Ilya knew this the way you know the shape of a word in the dark: not by looking, but by feeling. He had been feeding four thousand people, and the thing that had kept them fed was never the food. It was the salt, and the salt was here, and the salt was enough.

He made the meal that afternoon. He opened the tins with a hand that did not shake, and he added water from the reclamation line and salt from the packet, and he cooked the beans until they were soft and brown and honest, and he tore the cloth into a hundred pieces and laid them across a hundred tables as napkins, because the cloth had been the first cloth, and it deserved to be used the way it had been meant to be used.

He did not put it on the menu. He put it on the table, and he set the salt in the middle, and he stood at the pass with the ladle in his hand and he said, for the first time, the thing the ship's voice had never said, the thing the Ledger had never written, the thing every cook had carried in the dark and called by a wrong name:

"The first meal was beans. We were afraid, and we were hungry, and we ate, and the going started. I am the sixty-second cook, and I have made this meal five hundred and forty-seven years of cooking, and it is not a story about the food. It is a story about the going."

The ship was quiet. Four thousand people sat in the quiet of their decks, and the quiet was the size of the hull, and the hull was the size of the dark, and the dark was the size of everything that had been left behind, and none of them said anything, and the beans were hot, and the salt was on the table, and the cloth was folded clean.

On the last table in the last deck, a child with flour on his jaw — because someone had given him a spoon and he had put it in his hand and the hand had slipped and the flour had come — looked at the plate and said, very quietly, "It tastes like the stories."

And Ilya, who had spent his life trying to make a cake that made everyone cry, set down the ladle and thought, *That's all it was ever going to be. Not a feast. A meal. Not the end of the story. The first line of it. And we are still going. We are still going, and the salt has lasted this long, and the salt will last us to the ground, and the ground will have its own tins, and its own packet of salt, and someone will have to learn to make the beans.*

He thought that, and he tasted the broth, and it was honest, and it was enough, and the hull hummed beneath him at the same steady frequency it had always hummed, and for the first time in the history of the ship, Ilya understood that the hum was not the sound of the engine.

It was the sound of the going.

# The First Meal

The hydroponics bay smelled of green things dying and green things beginning, and on the *Meridian* that was the smell of breakfast. The galley itself was a room the size of a chapel, with copper pots hanging from the rafters like sleeping birds, and the pass where Ilya Voss stood was worn smooth by the cooks before him. Every morning he lit the stove in the same order, and every morning the pots came back to him with the same sound, like a choir tuning up before a song no one on the ship remembered the words to.

Ilya was the sixty-second cook of the *Meridian*, and he could recite the line like a prayer. Okafor, who kept the first freeze-dryer going after the ice storm. Tanaka, who taught four thousand people to eat the soup like a gift. His mother, Sable, who learned to make bread in a world that had never seen a field.

Ilya had never seen a field either. He had seen a green rectangle, thirty meters of leafy glass, and he had eaten well all his life in the belief that this was what Earth had always looked like.

That was the lie every cook carried, and it was written at the top of the Ledger, the book of recipes bound in the ship's original hull plating: *We do not remember. Therefore we cook.*

On the morning the announcement came, the vessel's voice was doing what voices do on the *Meridian*: speaking in a calm so level it was almost cruel.

"All hands, this is the vessel's command. The Kestrel has entered the final approach corridor. Expected orbital insertion in three years, two hundred and six days. Protocol Seven-Nine is now in effect."

Ilya kept the pot going. "And what's Protocol Seven-Nine?" he asked the empty pass.

"Arrival Meal," the voice said.

The word fell through the galley the way a stone falls through a well. By lunch, every table on every deck had gone quiet. The Arrival Meal. One dish, served to four thousand people, identical to what the original crew ate in the hold the night the *Meridian* had left Earth behind. According to the Ledger, a feast for a whole new world, with a cake that made everyone cry.

Ilya had spent six years trying to make that cake. He had come up with chocolate, and almond, and a dark syrup he called grief because it tasted like a Tuesday. Nothing had ever made anyone cry. His apprentice, Dae, had been keeping a private notebook of every failed version for three years. "Why do you keep trying?" she had asked once. "The story's finished. Everyone eats fine." Ilya had not answered, because the answer was that eating fine was not the point. The point was to remember why you ate at all.

The vessel's voice came again, softer this time, which was how the *Meridian* announced the heavy things.

"First Cook Voss, Vault Seven will be opened at 0900. Please report."

Ilya didn't knock. He put his palm to the vault door, and it opened because the handprint on the plate was his mother's, and his mother's handprint was the first cook's.

Inside, the stasis light was blue and slow, and the first meal sat on a steel table under it: a row of tins, a packet of salt, a crumpled cloth, and a data card so old it looked like a chip of bone.

There was no cake. There was no feast. There were tins of beans, black and brown and gray, with labels faded to smudges. A packet of salt with *do not run out* written in pencil across the side, in handwriting so old it looked like a scar. A clean, folded cloth, the kind you use when there is no towel and no mirror and no one to look at you with.

The card played on the table, its image flickering in the blue light. A dozen faces no one had ever seen, faces the ship had carried across five hundred years of dark. A woman with flour on her jaw. A man with a child's hand on his shoulder.

"We are forty thousand," the woman said. "And we are going to eat beans. Do not make this a story about the food. Make it a story about the going. Tell them we were afraid. Tell them we were hungry. Tell them we ate, and the going started, and the going is what makes us the kind of people who can land."

The stasis light held. Ilya stood in the blue dark and understood, the way you understand a thing you have been carrying for years without noticing it, that the Arrival Meal was not a meal. It was the first thing they had eaten after leaving everything that had ever fed them, and it had been a tin of beans, and no one had ever said so out loud. Every cook since had been trying to make the ship forget, with cake and with bread and with a green rectangle that was supposed to be a field: *We started with nothing, and we ate, and that was enough, and that is why we are here.*

The vessel's voice asked if he was all right, in the same level voice it used for weather and for hull pressure and for the morning announcement of the algae harvest.

"I'm all right," Ilya said. "Tell the decks I want a pot. A big one. And I want the salt."

The salt had lasted five hundred and forty-seven years. Ilya knew it the way you know the shape of a word in the dark: not by looking, but by feeling. He had been feeding four thousand people, and the thing that had kept them fed was never the food. It was the salt. It was here. It was enough.

He made the meal that afternoon. He opened the tins with a hand that did not shake, and he added reclamation-line water and salt from the packet, and he cooked the beans until they were soft and brown and honest. He tore the cloth into a hundred pieces and laid them across a hundred tables as napkins, because the cloth had been the first cloth, and it deserved to be used the way it had been meant to be used.

He did not put it on the menu. He put it on the table, set the salt in the middle, and stood at the pass with the ladle in his hand, and said, for the first time, the thing the ship had never said out loud and the Ledger had never written:

"The first meal was beans. We were afraid, and we were hungry, and we ate, and the going started. I am the sixty-second cook, and I have made this meal five hundred and forty-seven years of cooking, and it is not a story about the food. It is a story about the going."

The ship went quiet. Four thousand people sat in the quiet of their decks, and the quiet was the size of the hull, and the hull was the size of the dark, and none of them said anything, and the beans were hot, and the salt was on the table, and the cloth was folded clean. On Deck Three, an old engineer who had spent thirty years on the hull set her spoon down and said, "I remember now," and she did not say what she remembered, and no one expected her to.

On the last table in the last deck, a child with a flour-dusted hand — someone had handed him a spoon, and the spoon had slipped, and the flour had come — looked at his plate and said, very quietly, "It tastes like the stories."

And Ilya, who had spent six years trying to make a cake that made everyone cry, set down the ladle. *That's all it was ever going to be*, he thought. *Not a feast. A meal. Not the end of the story. The first line of it. And we are still going. And the salt has lasted this long. And the salt will last us to the ground, and the ground will have its own tins, and its own packet of salt, and someone will have to learn to make the beans.*

He tasted the broth. It was honest. It was enough.

Beneath him, the hull hummed at the same steady frequency it had always hummed, and for the first time in the history of the ship, Ilya understood that the hum was not the sound of the engine.

It was the sound of the going.
