The Last Recipe

The *Persephone* woke the way a sleeper wakes: with a shudder in the floor, then with the voice of the announcement.

"Attention to all hands. We have entered the approach corridor. Arrival in the Kepler system in thirty-one days."

Maren kept her hands in the stock. The broth had been going for two days; it was made from the trimmings of the hydroponic bay—carrot tops, the skins of the potatoes, the ends of the celery that no one ate. On a ship where every calorie was accounted for, that broth was her private language: a way of saying *waste is a word, not a law.*

For forty-three years she had been the cook of the *Persephone*, which meant that she had been the cook of 4,182 people who would never see the sea, and of another 3,000 people yet to be born, who would also never see the sea.

In the old Earth, they called the galley the heart of the house. Her grandmother, Elise, had cooked for three hundred souls in the colony of New Kyoto, and she had said: "A ship without a cook is a coffin with a schedule. A ship with a cook is a home in motion."

The ship was a home in motion. But a home that had been moving so long that "home" had become a technical term, like "orbit" or "pressure cycle."

Ido stood at the end of the counter, waiting for his turn with the bread dough. He was nineteen, the last cook in the next generation after Maren and her two apprentices. His hands were good; his nose was even better. He could tell when the yeast was tired before she could, and he had a habit of stealing the first crumb of every loaf, which she let happen, because a cook who didn't taste first was only doing math.

"The announcement," he said. "Thirty-one days."

"Thirty-one days," she confirmed. "How's the sourdough?"

"It's angry."

"Good. Angry bread rises."

He laughed, but his eyes didn't reach it. In the corridor outside the galley, people were stopping, pressing their ears to the bulkheads, whispering. Arrival. The word that had been a thing in the archives, a thing in the schoolbooks, a thing the children were told would happen to other people. They were going to land on a planet with an orange sun. The charts said the soil was dark and the rain was steady. The children had drawn it in crayon: a hill, a house, a tree, a door.

Maren took a loaf out of the oven and set it on the cooling rack. The smell of it went through the galley, and the crew stopped eating, and for one second the ship held its breath. Bread on the *Persephone* was not bread. It was a small, warm proof that the world outside the hull still existed in a form that could be broken, torn, eaten.

"Grandmother Elise," Ido said, "what did she do for the last meal?"

Maren set the loaf down. "What last meal? We've been on a ship for two hundred and twelve years, Ido. There's no last meal. There's just the next one."

"Before you," then. "What was it like? Before the archive?"

The archive. She had thought about it for three days, since the announcement. Twelve thousand recipes had been written into the *Persephone's* memory banks before departure. Soups, stews, cakes, curries, breads, sauces. They were not instructions; they were letters. Every recipe was a way of saying: *this is what we ate, this is what we loved, this is what we were.*

And she had been eating from that archive every day for forty-three years.

She had not been eating the recipes.

"Tell me," she said. "What did you taste this morning?"

Ido thought. "Water. Then the rice. Then the soup. Then the bread."

"And the soup?"

"Celery. Carrots. Potatoes."

"And the taste?"

He was quiet for a long time. "It tastes like soup," he said. "It tastes like what the children say soup is."

Maren nodded. She knew what he meant. She had known it since she was nineteen and her grandmother had handed her a knife and said, "You are not cooking the ingredients. You are cooking the memory of the ingredients. The ingredients are what's on the shelf. The memory is what's in the pantry."

On the *Persephone*, the pantry was the archive.

She had made the wedding cake for Ido's parents with hydroponic sugar and a vanilla substitute that tasted of nothing in particular. She had made Elise's birthday cake with the same sugar. She had made the anniversary cake with the same sugar. Every year, the same sugar, the same flavorless vanilla, and the people had smiled and said "magnificent." And she had let them, because it was true in the only way that mattered: it was a cake, and it was for them.

But tonight, tonight was different.

"Ido," she said. "Bring me the recipe for Elise's bread."

He brought it. She read it. Flour. Water. Salt. Yeast. And then, in a small, faded line at the bottom, in a hand that shook: *Use salt from the sea. Not the hydroponic salt. The real thing. The salt that comes from the sea and from the rain and from the earth and from the air. The kind you can taste in a person's tears.*

Ido read it over her shoulder. "That's not a recipe," he said. "That's a person."

"Yes," said Maren. "That's what a recipe is. A person who's gone."

She set down the recipe. She took off her gloves. For the first time in twenty years, she wasn't wearing gloves. The dough was on the counter, and her bare hands were in it, and it was cool and alive and it was the first thing in twenty years that had tasted like something other than the ship.

"Bring the people," she said. "Everyone. Tomorrow, we eat the last meal."

Ido looked at her. "The last meal?"

"The last meal of the *Persephone*." She met his eyes. "After tomorrow, we land. And we stop cooking on a ship. And we start cooking on a planet."

He didn't speak. He put on gloves, and he started the dough.

# The Last Recipe

The *Persephone* woke the way a sleeper wakes: with a shudder in the floor, then with the voice of the announcement.

"Attention to all hands. We have entered the approach corridor. Arrival in the Kepler system in thirty-one days."

Maren kept her hands in the stock. The broth had been going for two days; it was made from the trimmings of the hydroponic bay—carrot tops, the skins of the potatoes, the ends of the celery that no one ate. On a ship where every calorie was accounted for, that broth was her private language: a way of saying *waste is a word, not a law.*

For forty-three years she had been the cook of the *Persephone*, which meant she had been the cook of 4,182 people who would never see the sea, and of another 3,000 people yet to be born, who would also never see the sea.

On old Earth, they had called the galley the heart of the house. Her grandmother, Elise, had cooked for three hundred souls in the colony of New Kyoto, and she had said: "A ship without a cook is a coffin with a schedule. A ship with a cook is a home in motion."

The *Persephone* was a home in motion. But a home that had been moving so long that *home* had become a technical term, like *orbit* or *pressure cycle*.

Ido stood at the end of the counter, waiting for his turn with the bread dough. He was nineteen, the first cook of the generation after Maren. His hands were good; his nose was even better. He could tell when the yeast was tired before she could, and he had a habit of stealing the first crumb of every loaf, which she let happen, because a cook who didn't taste first was only doing math.

"The announcement," he said. "Thirty-one days."

"Thirty-one days," she confirmed. "How's the sourdough?"

"It's angry."

"Good. Angry bread rises."

He laughed, but his eyes didn't reach it. In the corridor outside the galley, people were stopping, pressing their ears to the bulkheads, whispering. *Arrival.* The word that had been a thing in the archives, a thing in the schoolbooks, a thing the children were told would happen to other people. They were going to land on a planet with an orange sun. The charts said the soil was dark and the rain was steady. The children had drawn it in crayon: a hill, a house, a tree, a door.

Maren took a loaf out of the oven and set it on the cooling rack. The smell of it went through the galley, and the crew stopped eating, and for one second the ship held its breath. Bread on the *Persephone* was not bread. It was a small, warm proof that the world outside the hull still existed in a form that could be broken, torn, eaten.

"Grandmother Elise," Ido said, "what did she do for the last meal?"

Maren set the loaf down. "What last meal? We've been on a ship for two hundred and twelve years, Ido. There's no last meal. There's just the next one."

"Before you, then. What was it like? Before the archive?"

The archive. She had thought about it for three days, since the announcement. Twelve thousand recipes had been written into the *Persephone's* memory banks before departure. Soups, stews, cakes, curries, breads, sauces. They were not instructions; they were letters. Every recipe was a way of saying: *this is what we ate, this is what we loved, this is what we were.*

And she had been eating from that archive every day for forty-three years.

She had not been eating the recipes.

"Tell me," she said. "What did you taste this morning?"

Ido thought. "Water. Then the rice. Then the soup. Then the bread."

"And the soup?"

"Celery. Carrots. Potatoes."

"And the taste?"

He was quiet for a long time. "It tastes like soup," he said. "It tastes like what the children say soup is."

Maren nodded. She knew what he meant. She had known it since she was nineteen and her grandmother had handed her a knife and said, "You are not cooking the ingredients. You are cooking the memory of the ingredients. The ingredients are what's on the shelf. The memory is what's in the pantry."

On the *Persephone*, the pantry was the archive.

She had made the wedding cake for Ido's parents with hydroponic sugar and a vanilla substitute that tasted of nothing in particular. She had made Elise's birthday cake with the same sugar. She had made the anniversary cake with the same sugar. Every year, the same sugar, the same flavorless vanilla, and the people had smiled and said *magnificent.* And she had let them, because it was true in the only way that mattered: it was a cake, and it was for them.

But tonight, tonight was different.

"Ido," she said. "Bring me the recipe for Elise's bread."

He brought it. She read it. Flour. Water. Salt. Yeast. And then, in a small, faded line at the bottom, in a hand that shook: *Use salt from the sea. Not the hydroponic salt. The real thing. The salt that comes from the sea and from the rain and from the earth and from the air. The kind you can taste in a person's tears.*

Ido read it over her shoulder. "That's not a recipe," he said. "That's a person."

"Yes," said Maren. "That's what a recipe is. A person who's gone."

She set down the recipe. She took off her gloves. For the first time in twenty years, she wasn't wearing gloves. The dough was on the counter, and her bare hands were in it, and it was cool and alive, and it was the first thing in twenty years that had tasted like something other than the ship.

"Bring the people," she said. "Everyone. Tomorrow, we eat the last meal."

Ido looked at her. "The last meal?"

"The last meal of the *Persephone*." She met his eyes. "After tomorrow, we land. And we stop cooking on a ship. And we start cooking on a planet."

He didn't speak. He put on gloves, and he started the dough.

She made the menu the old way: a small one. She did not try to make a feast, because a feast for seven thousand souls was a small thing on a ship, and a small thing in a different way on a planet. She made Elise's bread, because that was the recipe that was hers. She made a soup with the trimmings. She made a cake with the sugar that tasted like nothing. She set the table in the central atrium, where the lights were set to the color of late afternoon in Kyoto, which she had seen only in photographs and which had never once, in her life, been real.

The crew came slowly at first. Then in groups. Then in a stream. They filled the atrium, and the tables, and the chairs, and the floor, and they stood in the aisles with their arms at their sides and their mouths open and their eyes on the bread.

Maren set the first loaf on the counter. It was not perfect. The crust was too dark on one side, and the crumb was a little tight, and it was the first loaf she had made in twenty years without gloves, and it was the best thing she had ever made.

She broke it open, and the steam went up, and it went up through the atrium, and it went up through the ship, and it went up into the dark beyond the hull, and it went up into the orange sun that was growing on the charts, and it went up into the air that was coming, and it went up into the ground that was coming, and it went up into the rain that was coming, and it went up into the tears that were coming.

Ido stood at the end of the counter, and he was crying, and he was smiling, and he was reaching for the bread, and he was taking a crumb, and he was putting it in his mouth, and he was chewing, and he was swallowing, and he was saying, "This tastes like the sea." Then: "This tastes like the rain." Then: "This tastes like the tears." And, lastly: "This tastes like home."

Maren nodded. "Yes," she said. "It does."

And then the doors opened. And the air came in, and it smelled of nothing she had a name for yet.
