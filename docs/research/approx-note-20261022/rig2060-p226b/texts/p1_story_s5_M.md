## final text

# The Keeper of Light

The island had a name, though Elias Voss stopped using it after the third winter of his fourth year. To the postmark on the few surviving letters from the mainland, it was simply "Voss Light, Skellig Minor." That was enough. That was all of it. A rock no larger than a kitchen, a lighthouse the color of salt-stained bone, and a man of sixty-three who spoke to the gulls and called them the only reasonable company on God's forgotten coast.

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday in November, slipped beneath the door of his cottage while a squall hammered the glass. There was no return address. The envelope was plain, cream-colored, and the handwriting inside was small and careful, each letter formed as though the writer were afraid of taking up too much space.

*Dear Mr. Voss,*

*I don't know if you'll read this. I don't know if you know my name. But I think about the light. Every day I think about it, and I want you to know that the dark is not the same as the absence of the dark. The dark is a country. I am a citizen of it. Please keep the light on.*

Elias read it twice. He held the paper to the firelight. The ink was fresh, the paper not aged. He turned it over. Nothing. He set it in his coat pocket and went back to trimming the wick in the lamp room.

The second letter came six days later.

*My name is Mara. I am eight years old. I live in a town called Dunmore, which is where I was born, or where I will be born, or which is where I will be remembered. I tell you this so you know I am real, even if you cannot find me. My mother says I have a terrible habit of talking to people who aren't there. I think you are one of them. The lamp in your tower is the only thing in my whole life that points in a fixed direction. I have never seen anything else do that.*

Elias wrote back. He had a habit, when no one else could be reached, of writing to no one. He wrote in the margins of supply lists, in the blank spaces of the logbook. He wrote: *I am here. The light is on. It has been on for forty-one years and it will be on when I am not. I do not know a Mara in Dunmore. I do not know a Dunmore. But the light is on.*

The reply came in a week, which should have been impossible given the distance to any post office. It sat in his mailbox, which he kept only because the mainland courier still made the crossing twice a month with the mail.

*You will be dead in six years, Mr. Voss. I know this the way a river knows its delta. I am sorry. I am also not sorry. I needed to tell you someone before the telling, so that you wouldn't be alone in the last winter. I have a scar on my left knee from falling off the seawall at the harbor. My mother's hair is going gray at the temples. I am telling you these things as if they are finished, as if I am a book whose final page I have already turned. Is that strange? I think it is only honest. The light is on. I can see it from my window even though it is forty miles away and I have never been on your island. I see it on every night that the clouds permit. I have started a list. I put every name I know on it. You are not on it yet. You are on it in my head.*

Elias kept the letters on a shelf above his stove, in a row that grew longer with each month. There were forty of them by the time winter set in for good. Mara wrote about the shape of her classroom, about a boy who collected stones, about the particular blue of the sea after a storm. She wrote in the past tense of her own childhood, and Elias, who had never once in his life been told the future, found he preferred the tense to the present. The present was a wound. The future was a promise that hadn't yet learned how to hurt.

He began to leave the lamp room door unlocked at night, not because he feared theft but because he wanted to see the light from outside, the way she described it. From the rocks, the beam cut across the water in a slow, stately sweep, and it was beautiful in the way that a sentence is beautiful when you can finally see all of it at once. He stood there in his oilskin, cold to the bone, and he thought: *She says she sees it from a window forty miles away. I do not know if this is a miracle or a madness. I do not know if there is a Mara in Dunmore. I do not know if Dunmore exists. I know only that she is real to me, and that I am real to her, and that the light does not care which tense is true.*

In the spring of his seventy-second year, the mainland sent a man with a clipboard and a photograph of a new automated beacon. The light would be run by a satellite. The keeper would be released. Elias nodded at the man as the man spoke, but his eyes were on the beam still cutting its old circles in the sky.

The last letter came on the day before the automated beacon was installed.

*Mr. Voss,*

*I am thirty-one now. I have a daughter. I name her after the only person I have ever spoken to who is not in the room. I name her Voss, though she has never met you and I have never met you and no one who will live after me will either. I tell you this so you know that the light did not die. It changed. I stand in my daughter's nursery in Dunmore — yes, Dunmore is real, it is a town of nine hundred people with a harbor that floods every December — and I look out the window at the sea and I am not looking for a beam. I am looking for the place where the beam was, and the place is enough. It was enough for you. It is enough for me. I am telling you this as a finished thing. I am telling you this as a woman who has lived and died in the telling. You will be installed your beacon. I will not be there to see it. You will not be there to see it. But the dark will still be a country, and we will still be citizens, and the light will still have been on. I loved the light. I love the person who kept it. I love you, Mr. Voss, in the way that a reader loves a book they have read in a single sitting, all at once, with the whole architecture visible.*

*Thank you for keeping the dark company.*

*— M.*

Elias read the letter at the top of the tower, in the lamp room, with the great lens turning its slow circle of light around him. The beacon would come in the morning. He would pack his coat and his logbook and his forty-one years of logbook entries and his row of letters on the shelf, and he would walk down the rocks and take the crossing to the mainland, and he would be seventy-three, and there would be no one to tell him he had been real, and that would be all right.

He took a pencil from his pocket and, at the bottom of the letter, in small careful letters, he wrote: *The light is on. It will be on. I keep the dark company. It is not the same as the absence of the dark.*

He folded the letter, placed it in the row above the stove, and went to wind the final turn of the clock that drove the beacon. The beam swept out over the water, and for one long moment it caught the edge of the island, and Elias Voss stood in it, a man made of light, and the dark around him was a country he had been a citizen of all along.
