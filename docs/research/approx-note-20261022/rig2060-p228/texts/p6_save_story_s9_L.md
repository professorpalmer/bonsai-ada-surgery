# What the Garden Gave

When I was an apprentice in the lower galley, they told me that a generation ship doesn't run on fusion. It runs on appetite. The reactor keeps the lights on. The kitchen keeps people alive. I am Kest, third-generation cook aboard the *Meridian*, and in the forty-third year of our voyage to Tau Ceti, the tomato crop of bay nine died in a single night. Not gradually. Overnight. The vines turned to black lace, and by the time the hydroponics crew woke to the smell, the whole bay smelled of wet paper. I had smelled that once in my life, on Earth, in my grandmother's greenhouse after a storm. I was seven, and the greenhouse had survived the storm.

The protein vats could cover the loss. The vats grew anything: protein bricks grey as a child's school uniform, shaped into whatever the menu demanded. The chief engineer told me the shortfall would be made up by Thursday, and he said it with the flat courtesy of a man reading a forecast. But a grey brick is not a tomato. A tomato is not a memory. On the *Meridian*, the kitchen is where we keep the memory.

The memory lives in a glass case in the climate bay, and the case smells faintly of rain. Inside are the heirloom seeds of a world we will never set foot on: Cherokee purple, Black Krim, small round cherry tomatoes that split at the slightest pressure, like little promises breaking. Alongside them sits a paper recipe book, its pages so old they crack when you turn them. My grandmother's book. She carried it aboard on the first flight, along with a jar of honey that has since become a museum exhibit and a bottle of oil that I use in my own recipes. Every dish in the book has a date and a name. *Grandma Okafor's stew, 1988. My mother's bread, 2031. Kest's soup, year nine*, written in pencil by a sixteen-year-old who thought she could write with a steady hand and didn't.

We eat those recipes every year, not because the ship needs them but because they are ours. The *Meridian* feeds four thousand people, and the kitchen feeds four thousand stories. When a new child comes aboard, we let them taste the first thing I cook, and that taste follows them to adulthood. When someone dies, I cook the dish from their recipe. The kitchen is where the ship remembers that it began on a green planet with rain.

On Friday, Chief Engineer Rho stood in my kitchen with his tablet and told me to empty the seed case. "Bay nine is dead. The backup systems are marginal. The seeds in that case are the only viable biological material we have." He said it politely, the way a doctor says a thing that will be taken from you. "We'll grow them as a nutrient crop. The yield will be higher. Protein output stays the same. No one will eat the difference."

No one will eat the difference. He was not lying about the arithmetic. The arithmetic was the problem.

"There's the harvest festival," I said. "The children will be looking for vine tomatoes."

"The festival is on the lower decks. We'll use the hydroponic substitute."

"Everyone will know they're a substitute."

He looked at me the way engineers look at weather — something that happens to other people — and left the decision to the council. By sunset the council had voted. Four to three. Two of the votes were mine and my mother's, and the other three came from people who had never held soil in their hands.

I didn't sleep. I stood in the galley and watched the protein vats pulse their quiet orange, and I thought about what would become of a ship that fed four thousand people on nothing but bricks. We wouldn't die. We would become something else. Something that no longer asked for things, because it had forgotten how to miss them.

Around midnight I took the service elevator up to the climate bay. The case was already open. The engineers had access, but the glass still held the ghost of rain. I took the seed packets out one by one and set them in the pouch at my hip. I left the recipe book. The book had a place.

In the morning the hydroponics crew found the case empty, and I told them the seeds had been transferred to the reserve store. It was a small lie, and it tasted like salt on my tongue. I had a day before anyone would check the reserve store. The reserve store is below the deck plating, in the dark, and its climate is set for the long cold. You do not grow tomatoes in the dark. You grow nothing but the dark.

I needed a place that would lie less. I found one in the nursery.

The nursery is where the ship's children sleep and where the old ship's crew gathers on holidays because the air there is soft. I asked for a small plot in the teaching garden, the one with the warm floor and the forgiving light. I told the nursery chief I was planting something for the festival, something the children should learn to pull from the ground. She looked at the pouch at my hip, but children's cooks have always been allowed a little mystery, and she gave me the plot.

I planted the Cherokee purple first. Then the Black Krim. Then the cherry seeds, a scatter, the way my grandmother used to scatter theirs in her backyard in Lagos, where the tomatoes grew up around the fences and into the windowsills of the neighbor's house. I covered them with soil from a bag, watered them with rainwater that the ship had grown from sea, and I stood there for a long time while the garden learned my hands.

Weeks passed. I checked them at night when the nursery slept, kneeling in the dark to look for the first green line. I told no one. I told no one but the plants, which have always been good keepers of secrets and ask nothing in return but water and time. When the first true leaves appeared — two of them, like little green palms — I carried the pots out of the nursery in the small hours and set them on a shelf behind the stove, where the steam from the soup kettles rose and kissed their leaves.

The festival came on the twenty-second day of the cycle. The nursery children were brought to the long table in the lower deck, and I was cooking the harvest stew, the one from the book, my grandmother's, with the tomato paste from the reserve and the dried basil from the climate bay. It was a good stew. It was also, I knew, a lie.

My mother came to the table first, as mothers do. She tasted the stew and her eyes went quiet in the way they do when a memory arrives uninvited. She looked at me. I looked at her. She set her spoon down very carefully.

"The vine ones," she said. "From the case."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to answer.

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I have never seen her do in the forty-three years since I was born. She laughed. Not a soft laugh. A real one, the kind that makes a room feel wider. "You stole the garden from the engineers," she said.

"I saved it," I said.

She came around the table and took my hand, and the children, sensing that the adults had stopped being adults, began to whisper and pass around the bread. My mother took a small vine tomato — I had hidden one in my apron, the very first one the plant had given, the size of a thumbnail, red as a wound — and ate it whole. The juice ran down her chin, and she didn't wipe it away. She ate it like a prayer.

Around the table, people ate the stew and the bread and the first true tomatoes of the voyage, and I watched them eat, and I thought: this is what they voted to lose. Not the protein. Not the yield. The taste. The small green thing that says *this is where we were, and here we are*.

The engineers never found the nursery plot. I have kept it since, and the children have learned to pull tomatoes from the ground, and last year I wrote a new recipe in my grandmother's book, in my pencil, on the page I had kept blank for ten years. *Kest's festival stew, year thirty-nine. Vine tomatoes, nursery garden.*

The ship still flies. The reactor still hums its one low note through the dark, and in a little while — in nine hundred years, maybe a thousand — we will be at the edge of Tau Ceti and the children will be grown old enough to remember nothing of Earth, which is as it should be. But on the nights when the hull settles and the whole ship creaks like a great animal shifting in its sleep, someone in the lower galley opens the small plot in the nursery and checks the plants. And the plants, which have always been patient, come up green in the warm dark.

A generation ship runs on appetite. I am still learning what it runs on, too. I think it runs on this: the small red thing, the long flight, the hand in the dark.
