Chapter One  
The Last Whistle

The first thing you learn when you spend thirty years on a mountain line is that silence has a texture.

In Blackpine, it was never true silence. The town sat in a fold of the mountains where the wind came through the pines like a long, low complaint. Even in summer, when the air smelled of hot granite and damp moss, there was always something moving beyond the trees: a hawk, a falling branch, the faint percussion of ice cracking in the river. But on a winter night, with the snow lying deep and the old rail line silent beneath it, the quiet became almost solid. You could press your ear against a rail and feel the mountain breathing.

I knew that breathing better than most.

I was washing a coffee mug when I saw the headlights on the road below my cabin. They came up the switchback slowly, the way a man walks after too much coffee and not enough sleep. I set the mug down and went to the window. The snow was falling thin and straight, as if the sky had decided to be serious about it. Blackpine sprawled beneath me, half a dozen streetlights burning yellow in the dark. The old depot stood at the edge of town, its red paint peeling in long ribbons, its windows dark. I had not lived there officially for years, not since I retired from the Blackpine Branch and sold my uniform to the town hall. But I still had the keys, and I still paid rent to the historical society in exchange for letting them use the place as a storage shed.

My name is Elias Thorne. For thirty-four years I conducted the mountain branch that ran from Blackpine to the county line, and for the last eleven years I have tried to live like a man who no longer belonged to the railway. I fail at this, usually.

The truck stopped in front of the cabin. I opened the door before the engine cut off. Sheriff Mara Danner had been in Blackpine long enough to know that people here did not like to be made to wait in the cold. She sat in the cab with the door open, her hands on the wheel, her face gray under the pale sky.

“Mr. Thorne,” she said.

“Sheriff.”

“We found a body.”

It was not a sentence that invited small talk.

I looked past her. Her truck’s headlights threw a white cone over the snow, and beyond it I could see the dark mouth of the tunnel at the end of the old switchyard. It had been closed for years. The tunnel was short, only two hundred yards through the rock, and it had a bad name because of the way the wind came through it in winter, sounding like a whistle no one had blown.

“Who?” I asked.

She hesitated. “A woman. In the tunnel.”

I reached for my coat. “When?”

“About an hour ago.”

“By whom?”

“A hiker. He said he heard something moving in the snow. He found her near the tunnel mouth, half buried. She was dead before he could do anything.”

I pulled on the coat and followed her down the path. The air bit at my nose. My boots made small cracks in the snow. I walked the old route with the same patience I had once brought to a crowded platform: eyes forward, shoulders steady, no wasted motion. The mountain had taught me that if you respected the ground, the ground rarely fought back. But I was not a boy who had learned it from a timetable. I had learned it from the slow death of a railway that had once been proud.

The Blackpine Branch had been a narrow-gauge line, a stubborn thread of iron through a valley that never asked to be served. The mountain town needed it more than the valley did, because the valley was wide and forgiving, while Blackpine was narrow and unforgiving. We carried timber out, mail in, school children, the occasional cow, and once a man named Albert Prewitt, who had been carrying a coffin of his own mother in a box on the rear car because he could not afford a proper hearse. We carried funerals and freight and the small, stubborn hopes of people who did not want to leave.

The line closed in the winter after the flood. Not all at once. First the cars were gone. Then the signal. Then the little wooden office where we kept the schedules. At last, the rails were pulled up piece by piece and sold to the county for the school yard fence. Only the tunnel remained, too expensive to fill, too ugly to remove, and too close to the old depot to forget.

I stopped beside the snow.

Sheriff Danner had a cone of yellow light aimed at the tunnel entrance. The beam was not enough to show everything, only enough to show what she wanted it to show. A shape lay in the white, half covered by drifts. The body had been positioned at an angle, as if it had fallen or been placed. The face was turned toward the rock, and the snow had settled on it in a soft mask.

I knelt beside the edge of the light without waiting.

The woman’s coat was dark green, the kind worn in cities, not here. The fabric was stiff with ice. Her boots were brown leather, laced high, and the soles were worn flat in the places where a woman who walked with confidence had once stood straight. Her hair was dark and matted with snow. Her fingers were tucked beneath her chest, and in one hand she held a small brass object.

I did not touch it yet.

“Who is she?” Danner asked.

I looked at the coat, the boots, the way the body had been laid out. “Not a tourist.”

“Why not?”

“Tourists walk with their eyes on the camera. This woman’s boots are old, and she was carrying something.” I pointed to a leather strap hanging from the coat pocket. “She had a bag. A real one.”

Danner nodded slowly. “The hiker says she was alive when he found her, or at least moving. He said her eyes were open. Then she went still.”

I closed my eyes for a moment. In the dark behind my lids, I heard the old signal bell, the sharp ring it used to give when a train was about to leave the station. The mountain had a way of bringing back the sound even when no train came.

“Let me see her hands.”

Danner did not look pleased. “I’m the one in charge here.”

“You are,” I said. “But if this is what I think it is, you’ll need someone who knows what to look for.”

She stepped aside.

I put my bare finger near the wrist to feel for cold. The woman’s skin was stiff and blue at the edges. She had been dead some time. I looked at the brass object again. It was small, oval-shaped, and warm under the light. I reached out and picked it up.

It was a conductor’s token.

I knew it before I turned it over. It was older than the present branch, older than most of the men who had worked the line. The metal was worn smooth, and the face showed the profile of a mountain goat with a number stamped beneath it. I had seen one of these on my grandfather’s belt before I could read, and I had carried one myself for twenty years.

On the back, in faded letters, was the number 047.

I turned it in my hand.

Danner leaned closer. “What is it?”

I looked up. “A token. For the old branch.”

“Old how?”

“Before the branch became what it is.”

She stared at me. “You’re saying she has a token from a train that stopped running decades ago.”

“I’m saying she has a token that should not exist.”

That was not the whole of it. The token had been recently polished. There was a faint smell on the metal, not oil or rust, but something chemical, like the solvent used to clean old brass. Someone had cleaned it on purpose.

Danner’s face tightened. “Do you know where that comes from?”

“I do.”

“Then why didn’t you say so?”

“Because I wanted to be sure.”

I turned the token over again. On the underside, almost invisible, was a tiny scratch, as if someone had tried to erase a word. I touched the edge with my thumb.

There had been an initial.

I recognized it.

It was my initial.

Danner saw the change in my face. “Elias.”

I set the token in her palm and closed her fingers over it. “You’ll need to bag this,” I said. “And the boots. And that coat.”

“Whose initial?” she asked.

I did not answer at once. My mind was moving along the line the way it once had: from the tunnel to the depot, from the depot to the station house, from the station house to the last man who had ever seen the old token on a living man’s belt.

Myself.

The last man.

I looked at the dead woman again. Her lips were parted, and beneath the snow around her cheek there was a smear of something dark, dried to the color of old rust. It was not enough to say it was blood, but it was enough to make me think of the old accident in the tunnel, the one they never finished explaining.

“How long since you last saw a live conductor’s token?” Danner asked.

“Long enough.”

“Then you’ll tell me what this is.”

I stood, my knees stiff from the cold. The snow was falling harder now, and the wind had begun to push through the pines with a sound like a slow whistle. The tunnel mouth opened before us, dark and patient.

I thought of the last time I had carried a token. It had been the morning I retired. The branch had not yet closed, but it was so empty that we ran one train a day in the cold and two in the summer, just to keep the line from being forgotten. I had walked the platform slowly, as if I were checking for a train that no longer mattered. There had been no passengers. There had been only the old depot, the signal post, and the snow on the rails.

I had put the token in my pocket and left it there.

Danner followed me as I looked up the slope. “The hiker says he saw footprints leading out of the tunnel,” she said. “But not one set. Two.”

I stopped. “How far apart?”

“Three or four paces.”

“That would mean two people,” I said. “Or one person dragging something.”

She looked at me. “Which is it?”

I looked at the body again. The woman had been placed, not thrown. Her body was arranged as if by someone who knew the tunnel and had no intention of being seen. The snow around her was undisturbed, except for a narrow path of broken crust leading back into the dark. It had been covered and then uncovered, or perhaps never filled in at all.

“It could be either,” I said.

Danner’s hand went to her radio. “We’re calling this in.”

“Call it in. But keep it quiet.”

She looked at me. “You don’t like this.”

I almost smiled. “I don’t like any of this. I don’t like the body, I don’t like the token, and I don’t like the way the snow has settled on the face as if it were waiting to be shown.”

“What’s that mean?”

“It means someone knew where this tunnel was. Someone knew it would be quiet. Someone knew the wind would carry a whistle and make a man hear what he wants to hear.”

Danner’s jaw tightened. “You’ve been on this line too long, Elias.”

“No,” I said. “I’ve been on it too long, and now I’m off it.”

The wind moved through the pines. For a second, the sound was not a complaint. It was a note, low and long, drawn out from the dark like breath through a reed.

I turned back to the tunnel.

“Open it,” I said.

She frowned. “Open what?”

“The tunnel. From the other end. If someone placed her here, there will be tracks on the rock face where they moved. If they came from the valley, the snow on the outside will be broken. If they came from the switchyard, the old rail bed will tell us.”

Danner looked at me for a long moment, then nodded. “I’ll get some men.”

“No,” I said. “Don’t get too many.”

She stared at me. “That’s not your decision.”

“It’s yours,” I said. “But I’m the only one who knows this line well enough to read it.”

She hesitated, then pulled out her radio. “Control, this is Danner. Send two officers and a medical examiner to the old tunnel at Blackpine. Keep it under seal.” She paused. “And pull back the perimeter. I need access to the switchyard.”

The radio crackled. “Copy that, Sheriff. We’ll send a truck.”

She switched off the radio. “You really think this is a railway thing.”

I looked at the token in her hand. “I think it’s a message.”

“What message?”

I did not answer. The wind was louder now, and the snow was beginning to blur the line between the trees and the dark. I walked to the tunnel mouth and put my hand on the stone. It was cold enough to sting. I could feel the roughness of the rock, the old iron brackets set into the wall where the tunnel had once carried more than a single body and a hiker’s story.

Behind me, Danner said, “The woman’s coat is too new for this town.”

“Then she came from somewhere else.”

“Yes. But she had your token.”

I turned to look at her. “You’ve got it now. Keep it safe.”

“Elias.”

“I’m not asking you to believe me.”

She held my gaze. “I’m asking you to tell me who else knew about the old branch.”

I looked back into the tunnel.

The wind came through it.

It sounded, if I was honest with myself, exactly like a whistle.

“Everyone who was here when the line closed,” I said.

“That’s a lot of people.”

“It’s enough.”

The first snowflake hit the lantern light and vanished before it could fall. Danner stepped closer, and for the first time since the night had begun, she looked less like a sheriff and more like a woman standing at the edge of something she did not want to walk into.

“Then we’d better start with the depot,” she said.

I nodded. “We’d better.”
