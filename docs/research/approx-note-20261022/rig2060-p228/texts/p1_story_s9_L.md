## final text

**The Light Before the Name**

The lighthouse at Vetch Point did not appear in old postcards. It was too low for the romantic imagination, a squat stone tower with a bent lantern room, and the island itself was a narrow spit of rock that the sea chewed every winter. Still, the light worked. Ships that lost confidence in the charts turned their bows toward its slow wheel of white.

Ewan was the last keeper. He had served there for nineteen years, and in those years he had learned the tower better than he had learned his own family. He knew which brick letched water in March, which step sang underfoot when the wind came from the east, and which of the glass panels in the lantern room had a hairline crack that only showed itself at sunset. He knew the smell of the oil, the taste of the salt in the air when a gale was still days away, the way the fog bell sounded different when it was tired.

At fifty-six, he was no longer supposed to be there. The board had long since decided the light could be automatic. The automatic equipment had arrived in a crate with a modern clockwork and a small electronic timer, but it kept failing. Perhaps the sea air was too honest for it, perhaps the island had simply chosen its keeper. Whatever the reason, Ewan was still climbing the stairs at dusk, still turning the great lens with his gloved hands, still keeping the old logbook where the ink had begun to fade.

The first letter came in November.

It arrived with the ferry, folded inside the usual parcel of candles, oil, and a single newspaper that had been damp at the edges. Ewan recognized the envelope by its color before he saw the postmark. It was blue, not the blue of the sea, but a pale, dry blue, like sky after snow. There was no return address. There was only a name written in a small, neat hand.

Maren.

He opened it in the kitchen, where the stove ticked and the window looked out over the landing. The paper inside was thin and soft, almost like cloth. The writing was clear but uncertain, as if the writer had been taught only recently to trust a pen.

*Dear Keeper,*

*I am not yet here. My name is Maren. I am writing from a room that has not yet been made for me. I do not know if you will believe me, but I know you are there. I can hear the bell through the letter. I do not know how. Please do not be afraid. I am coming to the light.*

Ewan read it twice. Then he read it once more, slower, as if the words might change when he was not looking.

He looked at the window. The landing was empty. The sea was grey and patient.

He put the letter in his coat pocket and climbed the tower.

For three days he waited. Nothing came. He told himself it was a joke, a piece of paper sent by someone who had read the old stories about Vetch Point. There had been pranks before: a bottle with a dead crab, a postcard from a man who claimed to be the lighthouse’s ghost, a child’s drawing of a lighthouse with too many windows. None of it had stayed.

On the fourth morning, another blue envelope appeared on the landing.

This one had a stamp. It showed a lighthouse, but the light was not shining outward; it was shining down into a small circle of sand. Below it, in letters no larger than a fingernail, was written: *To be born.*

Inside, the letter said:

*Dear Keeper,*

*My mother has not yet come to Vetch Point. She will come when the sea is low and the wind is from the north. She is afraid of the dark, but she is not afraid of the light. I know this because I have seen her face in the dream where she is not yet me. I am not yet me yet.*

*Please leave the door unlocked. Please boil water. Please do not tell anyone about this letter.*

Ewan stood in the kitchen holding the paper. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had spent his life with steady hands. The lamp had to be kept clean, the wick trimmed, the glass wiped without a single scratch. He had never been given a reason not to be steady.

That night, when the wind came from the north, he boiled water.

He did not know why. He did not tell himself a reason. He only put the kettle on, and while it filled and rattled, he walked to the door and set the latch back. The island was quiet except for the lamp’s turning and the low sound of water against rock. He looked out toward the sea for a long time, and though nothing came, he did not feel foolish.

The next week brought three more letters.

They were shorter than the first, more careful. Maren wrote of a mother who sang to herself while she mended nets. She wrote of a father who was not there yet. She wrote of a ship that would pass by at night and a child on the deck who would point at the lighthouse and ask if that was the place where people waited. Ewan read each letter by the lamp, and after a while the words began to settle into the sound of the tower, as if they had always been part of it.

One evening he decided to answer.

There was no address. He wrote only:

*To the keeper of the light not yet born.*

He sealed the letter in an old tin and tied it to a buoy at low tide. It seemed ridiculous. He stood on the landing and watched the ferry carry the tin to the mainland. He expected it to be lost. He expected it to be read by a clerk and returned to the post office as undeliverable. He did not expect the reply.

It came with the next ferry.

*Dear Keeper,*

*You have sent me back a question I have not yet learned to ask. I am glad you asked. I think it will help me remember you.*

That night, Ewan climbed the tower and stood in the lantern room for a long while. The lens turned, throwing its white circle across the water. For the first time in years, he did not feel the usual ache of duty. He felt something lighter, something like the first warmth in a room after the door has been closed too long.

He began to prepare the cottage.

It was not a large house. There were two rooms, a small kitchen, a landing with a narrow staircase, and a back door that looked out over the grass. The walls were whitewashed and stained at the corners with age. The bed had been his for twenty years, the same narrow frame he had carried up from the mainland before his wife died. He had never used the second room. It had a small window and a table near the wall.

Now he swept it. He washed the window until the glass rang clean. He moved the table closer to the center and placed a folded cloth on the chair beside it. He put out a bowl of water and a small dish of sugar, as if some small animal might come to the door, though there was no animal, only the sea.

When the first frost came, he painted the back door blue.

The color was not like the envelope, but it was close enough. It made the old door look younger. It made the cottage look as though someone had recently been waiting for a visitor.

The letters slowed after that. For a whole month, no blue envelope came. Ewan went through the routine: oil, light, log, bell, meal, sleep. He told himself the silence was natural. He told himself a great deal.

Then one morning the ferryman did not stop.

The sea was high and grey, and the wind came in from the east with a low, continuous moan. The ferry did not come. The island was cut off. Ewan checked the radio, but the static was thick with distance. He went to the landing and stood there until his boots filled with salt water.

By noon, the fog had come in from the west.

It was not a gentle fog. It rolled in like a slow animal, erasing the rocks, the grass, the ferry landing, the sea itself. Ewan climbed the tower and lit the lamp early. The beam swung out into the white and could not find a thing. He kept the log, though there was little to write.

*Wind north-east. Fog. Light good.*

He thought of Maren.

He thought of the letter that had said, *My mother has not yet come.* He thought of the mother who was afraid of the dark but not of the light. He thought of the door he had painted blue.

When the fog finally thinned in the early afternoon, there was a small boat beyond the usual breaking of the rocks. It was too late for the ferry. It was too small for a ship. It was a rowboat, black and battered, pulling itself toward the landing with the patience of something that had already decided not to sink.

Ewan went down to the landing.

The woman in the boat was young, perhaps twenty-five, with her hair bound tight under a grey hood. She had one hand on the oar and the other on her stomach. She looked at the tower first, then at him.

“I can’t see the rocks,” she said. “The light was the only thing I could find.”

Ewan helped her tie the boat to the iron ring. He carried her inside. Her face was pale, but her eyes were steady. She sat by the stove and wrapped both arms around herself.

“Where is the doctor?” she asked.

There was no doctor on the island. The nearest village was an hour by road, two hours by sea in better weather.

“I know,” Ewan said.

He boiled water again.

This time he did not wonder why.

The hours passed. The fog lifted by nightfall, and the stars came out over the sea as if the water had been wiped clean. Ewan kept the light turning, but he was no longer thinking of the ships. He was thinking of the small room with the blue door, of the letters in his pocket, of the tin tied to the buoy and lost in the current.

At last the woman cried out, and then she was quiet again, and then Ewan heard something small and sharp and alive.

He stood in the doorway of the room for a moment before he went in.

The baby was wrapped in a clean cloth. The mother was asleep with her head against the chair. The child’s face was red, and its mouth was open, and its hands were closed into fists as if it had already begun to hold things.

Ewan looked at the baby.

He did not know the name. He did not know the mother. He did not know what had happened between this island and the woman’s life. And yet, when he looked at the small hand curled near the child’s cheek, he felt the same strange certainty he had felt the first time he read the blue envelope.

He bent down and lifted the child carefully. The baby stopped crying and looked up at him with eyes that were not quite open. The lighthouse beam turned outside, and for a moment the light came across the floor and touched the baby’s face.

Ewan set the child on the table beside the folded cloth.

He opened the small room’s door a little wider. He placed the blue cloth from the chair across the window. He stood there a long time, watching the sea and the tower and the light, until the baby made a sound like the first syllable of a word.

In the morning, the ferry came.

The woman was gone, or would be gone soon, with the sea and the road and the life she had brought back from the island. Ewan stood on the landing and watched the boat leave. He did not feel the old loneliness after it passed. He only felt the island settle again under him, as if it had been waiting for him to stop holding its breath.

That night, he climbed the tower one last time before dark. The lamp was clean. The lens was bright. The logbook was open on the small table.

He picked up a blue envelope from the landing. He had not seen it before. It lay there in the salt wind, pale against the grey stone.

He opened it.

Inside was a single line.

*Thank you for keeping the light until I could arrive.*

Ewan folded the letter and put it in his pocket. Below him, the sea moved in the dark, and the light turned, and somewhere beyond the rocks a life that had not existed that morning began, at last, to exist.
