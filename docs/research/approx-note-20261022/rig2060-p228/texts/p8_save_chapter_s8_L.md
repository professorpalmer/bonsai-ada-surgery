# Chapter One: The Leaning

The city of Keshara woke when the shell woke.

No bells. No roosters. A sound you felt before you heard — a low, rolling groan that started under the soles of your feet and climbed through your spine, as if the earth itself were turning in sleep. The ground leaned. For the first three minutes of every morning, Keshara tilted eastward, gutters ran backward, and every cat on the rim district yowled into the sky.

The turtle — Orath, the old maps called her, though the maps only showed a sliver of a back and a note in the margin reading *too big to be true* — stretched.

Keshara was built on Orath's back.

Not "built near." Not "built by." Built upon, the way a barnacle is built upon a hull, except that the barnacle is the whole city. Eighty thousand people lived on the plates of Orath's shell, on the ribs between the plates, on the spines along the ridge. They had anchored themselves to a creature that crossed an ocean so wide the cartographers called it "The Long" and stopped, because they ran out of coast.

For as long as anyone could remember, Orath swam east.

Maren lay awake and watched the lean. Her window faced the eastern edge of the city, where the shell fell away into a slope of barnacle-crusted stone and dropped into the sea — the Drop, the people called it, the part of the world where your house ended and the water began, where the air tasted of brine and the fog came in at night with something in it that the sailors would not name.

Below the edge, the sea was the color of wet slate, and it moved.

Not the way water moves when a wind crosses it. It moved the way the ground moves when something enormous has just passed beneath it. The sea heaved in slow waves, and far off, something dark and the size of a cathedral wall rose and sank. Rose. Sank.

"Another turn," Maren muttered, and got out of bed.

The lean was still on when she reached the stair. Her mother's hand was on the doorframe, a small, white-knuckled grip that said *this is how we've always been*. Maren's mother was a plate-mender, one of the women who climbed the ribs of the shell each night with a chisel and a pot of resin, sealing the hairline cracks that opened when Orath rolled. Cracks that no one could fully explain. Cracks that, according to the keepers of the Long Ledger, had been getting longer every year.

"Down, then?" her mother asked, without looking up.

"I've got the first climb."

The first climb was a climber's work, not a message-runner's, but Maren had been running messages along the shell's ridge since she was eleven — small, flat things, the kind the city needed to say things to itself. The tide-tables. The warning-chords. The names of the dead. Today she was climbing to the head-post.

She had to be at the head-post by the time Orath's morning stretch finished. That was the rule. The stretch lasted about ten minutes, and during those ten minutes, every plank in Keshara was a different angle. The message-chains had to be reset by hand, or the messages that came down the long slope would slide off the edge into the Drop and be lost to the sea. Maren had never been late. Maren had never missed.

The head-post was a pillar of iron and rope, bolted to the ridge three hundred steps up, and when the stretch hit, it sang. Not the metal — the rope. A long, drawn, singing note that the sailors said was the turtle's voice, though Maren had a different theory, and it was the kind of theory you didn't say out loud.

She climbed.

The shell was warm under her boots. Warm in a way stone doesn't get warm. Warm in a way that meant something below was alive.

Halfway up, she stopped and looked down.

Keshara was waking up. Smoke came out of ten thousand chimneys. The market on the northern plate was already moving — carts on rails that ran along the ridges, the sound of the ropes being hauled. And they were moving.

Not drifting. Moving.

The whole raft district, two hundred boats and maybe four thousand people, was sliding south along the sea. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a hand slides across a table.

And it was looking up.

Orath was afraid.

Maren started climbing faster.

The head-post was singing. The rope was singing. And somewhere below, in the water, something vast opened its mouth.
