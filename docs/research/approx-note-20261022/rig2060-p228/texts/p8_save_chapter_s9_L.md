# Chapter 1: The Weight of the Sky

The first thing you learn as a child in Ossuary is that the world is not solid.

Before the stones, before the bread, before a person can even form a sentence that isn't made of "stop" and "look," every child is set on the balcony of their tower at the edge of the city, feet dangling over the rim, while a mother or father whispers the old promise: *the land is only a roof. The ocean is beneath the roof. And beneath the ocean, something is sleeping.*

By the time the telling is finished, the child has stopped looking down. That is the point of it. That is the whole point.

The city of Ossuary sprawls across the broad, domed back of the great turtle as a vine strangles a tree. Towers of pale shell-stone rise from the ridges along its spine, their spires wrapped in copper that had long since gone green with age and salt air. Bridges of woven kelp-rope and iron cable swung over the hollows between the bones that still jutted through the crust where the turtle's ribs met the shell. In the lower quarters, where the streets ran narrow and the light never quite reached the floor, the air tasted of brine and rendering fat, and the people kept their lamps low, for they were told never to shine too brightly against the underside of the world.

Seren Kave had been told this a thousand times, and had begun, somewhere around the age of ten, to think it was a lie that no one could afford to be right about.

She worked in the Bellwrights' Row, hammering the great bronze bells that hung from the ridge-bones and rang at the turn of the tide. Three strikes meant the water was rising. Four, the current had shifted. Five—

No one would say what five meant. The bells had never rung five times in a hundred years, and the bellwrights who had been old enough to remember the old wars were dead, and their stories with them.

The morning Seren was brought to the Council Hall, the tide was turning, and the whole city swayed with it. That was the thing about Ossuary that the travelers' songs never got right: it was not still. Nothing in it was still. The city breathed with the creature beneath it, rising and sinking in slow swells that could be felt in your teeth, in the fillings of old men, in the deep hum the children learned to hum back to sleep with. The walls flexed. The foundations groaned. Every year the tide-wardens measured the drift with lines of chalk and chalk and chalk, and every year the city slid a little further north, toward the cold water, toward the open sea, as if the turtle were walking and would not stop.

Seren climbed the spiral stair of the Hall with her hammer still in her belt, because she had not yet earned the right to set it down. The Council sat in a chamber carved into the curve of a rib, and they did not sit so much as balance, their chairs bolted to the bone, their chins drawn up against the gentle, never-ending tilt. The High Warden, a woman whose face was a map of old scars and older decisions, did not look up from the scroll before her when Seren entered.

"Three bells," the Warden said.

"I heard them from the Row."

"Then you heard them." She finally looked up. "The current has taken the southern moorings. Two ketches lost. The deep-water bells are silent."

Seren's stomach dropped. Not the five. The *silence* of five. That was the part they never spoke of, the thing the old bellwrights had kept behind their teeth like a coin. When the deep bells fell silent, it meant the water below was no longer moving as it had always moved, which meant something had come up under the turtle, or gone down into the deep, or both.

"It is the tide," said the eldest of the Council, a man whose name was older than his language. "The moon pulls the water. The water pulls the city. This is natural. This is all."

"This is not all," Seren said, and was surprised to hear her own voice. It sounded strange in the rib-bone chamber, a small and hard thing in a vast and old place. "The tide does not take moorings. The tide does not go quiet."

The silence that followed was not the silence of five bells. It was the silence of the city itself, holding its breath.

The Warden looked at her for a long time. Then she reached into her robes and took out a small brass key, worn smooth by hands that had held it for a century, perhaps two. She set it on the table between them.

"There is a place under Ossuary," the Warden said quietly, "where the turtle's heart still beats. It was built by the first founders, in a time when we did not yet know the creature could be spoken to. You will go down there. You will find the heart. And you will do what it needs."

"And if I refuse?"

The Warden smiled, and there was nothing cruel in it, which was somehow worse. "Then the city will keep walking, and the sea will keep rising, and one morning a child will look over the edge of a balcony and find that the land beneath it has run out. I would rather you did not look."

Seren took the key. It was warm, as if it had been holding a secret so long it had grown hot.

Outside the Hall's window, the whole vast city swayed once, slowly, like a ship in a heavy sea. Far below, where the light went black and the streets ended in the dark, something enormous turned its face toward the sky.

It had not moved in a hundred years.

It had moved.
