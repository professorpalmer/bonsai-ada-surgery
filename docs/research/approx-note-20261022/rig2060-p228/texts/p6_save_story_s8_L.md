# The Recipe for the Meridian

The galley of the *Meridian* was the only place on the ship where the air did not taste of itself.

Every other deck carried the same recycled breath: the faint metallic taste of the oxygen scrubbers, the chemical sweetness of the hydroponic mist. But every morning at 0600, when Yara opened the galley door, a different air came out — warm, flour-dusted, sour with rising yeast. For four thousand and twelve souls, that smell was the smell of home, and home was a word that, three hundred and sixty years ago, had meant a planet.

Yara was the ship's cook. The job had no rank in the manifest and no title in any of the histories. It was listed only as *galley*, a long room behind the central hydroponics bay, with the largest single responsibility aboard. The *Meridian* had not left Earth carrying a single meal. It had left carrying seeds, and a small archive of recipes, and the knowledge that everything else had to be grown, cultured, or coaxed from the walls. The wheat under the UV lamps in the south bay, the soy protein that fooled the tongue well enough to pass for meat, the algae butter, the salt harvested from the condensation tanks — all of it fell to the cook to turn into something people would actually want to eat.

Pleasure was the hard part.

"Stop hovering."

Yara turned. Ilya stood in the doorway, and for once there was no flour on her.

"I'm not hovering," Yara said. "I'm reading the manifest."

"Which one? The one that says you're supposed to be in the mess hall at 1000 for the anniversary meal?" Ilya leaned against the doorframe. "Or the one that says the bread isn't going to rise?"

Yara's jaw tightened. "I've been cooking for thirty-two years."

"And for thirty-one of those years the anniversary loaf has come out right," Ilya said, and her voice had gone soft in the way it did when she had the argument fully prepared. "The whole ship knows the recipe. Everyone remembers the taste. Every single one of them, all the way up to Aunty Reyes, who is one hundred and twenty-one years old and remembers tasting it at her first anniversary dinner. And yet you can't tell me what's in it."

Silence. In the bowl before Yara sat a pale, flat disc of dough that had not risen at all.

"Tell me," Ilya said. "What's the recipe? The real one."

Yara turned back to the bowl. "You know what the recipe is."

"I know what's on the list. Flour, salt, water, algae butter, and the starter we've kept alive since the first year. That's all. Three versions of the list, Yara. Three cooks, three years apart, and none of them work. I've mixed every one of them. None of them rise."

That was the truth, and Yara had never said it aloud.

The archive held what most things on the *Meridian* held: fragments. The original document had been damaged in the turbulence of launch, or so the histories claimed. What survived was a set of three handwritten recipes, each a little different from the last. For three centuries and a half, cooks had done the only sensible thing. They had tasted the results, adjusted, and written down what had worked. But what worked changed from one cook to the next, because the yeast changed, and the humidity, and the wheat from the south bay. The true recipe migrated through the line of cooks like a story passed from mouth to mouth, each telling a little different.

Yara had inherited the problem the winter her mother died, taken by the lung disease that moves quietly through the lower decks. She had inherited the galley, the archive, and the fact that she had never actually known the recipe. No cook, in three hundred and sixty years, ever had.

For most of that time, it had not mattered. No one on the *Meridian* could compare one loaf to another. There was no "the way it was before." There was only the bread, and the meal, and the evening in the mess hall where four thousand people sat down together and said, with their chewing and their laughter and the clatter of dishes, *we are here; the ship is going; the future is still being made*.

But now Ilya was watching her, and the dough had not risen, and Yara felt the deck tilt beneath her.

"If I tell you," she said at last, "I can only make it with you. Together. In front of everyone."

Ilya's face changed. "That's the old story, isn't it? The one about the first cook, who had to bake with a child so no one would notice the difference from year to year."

"Someone told it. Maybe I did, a long time ago, when I needed a better answer."

Ilya rolled up her sleeves. "Then make it."

They made it. They argued about the salt, the water, the temperature of the oven. Yara pressed her hand into the dough and felt it answer — not obediently, but warmly, the way a living thing answers. The first loaf, from the oldest recipe in the archive, rose only a little. The second, from the newest, rose too much, and its crumb came out gummy and close.

The third time, Yara put the archive away.

She closed her eyes and did what she had been doing, without knowing it, for thirty-two years. She reached for the memory of a meal. Not a kitchen. A mess hall. An anniversary evening. The smell of the bread coming out of the oven. A small old man at the end of the table asking, for the hundredth time in his life, for one more slice. Four thousand people breathing and laughing and alive.

She added a little more salt. A little less butter. The dough met her hands and held.

It rose.

They baked it that night, and in the morning they carried it up to the mess hall in the silver trays, and the galley's smell followed them down the corridor like a banner.

The anniversary meal began at noon, as it always did, with the reading of the first year's logs — a list of names, a launch, a sky full of stars going black. When Yara broke the loaf, the mess hall went quiet in the particular way that only four thousand people can be quiet, which is to say: all at once, and gently, like water filling a room.

The old man from the end of the table came forward first. He took his slice, ate it slowly, and set it down.

"It's not the first year's bread," he said.

Yara's stomach fell.

The old man smiled at her. It was a small, sad, entirely kind smile.

"The first year's bread was never recorded," he said. "The first cook was a woman from a city on the sea, and she made the loaf from a recipe her mother had taught her, which the mother had learned in a kitchen that was never written down. She remembered it the way all of us remember anything — wrong, and beautifully, and completely. The first year's bread was that. And every year after, someone else remembered it a little differently. That was never a flaw, child. That was the recipe."

Ilya stood very still behind Yara, and Yara felt, for the first time in thirty-two years, the shape of it. Continuity was not in the ingredients. It had never been in the ingredients. It was in the making. It was in the bread rising in a dark bay on a machine that would outlast the sun that had launched it. It was in the argument about the salt, and the hand in the dough, and the old man's question for the second slice.

That evening, after the trays were empty and the mess hall was swept and the four thousand souls were drifting off to sleep on their decks, Yara went back to the galley. She took a clean sheet of paper from the archive — not the archive of recipes, but the archive of *her* records, the one no one had ever opened because it was only her work log.

She wrote down what they had done. Proportions, times, the exact way the dough should feel when it was ready. Then, where the older versions had carried the dates of three anonymous cooks, she wrote:

*Yara and Ilya. Year three hundred and sixty-two.*
*Version one of ours.*
*It is not the only version. It is ours, and that is enough.*

She pinned it to the wall above the mixing bench, next to the three old pages and the small tin of vanilla that no one had used in four generations because no one had thought to taste it.

In the morning the galley door opened, and the smell of yeast went out into the corridor, and down the ship, in every deck and every dormitory and every small room where a family slept together in a narrow line of bunks, four thousand people woke to a smell that was not a memory of a planet.

It was a promise, kept again, in a different hand.

And the *Meridian* went on, turning slowly through the dark, carrying its wheat and its salt and its small, warm, impossible bread toward a sky it would never see.
