The Last Loaf

Mara Voss had been cooking in the galley of the Ark Carrier Persephone for eleven years. Her kitchen was the size of a city block, and it smelled of flour and the green, wet sweetness of the hydroponic bays beyond the window. Outside that window there were no fields, no sky, no rain. There was only the ship: the ship was all there was, and the ship was 217 years out of Earth.

Every morning at 0600, Mara pulled the bread from the ovens. The Persephone's bread was, by the ship's standards, respectable: a uniform golden brown, soft inside, holding the shape of the tray. It was also, if you thought about it for a long time, the taste of nothing. For three generations, the bread had been that way, and no one had noticed. The ship had enough people to eat, and the ship had always had enough food. That was what the engineers promised.

But Mara had noticed.

It was the starter.

The Persephone's bread culture had been brought aboard in a small clay pot, wrapped in cloth, by a woman whose name the ship's records listed only as "First Cook, Okafor." For twenty-one centuries — no, for 217 years — that pot had been passed from one cook to the next. Each cook was expected to feed the culture, watch it rise, and keep it alive. That was the job. No one had written down why, because everyone assumed everyone else knew.

Mara did not know.

She had inherited the pot from the previous cook, a man named Delgado, who had died two winters ago with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he would never have finished. She had not even asked what was in the pot. It looked like nothing. It was gray, lumpy, and smelled faintly of a sour apple. She had been feeding it rice water, because the ship's manual said "feed the culture with any liquid, any starch, daily."

For two years, the culture had been slowly dying.

Mara did not know that, either. What she knew was that the bread had gone gray. What she knew was that the children's quarters had begun to ask, in the way children ask, for "something real." What she knew was that she could not explain why.

It was on a Tuesday that the truth came. She was scrubbing the floor at 0300, in the dark, with the hum of the recyclers as the only sound, when she found the logbook.

It was tucked behind the culture pot, in the shadow of the shelf where Delgado had kept his tools. It was small, bound in cloth that had gone the color of weak tea. The cover was handwritten, the ink faded to the brown of old blood.

*For the next cook who cannot remember why they are here.*

Mara opened it.

The first page was a list. Flour. Water. Salt. A pinch of sugar. And a number that was not a number but a date: 4th of March, year one.

*First bread,* it said. *Made in the galley of the Persephone, with flour from the first hydroponic bay, water from the ship's recycling, and the only thing I was not able to bring: the taste of rain. The culture responded. It rose. I think it knows what it is for.*

The rest of the book was a log. Two hundred and seventeen entries, one per year, written in handwriting that grew stranger as the centuries passed. Some entries were short: *Culture is good today. Bread is good. Children are eating.* Some were long: *The flour is thin this year. I made a flatbread instead. No one complained. I think they preferred it.*

The last entry, in handwriting so small Mara had to bring it close to the light, read:

*It is dying. I can feel it in the way it smells. I have been feeding it wrong, or maybe it is simply tired. I do not know how to help it. I am sorry. I leave it in the pot. I leave this book. If you are reading this, the culture is still alive. Feed it well. Remember the rain.*

Mara closed the book. She stood there for a long time, in the dark galley, with the pot at her feet and the hum of the recyclers in her ears.

She thought about the gray bread. She thought about the children's quarters. She thought about Okafor, whoever Okafor was, a woman who had stood in this room 217 years ago and put a handful of flour in a pot and decided that the ship would not be a tomb.

At 0400, Mara did something that was not in the manual.

She fed the culture.

But not with rice water. She took flour from the good bin — the fine, pale flour that came from the wheat that had been grown on the Persephone since the first year — and mixed it with warm water and a pinch of the salt that the ship's chemist kept in the galley. She mixed it slowly, with her hands, the way Okafor's logbook said she had done.

And then she did the thing that was not in any manual.

She waited.

She waited through the night. She waited through the morning. She watched the pot. She talked to it, because she did not know what else to do, and because a woman who talks to something while she feeds it is, in her own mind, a cook.

At 0600, the culture was different.

It was no longer gray and lumpy. It was a pale gold, and it had a surface that trembled, and it smelled — Mara would later describe this to the medic as "the smell of a place you have never been but miss." It had a smell. It had a life in it.

She baked.

It was a small loaf, nothing special in shape, nothing special in size. It went into the oven at 0800 and came out at 0900. It was brown, not gray. It was soft, but it held. And when Mara broke it open, the inside was pale and airy, full of holes, and the smell that rose from it filled the galley so completely that two of the junior cooks stopped what they were doing and simply stood still.

Mara carried it to the mess hall.

The mess hall was the largest room on the Persephone, and at midday it was always full. One thousand, two hundred people, sitting at long tables, eating the same gray bread, the same green stew, the same pale fruit. The lights were the lights of a ship that had been flying for 217 years and would fly for many more.

Mara set the loaf on the table in the center of the hall.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. The smell did it for her.

People turned. People stopped chewing. A woman at the far end of the hall — Mara would later learn her name was Anand, a structural engineer who had not spoken to her husband in six months — put down her spoon. An old man near the service door, a man who had been alive when the Persephone still had a name that was not its number, put his hand on his chest and said, very quietly, "That is the smell of the first year."

Mara cut the loaf.

She cut it thin, because she had read in the logbook that Okafor had done so, because thin bread was shared bread. She passed the plates down the tables, and people ate, and the hall went quiet in the way that only a hall full of 1,200 people can go quiet, and then —

Then a child, a small girl at the third table from the service door, looked up from her bread and said to no one in particular, "It tastes like my grandmother's house."

Mara stopped.

No grandmother. No house. No house had ever been built on the Persephone. No grandmother had ever lived in one. The child had never seen a house, or a grandmother, or a rain. And yet.

The hall was quiet again. The child looked at her grandmother — a real grandmother, a woman in her seventies who sat at the end of the table with her hands folded — and said, "It tastes like hers."

The grandmother put her hand on the child's head. She did not say anything. But her eyes were wet, and her hands were trembling, and she ate her bread very slowly, the way people eat things they are afraid to finish.

Mara stood in the center of the hall, with the knife in her hand and the knife mark on the table, and she understood, for the first time in eleven years, what she had been doing.

She was not feeding 11,400 passengers. She was feeding a memory that had no source. She was keeping alive a thread that stretched back 217 years to a woman in a clay pot who had decided, in the dark, that the ship would not be a tomb.

She looked at the logbook on the counter. She looked at the culture pot. She looked at the loaf, half eaten, and the small crumb on the table where the child had set her plate down.

She took a pencil. She wrote on the back of the last page of the logbook:

*Culture is good. Bread is good. The child said it tastes like her grandmother's house. I do not know what her grandmother's house was. I do not need to know. I will feed it well. I will remember the rain.*

She signed her name below it.

Mara Voss. First cook, second generation.

She put the logbook back in the shelf, behind the pot, in the shadow where Delgado had left it. She wiped the counter. She put the knife away.

And then she went to the oven, and she started to make another loaf.

# The Last Loaf

Mara Voss had been cooking in the galley of the Ark Carrier Persephone for eleven years. Her kitchen was the size of a city block, and it smelled of flour and the green, wet sweetness of the hydroponic bays beyond the window. Outside that window there were no fields, no sky, no rain. There was only the ship: the ship was all there was, and the ship was 217 years out of Earth.

Every morning at 0600, Mara pulled the bread from the ovens. The Persephone's bread was, by the ship's standards, respectable: a uniform golden brown, soft inside, holding the shape of the tray. It was also, if you thought about it for a long time, the taste of nothing. For three generations, the bread had been that way, and no one had noticed. The ship had enough people to eat, and the ship had always had enough food. That was what the engineers promised.

But Mara had noticed.

It was the starter.

The Persephone's bread culture had been brought aboard in a small clay pot, wrapped in cloth, by a woman whose name the ship's records listed only as "First Cook, Okafor." For 217 years, that pot had been passed from one cook to the next. Each cook was expected to feed the culture, watch it rise, and keep it alive. That was the job. No one had written down why, because everyone assumed everyone else knew.

Mara did not know.

She had inherited the pot from the previous cook, a man named Delgado, who had died two winters ago with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he would never have finished. She had not even asked what was in the pot. It was gray, lumpy, and smelled faintly of a sour apple. She had been feeding it rice water, because the ship's manual said to feed the culture with any liquid, any starch, daily.

For two years, the culture had been slowly dying.

Mara did not know that, either. What she knew was that the bread had gone gray. What she knew was that the children's quarters had begun to ask, in the way children ask, for "something real." What she knew was that she could not explain why.

It was on a Tuesday that the truth came.

She was scrubbing the floor at 0300, in the dark, with the hum of the recyclers as the only sound, when she found the logbook. It was tucked behind the culture pot, in the shadow of the shelf where Delgado had kept his tools. It was small, bound in cloth that had gone the color of weak tea. The cover was handwritten, the ink faded to the brown of old blood.

*For the next cook who cannot remember why they are here.*

Mara opened it.

The first page was a list. Flour. Water. Salt. A pinch of sugar. And a date: 4th of March, year one.

"First bread," the entry read. "Made in the galley of the Persephone, with flour from the first hydroponic bay, water from the ship's recycling, and the only thing I was not able to bring: the taste of rain. The culture responded. It rose."

The rest of the book was a log. Two hundred and seventeen entries, one per year, in handwriting that grew stranger with each century. Some entries were short: "Culture is good today. Bread is good. Children are eating." Some were long: "The flour is thin this year. I made a flatbread instead. No one complained. I think they preferred it."

The last entry, in handwriting so small she had to lean close, read:

"It is dying. I can feel it in the way it smells. I have been feeding it wrong, or maybe it is simply tired. I do not know how to help it. I am sorry. I leave it in the pot. I leave this book. If you are reading this, the culture is still alive. Feed it well. Remember the rain."

Mara closed the book. She stood there a long time in the dark galley, with the pot at her feet.

She thought about the gray bread. She thought about the children's quarters. She thought about Okafor, whoever Okafor was, a woman who had stood in this room 217 years ago and put a handful of flour in a pot and decided that the ship would not be a tomb.

At 0400, Mara did something that was not in the manual.

She fed the culture.

But not with rice water. She took flour from the good bin, mixed it with warm water and a pinch of salt, and stirred it slowly, with her hands, the way the logbook said Okafor had done it.

And then she did the thing that was not in any manual.

She waited. She waited through the night. She waited through the morning. She watched the pot. She talked to it, because she did not know what else to do, and because a woman who talks to something while she feeds it is, in her own mind, a cook.

At 0600, the culture was different.

It was no longer gray and lumpy. It was a pale gold, and it had a surface that trembled, and it smelled — Mara would later describe this to the medic as "the smell of a place you have never been but miss." It had a smell. It had a life in it.

She baked.

It was a small loaf, nothing special in shape, nothing special in size. It was brown, not gray. It was soft, but it held. And when Mara broke it open, the inside was pale and airy, full of holes, and the smell that rose from it filled the galley so completely that two of the junior cooks stopped what they were doing and simply stood still.

Mara carried it to the mess hall.

The mess hall was the largest room on the Persephone, and at midday it was always full. One thousand, two hundred people, sitting at long tables, eating the same gray bread, the same green stew, the same pale fruit.

Mara set the loaf on the table in the center of the hall. She did not say anything. She did not need to. The smell did it for her.

People turned. People stopped chewing. A woman at the far end of the hall put her spoon down. An old man near the service door, a man who had been alive when the Persephone still had a name that was not its number, put his hand on his chest and said, very quietly, "That is the smell of the first year."

Mara cut the loaf. She cut it thin. Thin bread is shared bread. She passed the plates down the tables, and people ate, and the hall went quiet in the way that only a hall full of 1,200 people can go quiet, and then—

Then a child, a small girl at the third table from the service door, looked up from her bread and said to no one in particular, "It tastes like my grandmother's house."

Mara stopped.

No grandmother. No house. No house had ever been built on the Persephone. The child had never seen a house. Or a grandmother. Or rain.

And yet.

The hall was quiet again. The child looked at her grandmother — a real grandmother, a woman in her seventies who sat at the end of the table with her hands folded — and said, "It tastes like hers."

The grandmother put her hand on the child's head. She did not say anything. But her eyes were wet, and her hands were trembling, and she ate her bread very slowly, the way people eat things they are afraid to finish.

Mara stood in the middle of the hall, knife in hand, and understood, for the first time in eleven years, what she had been doing.

She was not feeding 11,400 passengers. She was feeding a memory that had no source. She was keeping alive a thread that stretched back 217 years to a woman in a clay pot who had decided, in the dark, that the ship would not be a tomb.

She looked at the logbook on the counter. She looked at the culture pot. She looked at the loaf, half eaten, and the small crumb on the table where the child had set her plate down.

She took a pencil. She wrote on the back of the last page of the logbook:

"Culture is good. Bread is good. The child said it tastes like her grandmother's house. I do not know what her grandmother's house was. I do not need to know. I will feed it well. I will remember the rain."

She signed her name below it.

Mara Voss. First cook, second generation.

She put the logbook back on the shelf, behind the pot, in the shadow where Delgado had left it. She wiped the counter. She put the knife away.

And then she went to the oven, and she started to make another loaf.
