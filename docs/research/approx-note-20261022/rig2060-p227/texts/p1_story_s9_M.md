Edric Moss had kept the light for eleven years, and he had learned to read the sea the way a blind man reads a face: by pressure, temperature, and the way it touched him. The lighthouse stood on a black neck of rock, its lamp a white eye that never blinked. Below it, the village kept its own secrets, and above it, the sky kept its own.

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday in March, folded into a square of blue paper with no stamp, no address, and no return. Edric found it beneath the door of his small stone house, where the tide sometimes left pebbles and the postman left only rumors. Edric turned the paper over. There was no signature, only a small mark in the corner like a bird with its wings closed. He laughed once, because the island had taught him to laugh at strange things before they learned to frighten him. Then he looked toward the sea. The sky was the color of a bruise, and the waves were stacking themselves against the rocks with terrible patience.

He was a man of schedules. He cleaned the lamp, polished the brass, wound the clockwork, and logged the weather in a book with cracked leather.
