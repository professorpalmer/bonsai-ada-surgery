# Two Loaves of Salt

Gullport had three hundred and twelve people, a harbor, a fog bank that arrived every October and stayed most of November, and two bakeries set opposite each other on the same straight stretch of the harbor road, separated by a postbox, a bench, and exactly forty-seven paces of salt-worn pavement.

The postbox belonged to no one in particular and collected, every morning at seven, a small war.

Maren Voss's bakery was called Hearth & Salt. It had been a bakery for sixty-one years, and it announced this fact with a hand-painted sign that read *EST. 1963 — SAME HANDS* in letters gone the color of tea. Inside, the walls were the deep red-brown of brick baked past any point of usefulness, and the air had the specific, living smell of a sourdough starter that had been fed daily for four decades. Maren kept the starter in a ceramic crock on a shelf above the counter, where it sat like a pale, breathing thing. She called it Old Gull, and it answered every morning with a soft, wet sigh of bubbles.

Across the forty-seven paces, Silas Crane had opened Crane & Crumb. His shop had no "EST.," because he had not yet been open long enough to claim one. He was thirty-four, he came from a city where bread had a reputation for being difficult and people had reputations for being soft, and he brought with him a laminating table, a comb of cold butter, and a menu that read, in a thin modern font, *Harbor Brioche / Sea Salt Baguette / Fennel & Rye.* The sign was clean. The display case was glass. The loaves sat in rows, all golden, all even, all beautiful, and all — Maren was quite sure, and quite right — slightly too much like bread and not enough like the town.

This was the difference between them, though neither would have admitted it in so plain a word. Maren baked bread the way the harbor changed: slowly, by pressure, with the salt of the water working on everything. Silas baked bread the way a chef works a station: measured, precise, and finished. His loaves were technically excellent. They were also, to Maren, the pastry equivalent of a stranger's handshake — polite, complete, and left in your hand to cool.

For three years the town had enjoyed this arrangement the way it enjoyed everything in Gullport: by arguing about it. The rivalry was a local institution, older in spirit than either shop. When a fisherman came in with a day's catch, he would take a heel of rye from Maren or a warm brioche from Silas depending on the weather and his wife's mood. When two friends argued about the catch, they argued about the bread. The postbox between the shops had become, in effect, a scoreboard: Maren pinned notices to the outside of her window — *TODAY: LARDON BREAD. SELLING OUT EARLY* — and Silas pinned his across the road, and the notes grew longer, wittier, and occasionally unkind.

*Your "lardon" bread is lard and lies,* one said, in October.

*Your bread is a stone that learned to rise,* answered the other, by Christmas.

By February the postbox had accumulated four years of notes, most of them unread, and both bakers knew that the whole town kept a private list of which was funnier.

The trouble began, as trouble in Gullport usually did, with the sea.

It was the morning of the last Wednesday in June when the town council announced that the harbor light, the old lighthouse lamp that had guided ships into Gullport for a hundred and twelve years, was going dark for repair. In its place, the council proposed, would be the Harborlight Festival — a two-day celebration of the town's history, its fish, its weather, and its one small but stubborn point of pride. And at the center of the festival, on the old stone landing by the water, there would be a judging table for the town's annual Salt Loaf competition.

One loaf. One winner. One center of the town's attention.

Maren read the notice pinned to her own window and felt, for the first time in years, that the forty-seven paces between her and Silas's shop had become forty-seven miles. She read it again. She read the second notice, the one pinned across the road, which said only *CRANE & CRUMB — HARBORLIGHT ENTRIES OPEN — ASK US.*

She understood, then, that the postbox was over.

What followed was the kind of rivalry that had always lived in Gullport but had, until then, been a weather thing, something that passed. Maren baked a lard bread and, that evening, left it on the step of Silas's shop with a card: *FOR THE JUDGES. SUGGESTED. NOT SUGGESTED.* Silas, the next morning, left a card in the same spot: *MAREN. IF THE LOAF IS FOR THE JUDGES, THE JUDGES ARE FINE. IF IT IS FOR THE STREETS, THE STREETS ARE FINE. IF IT IS FOR YOU, IT IS FOR YOU.* It was the first time either of them had spoken in the second person in a note, and both of them found it, later, embarrassing.

The rivalry sharpened. Maren stopped saying the word "hello." Silas began baking two croissants per customer instead of one, which Maren pointed out, in a note, was a small act of theft. The town council, moved by the energy of it, added a second table to the landing: one for the Salt Loaf, one for a free-for-all of any bread. This was supposed to be a mercy. It was not.

The real crisis, however, was not the rivalry. It was the storm.

It came at four in the morning, as storms in Gullport come: first the fog, which thickened until the town became a sound rather than a place; then the wind, which moved through the streets like a person with a purpose; then the water, which the sea brought in, not as a flood but as a slow, patient filling. She bricked up the back window with a flour sack, pulled the shutters down, and set the starter crock on the high shelf, where it sat in the dark and did not, in the end, stop.

Across the road, Silas had been up since three. He had gone to his new commercial oven, the one he had installed that winter, and found it dead. He stood in the dark kitchen and looked at it, and he thought, with the particular clarity of a man who has never before had a machine refuse him, that the city had made him very good at things, and that none of those things were bread.

At six, the rain stopped. The wind did not.

Silas Crane.

His shop was under the same water. His display case, his laminating table, his new and expensive and useless oven — all of it, inside the low glass front of Crane & Crumb, was wet. He was standing on the dry side of his own door, which was a kind of border he had never before needed to hold.

"It didn't," Maren said.

"Good."

"Then what are you doing?"

She went inside. She went to the high shelf. She took down the crock. She carried it across to the counter, where the fire in the hearth was still low and the red-brown walls were warm. She set the crock down. Old Gull, she could hear, if she held the door still, was still breathing.

Silas came in. He did not ask. He took off his wet shirt, which was a gesture so abrupt it looked, for a second, like a threat, and hung it on the back of a chair. He stood by the counter with his arms at his sides, and Maren saw, in the grey light coming through the rain-streaked window, that his face was not the face of a rival. It was the face of a man who had been very good at a thing, and who had, in the last hour, lost the thing, and who had come across the road to the one person in the town who still had it.

"Two days," Maren said.

"I know."

"Two loaves," she said. "The competition. One for each of us."

He looked at her. "You're not going to let me have the starter."

"I'm not going to give it to you," she said. "I'm going to show you how to feed it. You'll make your own."

It was, she thought, the most honest thing she had ever said in the history of the rivalry. It was not a gift. It was a lesson. It was the thing she had been meaning, for three years, to do, in a place that was not the postbox.

Silas nodded. He rolled up his sleeves. He took a bowl from the shelf and a wooden spoon from the drawer, and Maren set the crock in front of him and said, "Now. Feed it. Half a cup of flour. A cup of water. The water's from the tap, not the cistern. The cistern water's too soft, it doesn't give the starter its body. The tap water's got iron in it. It's the iron that makes it rise like a thing that wants to be somewhere."

He fed the starter. He watched it, the way Maren had watched, for a long time. It rose. It did not, at first, in any visible way. It was a slow, wet, breathing thing, and it was not going to rise in the way a loaf rises, all at once, with a sound, but it was going.

They worked.

What happened in the kitchen of Hearth & Salt, in the two days before the festival, is something Maren never spoke of in quite the way it actually was, and what she did not speak of is the thing that made it true. They argued, in the way they always argued, about every step. They argued about the salt — Maren's salt, which came from the old pans out on the shore, which she had raked out herself in the autumn, which tasted, in the end, like the whole town; Silas's salt, which came from a bag, which was fine, which was fine, and which was not, to her, the thing. They argued about the flour — the mill flour, which the one mill on the hill made every week, and which was, for this purpose, all there was. They argued about the oven. Silas's oven was dead; Maren's oven was a hearth, a stone mouth, a thing that had been lit before either of them was born.

"Heats unevenly," Silas said. "You have to watch it. You have to turn the loaf every ten minutes, or one side burns and the other side never sets."

"I don't turn the loaf," Maren said. "I turn the loaf only when the loaf wants to be turned."

"That's not a method."

"It's a conversation."

"It's a conversation with a thing that is, in the end, just flour and water and time."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is."

And Silas, who was, in the city, a man who had never once said yes to a thing that was not his own, stood very still for a moment, and then said, "Show me."

And she showed him.

The loaves, in the end, were not the same loaf. That was the thing about them, in the end, that neither of them had planned and both of them had, in the end, made. It was a loaf that had been made, in the end, in a conversation, and it was not, to her, the best loaf she had ever made. It was the best loaf she had ever made with another person in the room.

Silas's loaf was a long one, a bât, with a pale, even crust, and a crumb that was, in the end, not the crumb of the city, but the crumb of a man who had, in the last two days, learned to watch a thing instead of controlling it. It was not a loaf that tasted like the shore. It was, to Maren, the first loaf he had ever made that had a place in it. A place. Not a city. Not a shop. A place.
