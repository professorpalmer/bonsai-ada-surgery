Chapter 1: The Tide That Breathes

The city of Oolath was not built on the ground.

It was built on a back.

For three hundred and twelve years, the city had ridden upon the vast shell of a turtle called Vethren, though the old texts had no name for the creature. Only the people knew what to call it: the Old Sea, the Walking Deep, the Slow God. The children called it simply the Turtl, and even they could not say whether the name had been a term of endearment or of fear.

Every morning before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, the city rocked. Slowly. Patiently. Like a ship at anchor in a tideless sea. And when it rocked, the bells of the Shell-Temple rang, and every citizen knew without seeing it that they were moving.

Kael stood on the rooftop of his family's bell-tower, watching the horizon. Below, in the city's lower tiers, the tide-keepers were already working, hauling their great copper scoops from the pools that had collected in the shell's depressions. The pools had been there since the city's founding. Rain had filled them. The sea had never touched the shell once — that was the first law, the first miracle, the first thing every child was taught: the turtle walked on the sea, and the sea bowed.

The old texts said that Vethren walked upon a sea that was older than the sky. No one had ever seen the water. The sea beneath the shell was as inaccessible as the stars above.

Kael turned from the horizon. He was nineteen, lean as a reed, with his mother's dark hair and his father's scarred hands — the scars coming from years of hauling rope in the bell-towers. He had been promised the bell-ringing at the next festival. The Shell-Temple's bells had not rung without a bell-ringer, and the last bell-ringer, an old man named Dorin, had died in the winter.

Now it was Kael's turn.

He descended the spiral stair of the bell-tower, his sandals slapping against the stone. The city stretched below him, tier upon tier, built into the shell's ancient curves. Houses of pale stone climbed the upper ridge. Bridges of woven kelp-thread spanned the valleys between tiers. In the lowest tier, the market squares bustled with traders selling salt, shells, and the small, precious stones that the shell shed as it grew. The shell grew, after all. That was the second law: the turtle's shell kept growing, and the city must keep building outward to follow it.

Kael's father met him at the tower's base.

"You're going to ring at the dawn ceremony," his father said. It was not a question.

"I'm going," Kael said. "I was told."

His father looked at him for a long moment. His face was like old leather, crinkled and sun-warmed. "Dorin rang for forty years. Never once did the bells sound the same way twice. That's what the Shell-Temple is for. Not the sound. The listening."

Kael wanted to ask what that meant, but his father was already walking away, already lost in the crowd.

Kael followed the stairs down into the market square, where the air smelled of salt and the roasted fish that the tide-keepers cooked every morning. He passed a group of children chasing a ball of woven kelp. He passed an old woman selling the small, iridescent stones that the shell shed. He passed a pair of twins arguing about whether Vethren was a god, a beast, or simply the world's way of keeping the city company.

He stopped at the edge of the lower pool, where the water was so still it looked like dark glass. The pool had been there since the first builder. No one knew if it was rain, or the sea, or something older still. The tide-keepers scooped from it every morning, and every evening they returned to the sea beyond the shell's edge, and the pool never changed.

That was the third law: the water of the pool was always the same. Always.

Kael looked at his hands. The scars on them were fading, but the memory of the rope was still there. He remembered the night Dorin had died, and how the bells had been silent. For three days, the city had not moved. Not once. No rocking. No shifting. Just stillness. The stillness was worse than the movement, Kael thought. The stillness was the worst part.

He remembered the old texts. He remembered the words, copied over and over by the scribes of the Shell-Temple, in a language no one still spoke:

When the shell stills, the walker wakes.

When the walker wakes, the city must choose.

Choose the deep.

Choose the shore.

Choose the end.

Kael looked up at the sky. The sun was rising. The city rocked, slow and patient, and the bells of the Shell-Temple rang.

He climbed the stairs.

The city of Oolath was not built on the ground.

It was built on a back.

That was why Oolath was called a city of breathing stone.

Kael stood on the rooftop of his family's bell-tower, watching the horizon.
