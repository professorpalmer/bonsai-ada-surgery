**Letters to the Unwritten**

Elias Wren climbed the Wickham Point tower each dawn slowly. The light turned white and patient above the black water. His boots rang on iron steps that smelled of salt. Below, the sea kept its own long ledger and stone. He was the last keeper on a dying island alone.

One cold Tuesday, a letter appeared in the lighthouse box. No stamp, no postmark, only a small blue wax seal. Elias cut it open with trembling, careful, salt stained fingers. The paper smelled of rain that had not fallen yet. Inside, one line waited in a neat childhand fresh ink.

Dear Elias, I am not yet born, but I am writing. My name is Maren, and I am coming to you. I will be born next spring in the village house. Please keep the Wickham light steady while the sea rises. I need you to remember my name before I learn it.

Elias read the words three times, then set them aside. A child not born could not write such letters here. He laughed until the sound became thin and afraid. The wind tapped the glass, as if asking for answers. That evening, he climbed the tower and watched light sweep.

On Wednesday, another letter lay on the same iron bench. It bore the same blue wax seal, still soft wet. Elias opened it without thinking, his hands no longer unsteady. The new letter asked him to bring red lanterns down. It said a storm would come when gulls flew low.

Elias wrote a long letter to the unborn girl Maren. He asked where she lived who was her mother why. He asked if she feared the cold water and dark. He sealed it, hoping the sea would carry the letter. He gave the envelope to no one, and waited patiently.

Before dawn, a third letter arrived in the iron box. It answered every question without apology though no one could send. Her mother, the letter said, was a fisherwoman named Celine. Celine lived in the white cottage below the lighthouse path. She was expecting a daughter in spring, said the letter.

The letters kept coming, one every morning of the week. They asked him to polish the brass until it sang. They told him to leave door open on windy days. They requested a small stone from harbor, smooth and warm. Each request seemed small but it touched deep inside Elias.

On Saturday, Elias took the boat to Crag Village alone. The island had once been busy, but now it slept. He followed the path to the white cottage below lighthouse. Celine stood in the doorway, holding a basket of fish. She looked at him with the patience of a keeper.

Elias introduced himself as the lighthouse keeper, though she knew that. Celine nodded, and her eyes moved toward his hands carefully. She asked why a man always walked slowly at dawn. Then she said that she had been expecting a daughter. Her voice was soft, but the words rang like bronze.

That night, the letters continued to arrive in iron box. They warned that a red light would burn behind cliffs. They told him that the wind would bring a boat in. The woman on a boat would need help before moon. If the keeper stayed, the light would fail, it said.

At midnight, the wind rose from the west with roar. Rain struck the tower glass in bright slanting sheets hard. Elias carried the red lantern from the lantern room door. The sea had already begun climbing rocks near the shore. A small boat rocked below the cliff, its mast broken.

Elias went down the wet steps, his old knees protesting. The boats rope had snapped and woman called for help. Celine was on the boat soaked and trembling in dark. He threw the rope, then red lantern into the water. Celine caught the lantern, and the light burned through rain.

He pulled the boat to the landing with desperate strength. Celine clung to the railing, her eyes wide and wet. Elias carried her to the lighthouse door where fire waited. She was not safe, but the storm could not reach. He gave her dry coat and a cup of tea.

Celine stayed in the lighthouse while the sea calmed slowly. Elias did not ask why she had been out there. He only asked if she needed a doctor from village. Celine shook her head Her hands rested on swollen belly. She said the child was already moving, and she feared.

In spring, while the cliffs bloomed with pale sea lilies. Celine gave birth to a girl with dark, quiet eyes. The baby did not cry. She opened her mouth, looked. Elias wrapped the child in a red blanket from bed. Celine named the baby Maren, and the letters stopped forever.

Elias wrote the first letter to Maren after her birth. He told the child that lighthouse light waited for her. He described the brass, the red lantern, and iron box. He asked the child to remember the sound of sea. He sealed the letter and put it in the box.

No answer came from the small room where Maren slept. Elias wrote again in summer when the gulls nested loudly. He wrote in winter when sea pressed against the tower. He wrote when Celine carried Maren to the sea path. Each letter disappeared as if the lighthouse had swallowed it.

Maren grew with the slow patience of a long tide. She climbed the iron steps before she could tie shoes. She asked Elias to show her lamp room at dusk. She loved the brass and the cold glass and wheel. Elias told her the light was a promise to strangers.

In winter, a new letter appeared again, signed by Maren. It said that she remembered every request before she spoke. She wrote that the lighthouse had waited for her then. She wrote that the red lantern had kept mother safe. She asked him to leave the door open when old.

Elias read Maren’s words as if they were carved stone. He set his own pen to paper with shaking hands. He told Maren that the lighthouse had been his home. He told her the light was not duty but hope. He told her the sea had always asked for kindness.

The village grew smaller, and the lighthouse became less necessary. A new machine arrived to burn the oil without keeper. Elias was told the light could be watched from afar. But Elias remained, because Maren had asked him to stay. He polished the brass each morning and left red lantern.

Maren came to the lighthouse more often as she grew. She brought books from the mainland and a blue pencil. She sat on the iron steps and listened to wind. She asked him why the letters had come before birth. Elias told her that light sometimes reaches ahead of time.

Maren asked Elias to keep the light for many years. He agreed, though the village gave him no reason to. The machine had already begun to fail in wet months. Each time it sputtered, Elias lit the old lamp steady. Maren watched the flame climb, and her face was quiet.

Elias grew old in the lighthouse like a lamp grows. His hair turned white, and his hands trembled at dawn. But he still climbed the steps, counting each careful number. Maren brought him soup, and his chair by the fire. The letters from the future grew fewer, then stopped entirely.

One spring morning, a final letter appeared in iron box. It was signed by Maren, and the handwriting was old. The letter said she had found the lantern by sea. It said the machine had failed, and she had come. It asked him to keep the door open for ever.

At noon, a woman climbed iron steps her coat wet. She carried a small blue pencil and a folded cloth. Elias looked at her for a long, quiet moment then. Her face knew him, though name was gone from village. Maren set the pencil on brass and smiled at him.

Elias felt the lighthouse tilt gently, as if it leaned. He did not ask how she had found the island. He did not ask whether the letters had been real. He only now, in a voice like old rope, said yes. The lamp room held them, and the sea waited below.

The light turned white and patient above the black water again. Maren placed her hand on the brass wheel, feeling its slow turn. Elias understood that every letter had been a step toward this morning. The tower had carried a voice from before birth into the present. The sea had kept its ledger, and the island had answered.

Maren told him she had carried the small blue pencil for years. It had been in her mother’s cottage since the winter storm. She did not know where it had come from. Elias smiled because the lighthouse had given and taken in the same hand. He said that light sometimes reaches forward before it reaches near.

They stood together while the morning light climbed the glass. The red lantern glowed softly on the iron bench. The machine sat silent in the corner, no longer needed. Elias saw that his life had not been a waiting room but a path. The girl who had not existed yet had become the reason the path existed.

At sunset, Maren took the pencil from her coat and wrote one line on the paper. Elias read it before she handed it to him. It said: Thank you for keeping the door open for me. He closed the letter carefully, and the tower held its breath. The sea returned, soft now, to the stones.
