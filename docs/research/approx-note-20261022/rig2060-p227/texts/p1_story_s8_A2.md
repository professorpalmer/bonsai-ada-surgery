Title: "The Unborn Light"

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday in a blue envelope with no stamp, no return address, no postmark. Eamon Voss, keeper of Gull Point Light, set it on the kitchen table beside the oil can and the tin of biscuits, as if it were a strange sea animal that had come up on the rocks.

Inside, in ink the color of wet sand, were these words:

Dear Father,
I have not yet been been born. (typo avoid) Do not be afraid. I am Wren. I am only a small sound in the dark, but I can hear the sea through the glass, and I think it is your lamp I remember. When the wind comes Friday, clean the wick at two o'clock. The light will gutter. If it goes out, the fishing boat The Heron will hit the teeth of the rocks.
I will be born when the tide is high, though I do not know the hour. My mother says I am restless. My father says I am a storm. I am both, but I am also only a possibility, and I am coming.
Please save a shell for me.

Eamon read it twice. The paper smelled of kelp and cold brass. On the back, in the same careful hand, was a drawing of the lighthouse with a crack down the side and a small bird perched on the lantern room. The bird had no wings, only two black dashes where wings should be, as if it had not yet learned how to be a bird.

He thought it must be Celia's doing. She had been restless since the pregnancy, speaking in her sleep about storms and names, and she had a habit of writing strange notes on the backs of invoices. But the note was not on the back of any paper he recognized. It was not in her handwriting. It was in a hand so small and precise it looked like it had been written by someone who had never held a pen long enough to make a mistake.

On Friday, the wind came before dawn.

By two o'clock, the wick in the lamp was guttering, the oil low, the light thinning like a man's voice in a crowded room. Eamon should not have believed the letter. He should have left it in the drawer and let the automatic lamp do its work. But he had kept the light for forty years, and he had learned that a lighthouse keeper did not argue with the sea. He climbed the one hundred and twelve steps with his lantern in one hand and the wick knife in the other, and he cleaned the wick at exactly two o'clock.

The light steadied.

At six, a radio crackled in the boathouse below. The crew of The Heron were in, soaked and shouting, saved by a beam that had not died. Eamon stood in the gallery and watched them go up the rocky path, and for the first time in his life he felt the sea was not only a thing that wanted him, but a thing that might, once in a while, be kind.

That night, Celia was sitting by the window with a book in her lap. Her hands were on the swell of her stomach. The house was very quiet except for the turn of the lamp above them.

"Yes."

"Yes."

The next letter came before he had time to put the first away. It was in the same blue envelope, and inside was another page, smaller this time, as if the writer had pressed the pen harder.

Dear Father,
I am still not born, but I am learning the words you will use for me. I know I will have a mark like a wave on my shoulder. I know I will be afraid of thunder. I know I will ask you to tell me the names of the stars before I know the names of the days. Please make a chair for me. Not a big one. A small one, by the window, where the light can touch it.
My mother says I am coming through a door that is not yet open. I do not like the sound of that. I would rather come through the lamp room, if you would let me.
I will be red and small and angry at the first. I will cry when I see your hands. Do not be sorry for the years.
Love,
Wren

Eamon read the letter while the wind knocked at the door. He thought about the years. He thought about Celia, who had carried a child like a secret after so many winters. He thought about the lighthouse, which had stood on the rock for a hundred and forty years, and about his own life, which had been mostly the keeping of small fires.

He went into the store room and looked for the small chair. There was one, with a cracked wooden seat, that had belonged to a boy who had come to the light once and been sick with the sea. He repaired it with glue and patience. He placed it by the window where the lamp's light fell in a pale wheel on the floor.

Celia found the chair and did not ask what it was for. She put a thin blanket over it.

After that, the letters came weekly. They were never long. Wren wrote about things that had not happened yet: a ship that would pass the rock without seeing the light, a gull that would learn to steal fish from the boathouse, the first snow that would make the gallery look like a crown. She wrote about her mother, who was tired and hopeful. She wrote about the sea, which she called "the big patient thing." She wrote, "I do not know what it is to be small, but I think it will be like being inside a lantern."

Eamon began to write back, though he did not send anything. He wrote in the keeper's logbook on pages that no one would see, because the logbook was for weather, tides, lamps, and the names of vessels.

Dear Wren, he wrote. The chair is by the window. The blanket is blue. I have saved a shell that looks like an ear. The storm has passed, and the sea is black and still. I do not know if this is foolish, but I am glad you are coming.

He signed his name as if signing a letter to a child he had not yet met.

Eamon found the wick soaked and the lens clouded with salt. He climbed the steps in the dark with his lantern, his hands shaking, and he worked by touch until the flame came back. The beam swept the water.

Celia was in the bed when he came down. She was pale and breathing hard. The midwife from the village was not coming; the road was blocked, and the boat could not land. Eamon brought her water, a towel, a chair, and the small wooden stool he had fixed. He sat on it with his hands on the bed frame, and he did not say anything except "Breathe" and "Almost."

"Hold me," she said.

Years later, when the lighthouse was closed and the automatic lamp took over the night, Wren returned to Gull Point as a girl of twelve, with sand in her hair and a book of stars in her bag. # The Unborn Light

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, in a blue envelope with no stamp, no return address, and no postmark. Eamon Voss, keeper of Gull Point Light, set it on the kitchen table beside the oil can and the tin of biscuits, as if it were a strange sea animal that had come up on the rocks.

Inside, in ink the color of wet sand, were these words:

Dear Father,  
I have not been born yet. Do not be afraid. I am Wren. I am only a small sound in the dark, but I can hear the sea through the glass, and I think it is your lamp I remember. When the wind comes Friday, clean the wick at two o’clock. The light will gutter. If it goes out, the fishing boat The Heron will hit the teeth of the rocks.  
I will be born when the tide is high, though I do not know the hour. My mother says I am restless. My mother says I am a storm. I am both, but I am also only a possibility, and I am coming.  
Please save a shell for me.

Eamon read it twice. The paper smelled of kelp and cold brass. On the back, in the same careful hand, was a drawing of the lighthouse with a crack down the side and a small bird perched on the lantern room. The bird had no wings, only two black dashes where wings should be, as if it had not yet learned how to be a bird.

He thought it must be Celia’s doing. She had been restless since the pregnancy, speaking in her sleep about storms and names, and she had a habit of writing strange notes on the backs of invoices. But the note was not on the back of any paper he recognized. It was not in her handwriting. It was in a hand so small and precise it looked as though it had been written by someone who had never held a pen long enough to make a mistake.

On Friday, the wind came before dawn.

By two o’clock, the wick in the lamp was guttering, the oil low, the light thinning like a man’s voice in a crowded room. Eamon should not have believed the letter. He should have left it in the drawer and let the automatic lamp do its work. But he had kept the light for forty years, and he had learned that a lighthouse keeper did not argue with the sea. He climbed the one hundred and twelve steps with his lantern in one hand and the wick knife in the other, and he cleaned the wick at exactly two o’clock.

The light steadied.

At six, a radio crackled in the boathouse below. The crew of The Heron were in, soaked and shouting, saved by a beam that had not died. Eamon stood in the gallery and watched them go up the rocky path, and for the first time in his life he felt the sea was not only a thing that wanted him, but a thing that might, once in a while, be kind.

That night, Celia was sitting by the window with a book in her lap. Her hands were on the swell of her stomach. The house was very quiet except for the turn of the lamp above them.

“Did you hear about the boat?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“You cleaned the wick early.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him. “You’ve been strange lately.”

“I have letters.”

She laughed. “Letters from who?”

He did not answer.

The next letter came before he had time to put the first away. It was in the same blue envelope, and inside was another page, smaller this time, as if the writer had pressed the pen harder.

Dear Father,  
I am still not born, but I am learning the words you will use for me. I know I will have a mark like a wave on my shoulder. I know I will be afraid of thunder. I know I will ask you to tell me the names of the stars before I know the names of the days. Please make a chair for me. Not a big one. A small one, by the window, where the light can touch it.  
My mother says I am coming through a door that is not yet open. I do not like the sound of that. I would rather come through the lamp room, if you would let me.  
I will be red and small and angry at the first. I will cry when I see your hands. Do not be sorry for the years.  
Love,  
Wren

Eamon read the letter while the wind knocked at the door. He thought about the years. He thought about Celia, who had carried a child like a secret after so many winters. He thought about the lighthouse, which had stood on the rock for a hundred and forty years, and about his own life, which had been mostly the keeping of small fires.

He went into the store room and looked for the small chair. There was one, with a cracked wooden seat, that had belonged to a boy who had come to the light once and been sick with the sea. He repaired it with glue and patience. He placed it by the window where the lamp’s light fell in a pale wheel on the floor.

Celia found the chair and did not ask what it was for. She put a thin blanket over it.

After that, the letters came weekly. They were never long. Wren wrote about things that had not happened yet: a ship that would pass the rock without seeing the light, a gull that would learn to steal fish from the boathouse, the first snow that would make the gallery look like a crown. She wrote about her mother, who was tired and hopeful. She wrote about the sea, which she called “the big patient thing.” She wrote, “I do not know what it is to be small, but I think it will be like being inside a lantern.”

Eamon began to write back, though he did not send anything. He wrote in the keeper’s logbook on pages that no one would see, because the logbook was for weather, tides, lamps, and the names of vessels.

Dear Wren, he wrote. The chair is by the window. The blanket is blue. I have saved a shell that looks like an ear. The storm has passed, and the sea is black and still. I do not know if this is foolish, but I am glad you are coming.

He signed his name as if signing a letter to a child he had not yet met.

The letters changed near the end. They became less certain.

I am coming through a storm, it said. I am coming through a door that is not yet open, and the door is afraid of me. Do you think the light will be on?

Another: I am not a bird, though I am called Wren. I am more like a sound before the bird. I will not be able to tell you what I remember before I am born. I only know that I will come to you because you are the one who keeps the light for anyone lost.

The last letter before the birth was only a line:

Hold me, and do not let the light go out.

On the morning of the birth, the light was out. Not guttering, not thinning, but out. The lamp had failed in the night, and the oil had run low. Eamon found the wick soaked and the lens clouded with salt. He climbed the steps in the dark with his lantern, his hands shaking, and he worked by touch until the flame came back. The beam swept the water.

Celia was in the bed when he came down. She was pale and breathing hard. The midwife from the village was not coming; the road was blocked, and the boat could not land. Eamon brought her water, a towel, a chair, and the small wooden stool he had fixed. He sat on it with his hands on the bed frame, and he did not say anything except “Breathe” and “Almost.”

When the baby came, she was small and red and angry at the first. She was not crying. She was looking at him.

Eamon took her into his arms. She was warm and heavy, and for a moment she seemed not like a person but like a whole weather system, small and furious. Then she opened her mouth and made a sound so high and clear it was almost a bell.

“Hold me,” she said.

The words were tiny and impossible, but they were there.

Celia laughed and cried. “She says hold you,” she said.

Eamon looked down at the child in his arms. “I know,” he said.

She did not say Wren. She did not say father. She only held his hand with both fists, and her thumb was blue in the cold.

Years later, when the lighthouse was closed and the automatic lamp took over the night, Wren returned to Gull Point as a girl of twelve, with sand in her hair and a book of stars in her bag. She had grown into a small, sharp-faced thing who asked questions without waiting for answers. She had the wave-shaped mark on her shoulder, and she was afraid of thunder, and she loved the smell of kelp more than she was allowed to admit.

Eamon had not been to the light for months. His knees were bad, and his hands ached in cold weather. But when Wren climbed the one hundred and twelve steps beside him, he felt as if he were going back to the first letter.

She stood in the gallery and put her hand on the brass.

“I found the chair,” she said.

“It’s still there,” he said.

“I read the notes in the logbook.”

“Yes.”

She turned to him. Her eyes were the color of the sea after rain.

“I never remember writing those letters.”

“No,” he said. “I suppose not.”

She looked out at the water. “But I remember a blue envelope.”

Eamon put his arm around her. “You did,” he said.

Below them, the automatic lamp turned without sound, without breath, without knowledge. But in the old oil room, on a shelf among tins and tools, the blue envelope from the first letter still lay open, and inside it, though no hand had written it since before Wren was born, a single word remained in ink the color of wet sand:

Again.

Years later, when Wren herself stood in the gallery as the new keeper of Gull Point, a blue envelope appeared in the mail with no stamp. Inside, in ink the color of wet sand, were words she recognized before she read them:

Dear Keeper,  
I have not been born yet. I can hear the sea through the glass. I think it is your lamp I remember.

Wren closed the envelope and opened the light. That night she cleaned the wick at two o’clock, because she had learned that a keeper does not argue with the sea.
