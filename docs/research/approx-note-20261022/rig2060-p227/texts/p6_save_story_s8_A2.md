# The Recipe for the Meridian

The galley of the *Meridian* was the only place on the ship where the air did not taste of itself.

Every other deck carried the same recycled breath: the faint metallic taste of the oxygen scrubbers, the chemical sweetness of the hydroponic mist. But every morning at 0600, when Yara opened the galley door, a different air came out — warm, flour-dusted, sour with rising yeast. For four thousand and twelve souls, that smell was the smell of home, and home was a word that, three hundred and sixty years ago, had meant a planet.

Yara was the ship's cook. The job had no rank in the manifest and no title in any of the histories. It was listed only as *galley*, a long room behind the central hydroponics bay, with the largest single responsibility aboard. The *Meridian* had not left Earth carrying a single meal. It had left carrying seeds, and a small archive of recipes, and the knowledge that everything else had to be grown, cultured, or coaxed from the walls. The wheat under the UV lamps in the south bay, the soy protein that fooled the tongue well enough to pass for meat, the algae butter, the salt harvested from the condensation tanks — all of it was raw matter. The cook's task was to turn it into something people would eat with pleasure.

Pleasure was the hard part.

"You're staring again."

Yara turned. Ilya stood in the doorway with her sleeves rolled to the elbows, a smear of yeast on her chin. She was twenty-two, the first apprentice cook in four decades, chosen not for skill but for an opinion so fixed in its stubbornness that the selection committee had called it "useful."

"I'm reading the manifest," Yara said.

"Which manifest? The one that says the anniversary meal is in three hours and the whole ship is coming to the mess hall? Because the one you're looking at is a list of rations for the agricultural deck."

Ilya crossed her arms. "For nine days you haven't come into the mess hall. You've been in here all morning, and all day before that, and the day before that. I know because the hydroponics crew sent me to fetch extra flour and you said no. The bread didn't rise in the last three batches."

Yara's jaw tightened. "It's a complicated dough."

"It's bread, Yara. It's flour and water and salt and a yeast starter that's older than most of us." Ilya leaned against the doorframe. "The anniversary is tonight. Four thousand people. The old ones. The ones who remember the story about the first bread. And the bread isn't rising."

Silence in the galley, except for the low hum of the fermentation vats. In the bowl at Yara's elbow, the dough sat pale and flat, a small gray disc that had not moved at all.

"Tell me," Ilya said quietly. "What is the recipe? The real one."

"You know what the recipe is."

"I know what goes in it. I've read the archive. There are three versions, all handwritten, all signed by different cooks, all dated within a year of each other." Ilya shrugged. "None of them work."

That was the truth, and Yara had never spoken it aloud.

The recipe archive held, like everything else on the ship, the fragments of a past that had been damaged in the turbulence of launch. What remained of the anniversary bread document was a tangle of three versions, each a slightly different arrangement of ratios and temperatures. For the first century or so, the cooks had done what any sensible cook would have done: tasted the results, adjusted, and wrote down what worked. What worked changed from one cook to the next, because the yeast changed, because the humidity changed, because the wheat from the south bay grew up in a slightly different year — and so the so-called real recipe migrated, year by year, cook by cook, like a story passed through many mouths.

Yara had taken the galley when the lung disease took her mother. She had taken the archive with it, and the secret that no one had ever told her, because the secret was that there had been nothing to tell. She did not know the recipe.

No cook knew it.

For three hundred and sixty years, the anniversary bread had been whatever the cook on duty managed that year. And it had been enough. The people of the *Meridian* were not comparing Yara's loaves to anything. There was no *before*. There was only the bread, and the meal, and the evening in the mess hall when four thousand people sat down together and said, without words, that we are here, we are alive, and the ship is still going.

But now Ilya was looking at her, and the dough in the bowl had not risen, and something in the room had gone quietly very far away.

"If I tell you," Yara said at last, "I can't do it alone. It has to be both of us. And everyone gets to see."

Ilya's expression changed. "That's the old story, isn't it. The one about the first cook and the child at her side, so no one would notice the difference between the years."

"Someone told that story. I don't know who. Maybe me, a long time ago, when I needed a better answer than the true one."

Ilya was quiet for a moment. Then she rolled up her other sleeve. "Then make it."

They made it.

They argued about the salt and the water and the heat of the ovens. The first loaf, mixed from the oldest of the three archived versions, rose only a little, pale and sullen. The second, from the newest version, rose too much and came out gummy, with a crumb that fell apart under the knife. The third time, Yara set the archive aside.

She closed her eyes and reached into a memory.

Not her mother's kitchen. The mess hall. An anniversary evening, some year she could not place. The smell of bread coming from the ovens. The old man at the end of the table who always asked for a second slice. The sound of four thousand people at once, breathing, laughing, alive. She had been young then, or perhaps old. Time had worn the year smooth. But the smell was still there, warm and sour and sweet, and it sat in her hands like an ingredient she could not name.

A little more salt. A little less butter. A little longer rest, with the damp cloth off.

The dough met her fingers and held.

It rose.

They baked until the galley glowed, and by the time the mess hall doors opened, the air on the deck had changed. Four thousand people stood in the hall with their plates in their hands, and the bread went out in batches, still warm, still breathing, and the sound that rose from the table rows was not applause exactly. It was the sound of a long-held breath finally let go.

Aunty Reyes was the first to sit at Yara's station. She was one hundred and twenty-one, the oldest living person on the ship, and she ate with the slow ceremony of someone who had once owned a kitchen of her own on a world that no longer existed. She broke off a piece of the bread, chewed, and said nothing for a long time.

"It's not the same as the first year," she said at last.

Yara felt the floor tilt. "No."

"No," Aunty Reyes repeated, and smiled. "It's not. It wasn't. The first cook was a woman from a city whose name we don't use anymore. Her mother taught her a recipe she had learned in a kitchen she never wrote down, and she remembered it the way all of us remember things: wrong, completely, beautifully. The bread of the first year was that. And every year after, someone else remembered it a different way, and baked it, and the next year someone else remembered that. The recipe was never in the archive, child. The recipe was the remembering. The bread was just the evidence."

Yara looked at Ilya. Ilya was already writing, in the small logbook she kept on her hip, the ratios they had used, the heat, the time.

"What do you want it to say?" Ilya asked.

Yara looked at the logbook, then at the loaf, then at Aunty Reyes, who was already asking for a second slice.

"Say," Yara said, "Yara and Ilya, Year Three Hundred Sixty-Two. Version One of Our Own."

The *Meridian* turned in the dark, as it had always turned, carrying its wheat and its salt and its four thousand and twelve hearts toward a star no one on board would ever set foot on. On the galley, the fermentation vats hummed, and the next dough was already rising, patient as a promise, in the warm air of a ship that smelled, on every deck it passed, of something that was not quite home but had been home for a very long time.
