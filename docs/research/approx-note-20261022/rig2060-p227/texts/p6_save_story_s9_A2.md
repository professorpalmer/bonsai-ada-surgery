# What the Garden Gave

When I was an apprentice in the lower galley, they told me a generation ship doesn't run on fusion. It runs on appetite. The reactor keeps the lights on. The kitchen keeps people alive.

The galley smells of reconstituted water and warm metal, and if you are quiet enough you can smell it too: the green breath of the bay vents, drifting in thin green threads. The day runs on a cycle of forty meals — broth on the first day, grain on the second, the long stew on the ninth, and bread on the fortieth. Bread is the most important day. Bread is the day the ship remembers it came from somewhere.

I am Kest, third-generation cook aboard the *Meridian*, and in the forty-third year of our passage to Tau Ceti, the tomato crop in bay nine died in the night. Not gradually. Overnight. The vines blackened, and by the time the hydroponics crew woke, the bay smelled of wet paper. I had smelled that once in my life, on Earth, after a storm.

The protein vats could make up the shortfall. They can grow anything: grey as a school uniform, shaped into whatever the menu wants it to be. Chief engineer Rho said the shortfall would be covered by Thursday. But a grey brick is not a tomato, and a tomato is not a memory. In the *Meridian*'s kitchen, we keep the memory.

The memory is the seed vault — a glass case in the climate bay that smells faintly of rain. Inside are Cherokee Purple, Black Krim, small Italian cherries, a few beans, a few peppers, herbs no one aboard has seen bloom. Beside the seeds is a book printed on paper so old it cracks when you turn the pages: my grandmother's recipe book, brought aboard on the first flight. Every dish in it has a date and a name. *Grandmother Okafor's stew, 1988. My mother's bread, 2031. Kest's soup, year nine.* I wrote that last one in pencil when I was sixteen, in a kitchen that no longer exists.

We eat all of it every year. Not because we have to. Because it is ours. The ship feeds four thousand people, and the kitchen feeds four thousand stories.

On Friday, Rho came into my kitchen with his tablet and told me to empty the seed vault.

"Bay nine is dead. The backup systems are marginal. Those seeds are the only viable biological material on the ship." He said it kindly, the way you say you are taking a child's toy. "We will grow them as a nutrient crop. The yield will be higher. No one will eat the difference."

*No one will eat the difference.* He was not lying about the math. That is the problem with the math.

"There is also the harvest festival," I said. "The children will be looking for vine tomatoes."

"The festival is on the lower deck. We can use the substitutes."

"Everyone will know."

He looked at me the way engineers look at weather — something that happens to other people — and left it to the council. By sunset the council had voted, four to three. Two of the four had never tasted a vine tomato. One of the three was my mother, who had cried in the climate bay the year she saw a sprout from her own seed. I got the third vote by standing in the door and not moving.

I did not sleep. I stood in the galley and watched the protein vats pulse their quiet orange, and I thought about what it would become of a ship that fed four thousand people on grey. We would not die. We would become something else. Something that misses nothing, because it has stopped asking.

Around midnight I took the service lift to the climate bay. The vault case was still shut — no one had taken the seeds yet, and there was a census at six. I had a small keycard from a maintenance rotation, a cloth bag, and about two hours.

The vault opened with a whisper that sounded like rain. I took the seed packets one by one and put them in my bag. I left the paper book where it stood.

There was a note tucked between the pages. Small, in pencil, in a hand I had seen before in the oldest photograph in the galley. The first cook.

*If you are reading this, we made it this far. Keep cooking. The children who land will be hungry for something we cannot give them. Feed them anyway.*

I did not put the note in my bag. Some things are not for pockets. Some things are for the table.

The first cook's name was Adaeze, and she is the reason there are recipes in this galley at all. The ship's designers had planned for calories and nutrients and the long arithmetic of survival. Someone else — Adaeze, I think, because the first generation got exactly one cook — had planned for the rest of it. The part that doesn't show up on a nutrition chart.

The next morning the engineers found the case empty. I told them the seeds had been moved to the emergency stores. It was a small lie, and it tasted like salt on the tongue. The emergency stores are down below, in the dark, and their climate control is set for long darkness. You don't get tomatoes in the dark.

The nursery is the quietest place on the ship. The children's sleepers have small hydroponic trays, enough just to keep the air sweet. I found a hollow behind the wall, a cold spot the crew had never fixed, and I dug it with a spoon from the drawer of broken things. I put the seeds in and ran a thin line from the water pipe.

After that I did a little bit of everything. I cooked the meals for four thousand as if nothing had changed. I kept the vats humming. I told the nursery children, after the feedings, about tomato trees in the old world, how the fruit hangs so low the children can pick it, and how the vine grows toward the light. I told them I would be watching. I told them the truth of it, in a voice too small to be a warning and just big enough to be a promise.

Three weeks later the first sprout came up. Green, thin, impossible. The full inventory came that week, and by then I had told them the seeds were in the emergency stores for long enough to become the truth. The lie held warm. It was, I am beginning to think, a lie I had earned.

On the morning of the festival I carried a small box down to the lower deck, against my chest, under my coat. The first tomato was the size of a plum and darker than anything in the records. I bit into it and tasted summer. I had never seen a real summer, not since I was small. And I thought: this is what the ship is for.

The lower deck was a sea of faces. Children in their color jackets ran in circles. Someone had hung the bay nine signs in the center of the hall, and the adults nodded as they passed. My mother stood by the serving line with her arms crossed, watching me the way she watched me when I was small and I had secrets.

"Is it ready?" she asked.

"Yes, Mama."

"Good." She didn't ask anything else. Some questions you don't ask a cook.

I set the tomato in the palm of a girl of maybe four who had been crying at the festival all day. She looked at it the way you look at something you cannot put a price on. She ate it in three bites. Then she looked at me and said, "Can we have more?"

"Yes," I said. "We can."

That night I went back to the hollow behind the nursery wall and added a line to the recipe book. *Kest's tomato, year forty-three.* And beneath it, the first cook's words, which I had been carrying around for a month. Now the book held three of us: her, my grandmother, and me.

I stood in the nursery and listened to the ship breathe. Above us the reactor hummed its low constant note. Below us the emergency stores kept their cold, empty silence. And somewhere in the wall, something small and green was reaching toward the water. The honest part of the ship.

The *Meridian* has another fifty-six years to go. I will be dead, and my daughter, and my daughter's daughter, before we land. They will come down to a sky I will never see, on a shore I cannot name, hungry for something I cannot give them.

But not empty. Nothing on this ship is empty. The vats are full. The recipe book is full of dead people's hands. And in the wall, something green and impossible is growing, learning what a tomato is supposed to taste like, learning what we were supposed to be.

That is the job. That is the whole of it: keep a fire lit in the dark, feed four thousand stories a day, so that when the first child of the next world eats a tomato, they will know — just for a moment — what Earth tasted like.
