# Salt to Taste

On the generation ship Meridian, the smell of the galley is the only thing that belongs to Earth.

Mara has been a cook on the ship for thirty years, ever since the morning the colony on Mars slipped past her window, and her grandmother, the only person who had ever tasted the sea, stayed behind on the docks.

The galley is a curved room at the bottom of the ship, where the air shimmers with heat and the walls are stained with thirty years of steam, sauce, and flour. Above the great ovens hang the lights Mara's mother installed, the only ones that have ever glowed the color of a sunset on Mars. Everything else on the Meridian is white, clean, and new. The galley is not. The galley is a place that remembers.

Outside, the ship drifts in the dark between stars. Kestrel is a point of light still small enough to hide behind a finger, though it is growing, and every morning it grows a little larger. They will reach the system in six weeks. In six weeks, the Meridian will be a relic. The galley will be a memory. The people inside it will be strangers to a place they can almost see but cannot yet touch.

Today is the last cooking of the voyage. Mara knows this the way you know the first cold morning of winter, before the calendar has told you. She sets out the pot she has used every day for twenty-six years, the one with the handle bent by a hundred small lifts. She grinds coriander with a stone mortar, and the smell of the dust that rises is so thick she has to hold her breath.

The door of the galley opens, and Lina stands in the frame, still in the coveralls of the hydroponics deck. Her hair is tied back with a strip of green fabric. She is thirty-four. She has the same hands as Mara, but steadier. The two of them have worked side by side for six years, and today the order is finally going to be read the other way around.

"Mother," Lina says.

"Not yet," Mara says. "First, the pot. I want to hear you say it back to me."

"Salt to taste," Lina says.

"To what? The ship has been tasting salt for thirty years. I want you to taste the pot."

Lina tastes. She does not frown. She closes her eyes, which is worse, because Mara's grandmother used to do that, too, standing in the market at Mars Base 9, holding a cupped handful of tomatoes and looking for something she could not name.

"Good," Mara says. "Now the sauce."

Mara pours the pot over the rice and watches the steam rise. It looks like breath. It looks like the fog that used to sit over the water in the old colony, the water they had only ever seen in pictures and never once tasted, the way her grandmother had tasted the sea with one long sip and had not spoken to anyone for a week.

"The ship will be a memory," Lina says softly. "But the food will not. I've counted the children who came from the garden deck. Forty-three. They'll all want to be here tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that."

Mara reaches beneath the counter and brings out a leather-bound notebook. It is thinner than it looks. Its cover is worn smooth by four generations of hands, and its pages are stained with oil, turmeric, and the salt of a kitchen that has never known the sun.

"My great-grandmother wrote the first page," Mara says. "She wrote down a recipe for a bread with yeast that could only be raised with warm hands. I have read it four thousand times.
