Chapter One: The Whistle in the Pines

The first sound came at 2:17 in the morning, a thin copper wail that climbed through the pines and slipped under Art Calloway's bedroom door.

For a man who had spent forty years listening to rail, there was no such thing as a sound that merely arrived. It announced itself: the weight of it, the distance, the way the air bent around a ridge. Art sat upright in the chair by the window, his bare feet cold against the floor, and listened.

The whistle was not from the town. It came from the old Cedar Hollow spur, a dead line that had not carried a regular passenger train for eleven years. Yet the sound carried the exact, stubborn character of the narrow-gauge engine they still kept for summer excursions: low, sweet, and slightly out of tune.

Art pulled on his boots.

The snow on the porch was new and bright, undisturbed except for the prints of a few mountain hawks. Beyond the dark treeline, the valley lay folded in fog, the river a silver seam where the moon caught it. Cedar Hollow was a small place, the kind of town that kept its secrets in the same way the mountains kept their snow—tight, patient, and only at the mercy of the season.

He stepped outside and felt the cold climb his ankles like a living thing. He had been a conductor for the Cedar Hollow Mountain Railroad from 1964 to 2011, long before the company sold the tracks to the county and long before the last regular passenger car rattled over the trestle. People still called him "the Conductor," even though he hadn't worn a cap in fifteen years. It stuck to him the way a train number stuck to a schedule: once you heard it, you kept hearing it.

By the time he reached the old station platform, the air was so still that his breath made a small, white ghost in front of him. The station was a narrow wooden building with a red roof and a ticket window painted white. The door hung loose on one hinge. Art pushed it open, and the smell of cedar, cold dust, and something else reached him before the darkness did—something sharp and sweet, like copper left too long on the tongue.

He did not go in.

At the far end of the platform, where the platform met the old track bed, there was a shape that did not belong.

Art stopped.

The snow around the shape had been broken in a circle, as if someone had fallen and lain there a long while. The shape was a man. He wore a dark coat, too clean for a fall from a trestle, and beneath the coat, near the chest, a small brass object caught the moonlight. A conductor's key.

Art's mouth went dry.

The key was not a key, exactly. It was a token, the old kind they used to stamp tickets, stamped with the number 34. Train 34 had never run in his memory, not since the company renumbered the schedule. He knew it was a ghost number, a leftover from the original line, but the key— the key was real, and it had been left there, not thrown, not hidden.

He crossed the platform quickly, his boots crunching on the ice that had formed along the edges. He crouched, but only far enough to see without touching. The man's face was turned upward, eyes closed, cheeks pale in the moonlight. There was no blood where Art expected. That was wrong. There had to be blood.

"Art."

The voice came from the far end of the platform. Sheriff Dale Mercer was walking in from the dark, his boots leaving a long trail in the snow, his face grim.

"You saw something," Dale said.

"I saw a lot of things," Art replied. "Mostly that we're going to need more than a flashlight."

Dale stopped at the edge of the platform and looked at the body. His jaw tightened. He was a good man, a fair man, and a bad habit of the law: he believed in doing the right thing first, even when the right thing was slow.

"Who called it?" Dale asked.

"Who?"

"You know. Someone called it in."

Art shook his head. "No one called it in. That's what frightens me."

Dale turned to him. "Frightens you? You been a conductor for half a lifetime. You know this line better than a man knows his own name."

"I know what this line should be doing," Art said. "It should be quiet."

Dale nodded slowly. He pulled his phone from his coat pocket, but the screen was dark, and the battery was dead, as it was every January when the cold crept into the town. He cursed under his breath and put it away.

"There's a landline at the station," Art said.

"Let's go."

They went to the small office behind the ticket window. Art unlocked the back door, and the room smelled of old paper and radiator heat. A single lamp hung above the desk, and the telephone sat on a wooden stand like a thing that had been waiting a long time to be used. Art lifted the receiver and dialed the number for the county dispatcher.

It rang four times. Then a woman's voice, thin and sleepy. "County dispatch."

"Dispatcher, this is Art Calloway. Cedar Hollow. Old station, old platform. There's a body. I need a medic and a detective, and I need you to get them here fast."

"Sir, there's no one else around. We're sending Sheriff Mercer's unit."

"I know who's here. But I need a medic."

A pause. Then: "I'll do what I can, Mr. Calloway."

Art hung up. Dale looked at him.

"You sounded like you were calling in a murder."

"I am," Art said. "And I'm not going to stop calling until someone listens."

The next hour was a blur of waiting and cold. Dale called the county by phone from a nearby cabin, then went to fetch a flashlight and a blanket. Art stayed with the body, though he did not touch him. He looked at the train tracks beside the platform, the old ties blackened with age, the rails bright in the moonlight. He traced the distance with his eyes.

The body was six feet from the edge of the platform, close enough to be a fall, far enough to be a placement. The snow around it was undisturbed except for a single line of prints, small and deliberate, leading from the treeline to the body. They stopped just short of the man's shoulder.

"Who walks like that?" Dale asked when he came back.

Art looked at the prints. "Someone who has walked this track before."

Dale crouched beside him. "You think someone put him there on purpose?"

"I think someone wanted him found."

The words hung between them.

Art looked at the body. He had seen the man before, but only briefly. "His name is Jonah Pryce," Art said. "He was the stationmaster's nephew. He came to town a week ago and started asking questions about the old rail line."

Dale's eyes narrowed. "The old rail line?"

"The new people who bought the land for the ski resort. They want to put a cable car up the north slope. But the land under the slope is the old spur. They don't want it cleared because the old track runs right under the proposed lift tower."

Harlan looked up. "You think this has something to do with the resort?"

Art did not answer. He was looking at the brass token on the man's chest.

Lena lifted the coat slightly to check for injuries, and the token came loose. It fell to the snow with a small clink. Art caught it before it rolled too far.

It was heavier than he expected. The number 34 was stamped into the metal, and beneath it, in letters no bigger than a pinhead, were the words: Last Train.

He looked at Harlan. "Do you know what that means?"

Harlan shook his head.

"It's a number that never ran," Art said. "But in my day, we used to say, when a train was late, that it was on the last train. You don't miss it. You wait for it. And when it finally comes, it's the only one you'll ever catch."

The detective stared at him for a long moment. Then he put the token in a paper bag.

"Let's get this man back to the station," Harlan said. "I want to talk to the family."

As they moved the body, Art noticed something else. On the platform, near the edge, there was a single boot print, facing the body, but it had been covered in snow and then scraped clean. Not covered by the wind. Scraped by a hand.

He told no one.

Instead, he went back to the station office and opened the old schedule drawer. The papers inside were yellowed and brittle, but he knew them by heart. Train 34, he remembered, had run only in the winter, a short freight service that stopped at no station except the old one. It carried timber and, once, a man who was not supposed to be there.

Art pulled a faded map from the drawer and spread it on the desk. The line ran from the station to the trestle, then curved beneath the north slope. At the trestle, a small circle marked the site of a bridge collapse in 1974. A man had died. A conductor had been blamed, though not in court.

That man had been Art's partner.

The office door creaked.

Dale stood there. "You all right, Art?"

Art closed the drawer. "Yes. I'm fine."

"Good. Because we've got a body, and I don't know what to do with a dead man in a small town."

Art looked at him. "You don't have to do it alone. But you've got to let me help."

Dale hesitated. Then he reached out and put his hand on Art's shoulder. "You're the only one who knows this line."

Outside, the wind picked up, and the pines began to moan. Somewhere in the valley, a train whistle sounded again—short, sharp, and unmistakably close.

Art went to the window.

The snow was falling harder now, and the tracks were disappearing beneath it. But at the far end of the spur, where the line curved out of sight, a single light glowed.

Chapter One: The Whistle in the Pines

The first sound came at 2:17 in the morning, a thin copper wail that climbed through the pines and slipped under Art Calloway’s bedroom door.

For a man who had spent forty years listening to rail, there was no such thing as a sound that merely arrived. It announced itself: the weight of it, the distance, the way the air bent around a ridge. Art sat upright in the chair by the window, his bare feet cold against the floor, and listened.

The whistle was not from the town. It came from the old Cedar Hollow spur, a dead line that had not carried a regular passenger train for eleven years. Yet the sound carried the exact, stubborn character of the narrow-gauge engine they still kept for summer excursions: low, sweet, and slightly out of tune.

Art pulled on his boots.

The snow on the porch was new and bright, undisturbed except for the prints of a few mountain hawks. Beyond the dark treeline, the valley lay folded in fog, the river a silver seam where the moon caught it. Cedar Hollow was a small place, the kind of town that kept its secrets in the same way the mountains kept their snow—tight, patient, and only at the mercy of the season.

He stepped outside and felt the cold climb his ankles like a living thing. He had been a conductor for the Cedar Hollow Mountain Railroad from 1964 to 2011, long before the company sold the tracks to the county and long before the last regular passenger car rattled over the trestle. People still called him “the Conductor,” even though he hadn’t worn a cap in fifteen years. It stuck to him the way a train number stuck to a schedule: once you heard it, you kept hearing it.

By the time he reached the old station platform, the air was so still that his breath made a small, white ghost in front of him. The station was a narrow wooden building with a red roof and a ticket window painted white. The door hung loose on one hinge. Art pushed it open, and the smell of cedar, cold dust, and something else reached him before the darkness did—something sharp and sweet, like copper left too long on the tongue.

He did not go in.

At the far end of the platform, where the platform met the old track bed, there was a shape that did not belong.

Art stopped.

The snow around the shape had been broken in a circle, as if someone had fallen and lain there a long while. The shape was a man. He wore a dark coat, too clean for a fall from a trestle, and beneath the coat, near the chest, a small brass object caught the moonlight. A conductor’s key.

The platform had a crack along its third tie, a crack that had been there for twenty years, the kind of crack a conductor learned to step over. Art noticed the snow in the crack was clean, untouched. But the body was not on the crack. It lay on the old walking board, the narrow strip of wood used by station workers to cross the platform without stepping near the rails. Someone had placed him exactly where a worker would find him first.

Art’s mouth went dry.

The key was not a key, exactly. It was a token, the old kind they used to stamp tickets, stamped with the number 34. Train 34 had never run in his memory, not since the company renumbered the schedule. He knew it was a ghost number, a leftover from the original line, but the key—the key was real, and it had been left there, not thrown, not hidden.

He crossed the platform quickly, his boots crunching on the ice that had formed along the edges. He crouched, but only far enough to see without touching. The man’s face was turned upward, eyes closed, cheeks pale in the moonlight. There was no blood where Art expected. That was wrong. There had to be blood.

“Art.”

The voice came from the far end of the platform. Sheriff Dale Mercer was walking in from the dark, his boots leaving a long trail in the snow, his face grim.

“You saw something,” Dale said.

“I saw a lot of things,” Art replied. “Mostly that we’re going to need more than a flashlight.”

Dale stopped at the edge of the platform and looked at the body. His jaw tightened. He was a good man, a fair man, and a man of the law: he believed in doing the right thing first, even when the right thing was slow.

“Who called it?” Dale asked.

“Who?”

“You know. Someone called it in.”

Art shook his head. “No one called it in. That’s what frightens me.”

Dale turned to him. “Frightens you? You been a conductor for half a lifetime. You know this line better than a man knows his own name.”

“I know what this line should be doing,” Art said. “It should be quiet.”

Dale nodded slowly. He pulled his phone from his coat pocket, but the screen was dead, as it was every January when the cold crept into the town. He cursed under his breath and put it away.

“There’s a landline at the station,” Art said.

“Let’s go.”

They went to the small office behind the ticket window. Art unlocked the back door, and the room smelled of old paper and radiator heat. A single lamp hung above the desk, and the telephone sat on a wooden stand like a thing that had been waiting a long time to be used. Art lifted the receiver and dialed the number for the county dispatcher.

It rang four times. Then a woman’s voice, thin and sleepy. “County dispatch.”

“Dispatcher, this is Art Calloway. Cedar Hollow. Old station, old platform. There’s a body. I need a medic and a detective, and I need you to get them here fast.”

“Sir, there’s no one else around. We’re sending Sheriff Mercer’s unit.”

“I know who’s here. But I need a medic.”

A pause. Then: “I’ll do what I can, Mr. Calloway.”

Art hung up. Dale looked at him.

“You sounded like you were calling in a murder.”

“I am,” Art said. “And I’m not going to stop calling until someone listens.”

The next hour was a blur of waiting and cold. Dale called the county by phone from a nearby cabin, then went to fetch a flashlight and a blanket. Art stayed with the body, though he did not touch him. He looked at the train tracks beside the platform, the old ties blackened with age, the rails bright in the moonlight. He traced the distance with his eyes.

The body was six feet from the edge of the platform, close enough to be a fall, far enough to be a placement. The snow around it was undisturbed except for a single line of prints, small and deliberate, leading from the treeline to the body. They stopped just short of the man’s shoulder.

“Who walks like that?” Dale asked when he came back.

Art looked at the prints. “Someone who has walked this track before.”

Dale crouched beside him. “You think someone put him there on purpose?”

“I think someone wanted him found.”

The words hung between them.

At 4:02, the first ambulance lights swept the treeline, red and white, slow at first, then faster. The medic, a young woman named Lena, got the body moved onto a stretcher, and the detective, a lean man from the county named Harlan, crouched by the tracks with a notebook in his hand.

Harlan had the face of a man who had been asking the same question over and over. “Who’s the dead man?”

Art looked at the body. He had seen the man before, but only briefly. “His name is Jonah Pryce,” Art said. “He was the stationmaster’s nephew. He came to town a week ago and started asking questions about the old rail line.”

Dale’s eyes narrowed. “The old rail line?”

“The new people who bought the land for the ski resort. They want to put a cable car up the north slope. But the land under the slope is the old spur. They don’t want it cleared because the old track runs right under the proposed lift tower.”

Harlan looked up. “You think this has something to do with the resort?”

Art did not answer. He was looking at the brass token on the man’s chest.

Lena lifted the coat slightly to check for injuries, and the token came loose. It fell to the snow with a small clink. Art caught it before it rolled too far.

It was heavier than he expected. The number 34 was stamped into the metal, and beneath it, in letters no bigger than a pinhead, were the words: Last Train.

He looked at Harlan. “Do you know what that means?”

Harlan shook his head.

“It’s a number that never ran,” Art said. “But in my day, we used to say, when a train was late, that it was on the last train. You don’t miss it. You wait for it. And when it finally comes, it’s the only one you’ll ever catch.”

The detective stared at him for a long moment. Then he put the token in a paper bag.

“Let’s get this man back to the station,” Harlan said. “I want to talk to the family.”

As they moved the body, Art noticed something else. On the platform, near the edge, there was a single boot print, facing the body, but it had been covered in snow and then scraped clean. Not covered by the wind. Scraped by a hand.

He told no one.

He stood in the doorway for a long moment, listening to the town. The river moved below. Somewhere, a dog barked once and stopped. The station’s old clock ticked in the corner, slow as a man who has learned not to rush. Every clock in Cedar Hollow had been late for some reason, and the body on the platform had only made the lateness more real.

Instead, he went back to the station office and opened the old schedule drawer. The papers inside were yellowed and brittle, but he knew them by heart. Train 34, he remembered, had run only in the winter, a short freight service that stopped at no station except the old one. It carried timber and, once, a man who was not supposed to be there.

Art pulled a faded map from the drawer and spread it on the desk. The line ran from the station to the trestle, then curved beneath the north slope. At the trestle, a small circle marked the site of a bridge collapse in 1974. A man had died. A conductor had been blamed, though not in court.

That man had been his partner.

It had been Martin Reyes. Martin had always been the one who checked the brake pipes by hand, who could tell a loose rail by the sound of a wheel passing over it. In 1974, the trestle gave way in a storm, and Martin went down with a car of timber. Art had been on the next train, the one that found them. He had not been on the train that caused it, but the town had not needed a court to decide which train it was.

One sheet had slipped out from under the map. Art picked it up. It was a winter freight list, dated thirty years earlier. At the bottom, in pencil, someone had written: No passenger after dark. If seen, do not stop.

The office door creaked.

Dale stood there. “You all right, Art?”

Art closed the drawer. “Yes. I’m fine.”

“Good. Because we’ve got a body, and I don’t know what to do with a dead man in a small town.”

Art looked at him. “You’re the only one who knows this line.”

Dale nodded, but he did not seem convinced. In small towns, trust was a currency that did not inflate. It wore thin. Art understood that. He had spent his life on a job where trust was the only thing between a crowd of passengers and a wall of iron. If a conductor was honest, the train moved. If he was not, it stopped. And in Cedar Hollow, stopping was the only kind of accident anyone could hide.

Dale hesitated. Then he reached out and put his hand on Art’s shoulder.

The token in Harlan’s paper bag caught the lamplight, and for a second it looked like it had moved on its own.

Outside, the wind picked up, and the pines began to moan. Somewhere in the valley, a train whistle sounded again—short, sharp, and unmistakably close.

Art went to the window.

The snow was falling harder now, and the tracks were disappearing beneath it. But at the far end of the spur, where the line curved out of sight, a single light glowed.

Not a car. Not a lantern.

A headlamp.

And moving.
