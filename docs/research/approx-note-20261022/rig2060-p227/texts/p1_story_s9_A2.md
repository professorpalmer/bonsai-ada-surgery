*Clara*

The third letter arrived with a sentence that stopped his breath: *You wrote, I do not know Clara. I know you only by the light you keep. I am coming, though I have not yet been born.*

He was old enough to be afraid of simple things. He had kept the light for thirty-one years, long enough to learn that loneliness could wear the shape of a person. On the fourth day, Nora came with the ferry. She stood in the doorway of the lamp room with her coat still buttoned and her hair full of wind. Nora touched her stomach. “Yes. I was going to tell you after I installed the timer. I’m due in spring.”

*Dear Mr. Vane,*

Elias read it until the words blurred. Nora was looking at him with the careful face of someone trying not to worry him. “What is it?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

She should have asked again. Instead, she opened the box of parts.

Over the next few weeks, the letters became part of the routine, like the oiling of gears or the wiping of glass. They came in the mail slot at odd hours, always dry, always with no postmark. Clara wrote from a place Elias could not place. She described a town with a harbor, a house with a blue door, a window that faced the island. She said she had grown up hearing stories about Gull Rock from a grandmother who never visited but kept photographs of the lighthouse on the mantel. She said the stories had become a kind of weather in her family, something that changed the way people spoke when they were sad.

“I don’t know how I know these things,” she wrote in one letter. “I am not old enough to have a childhood yet, but I remember the color of the lamp when the fog comes up. I remember the sound of your boots on the stair. I remember the way you say the sea does not forgive, but it can wait.”

He thought of the men who had come before him, their names worn thin on the brass plate at the door. He thought of the boy who had once run a message down the coast and never returned. He thought of the night the lamp had gone dark and a trawler had run aground on the black rocks, and how the crew had cursed the sea and thanked the light with the same breath. That was the work, he told himself. It was not romance. It was keeping a promise to people he would never meet.

Elias began to answer by leaving notes on the desk, as if the next letter were a continuation of a conversation. He wrote: *I have kept the light through storms that would have taken a younger man. I wrote that because the tower taught me more than the sea did. I wrote that because someone should have.*

Nora found the notes and read them. She did not mock him. She only put her hand on the desk once, near his, and said, “If this is your way of keeping us company, Father, it is not the only one.”

He did not sleep well. He began polishing the lens with more care than the manual required. He wrote the old songs in the logbook, the ones he had not sung in years. He saved the best oil for the winter. When the mainland office sent a notice that the lighthouse would be decommissioned after the spring, he copied it, then burned it in the grate, then felt ashamed, then wrote it back out in his own hand and pinned it to the wall.

Clara wrote of a woman who kept a photograph of the lighthouse on the mantel, the same photograph Nora kept in a drawer. She wrote of a child who would one day climb the stair with bare feet and ask why the light could not stop. She wrote of a future when the island would be visited by tourists who took pictures of the tower but not of the keeper, because the keeper would be a memory. She wrote, *A memory is not less real because it is not there yet.*

The last letter before Clara was born arrived in a storm. The ferry did not come. The waves broke the rail at the base of the tower, and the wind made a sound like something being pulled out of the world. Elias climbed the stair with a lantern and found the letter on the landing, dry as a promise.

*Dear Mr. Vane,*  
*I am almost here. My mother is afraid. The sea is loud. Tell her the light will still be there when she comes. Tell her I will be a keeper too. I promise to keep it.*

He stood in the storm and felt his age, not as a weight but as a shore. He thought of Nora alone with the child, of the small black machine waiting in a box, of all the nights he had kept the lamp because duty was the only name he knew for love.

In the morning, the storm passed. Nora came by the only boat that could manage the rocks, her face thin with sleep. She carried a hospital bag and a small wooden crate. Inside were the letters, all of them, tied with blue string. She had been collecting them, she told him, in the days after they began to appear.

“I thought it was you,” she said.

“You?”

“I thought you were writing to yourself. A man who is afraid of being forgotten sometimes writes to the future and calls it a letter.”

He looked at the letters. They were addressed to him, not to her. “I don’t understand.”

“No,” she said. “But I’m going to name the baby Clara.”

The child was born three days later in a small house on the mainland, in the blue room Clara had described. The midwife said the girl had the strong lungs of a sailor and the quiet eyes of someone who had been listening. Nora held her for an hour and did not speak.

When Elias arrived with a basket of eggs and the last of the winter oil, Clara was asleep. He lifted the blanket just enough to see her face. There was no birthmark, no sign of the future, only the ordinary miracle of skin and breath. But the child turned her head toward the window, where the lighthouse stood on the island, and the light began to turn.

“Is it the light?” Nora asked.

Elias nodded. “I think so.”

He stood a moment by the railing and felt the stone beneath his boots, steady and worn by decades. The island had a way of making time seem circular, each lamp night folded into the next until a man forgot where he had begun. Clara, he thought, would not begin where he did. She would begin with the light already turning, and she would have to learn that the turning was a choice, not a habit.

That night, before he climbed into the bed they had set up in the lamp room, he found a new envelope on the desk. It had no stamp. The paper was warm, though the room was cold.

He opened it.

*Dear Mr. Vane,*  
*I am here now. I am small, and I do not know your name yet, but I know the light. Keep it going until I come to the island. Keep it going because I am coming.*

He folded the letter carefully and put it in the drawer with the others. Outside, the lamp turned, and for the first time in thirty-one years, Elias Vane did not feel he was alone on the rock.
