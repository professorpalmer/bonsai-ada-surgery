# Chapter One: The Last Departure

The rain in Pine Hollow did not fall so much as it leaned, sliding down the granite shoulders of the Black Ridge and dripping from the eaves of the old depot in long, silver threads. From the window of the general store, where Elias Crane had spent the afternoon mending a broken clock, the town looked like a watercolor left out in the rain. The church steeple blurred into the hills. The main street lost its shadows. Even the mountains seemed to recede, as if the storm were pulling the world farther away.

Elias was sixty-eight, broad in the shoulders but no longer broad in the hips, and his gray hair was pulled back from a forehead so marked by sun and cold that it looked like the side of a railroad trestle after a hard winter. He had been a conductor for thirty-one years on the Mountain Line, the old rail that once ran through the mountains to the mining town of Black Hollow before the last mine closed and the last freight train stopped running. Now he kept a small shop beside the depot, where he sold maps, old tickets, and clocks that had outlived the people who had bought them. He also listened. That had always been his trade.

The clock on the counter ticked unevenly, a defect he had not yet found. He set down his tiny screwdriver and listened again. The sound came from the pendulum, or perhaps from the gear train beneath it. Elias tapped the face gently with the back of his finger. The tick changed. Not fixed, but nearer. He smiled. Most people heard a clock and heard time. Elias heard mechanism.

The bell above the store door rang, and the smell of wet wool came in with a woman.

She was younger than Elias, though not young enough to be careless in a storm. Water beaded on the brim of her hat. She wore a dark coat with a brass button missing at the collar, and when she looked up from the rack of maps, her face had the flat, watchful expression of someone who had already decided what she wanted but was waiting to see if the shopkeeper was the sort of person who would ask.

“Closed?” she asked.

“Depends what you mean,” Elias said.

The shop was small, narrow, and crowded with old things: a brass station lantern, a stack of faded schedules, a shelf of tin railway tokens, and a map of the town pinned to the wall where the mountains rose like a broken spine. Behind Elias stood a window with a view of the depot platform, now overgrown with moss and a few stubborn blackberry branches.

“I’m looking for the place where the old train used to stop,” she said.

Elias studied her. The rain had not fully dried from her boots, and there was a streak of mud on the inside of one. Not the gray mud of town, but the brown, gritty stuff from the upper road.

“Depends what you want to know,” he said.

She set a folded piece of paper on the counter. It was a photograph, though the word seemed too generous for the brittle, sun-bleached image on its surface. The picture showed a group of men and women standing on a platform. The station sign had been cut off, but the mountains behind them were unmistakable. In the center of the photo, a woman held a child. The man beside her wore a conductor’s cap with a brass plate worn bright at the edge.

Elias had seen that cap before, though not often enough to name the man from the photograph. But he recognized the style, the kind of cap only worn by the old crew before the company changed its uniforms.

“I found this in the wall,” she said.

“Found?”

“Not in the house. In the wall of the old depot. It had been loose, and the plaster came away when I was moving the bench.” She paused. “There was also a note.”

“Give it to me.”

She handed over the folded paper. The ink was faded, brown in places, as if it had been exposed to damp for many years. It was a train schedule, hand-ruled and marked in pencil. The dates ran across the top, but only one day had been circled twice: October 17, 1974. Beneath it, in small letters, was a single line.

*Last departure. Do not let her board.*

Elias did not look up at the woman. He held the paper to the light and studied the handwriting. It was careful, the letters tight and even, the kind of writing that belonged to a man who had spent years marking tickets and counting passengers. The circle around the date had been made with a different pen, heavier, pressed hard enough to indent the paper.

“When did you find it?” he asked.

“This morning.”

“Before the rain?”

“No.”

He looked up then. “And you have been walking in it all morning.”

She hesitated. “Yes.”

Elias set the schedule down and reached for a cup. His hands were steady, but the cup trembled slightly as he lifted it. Rain drummed against the window. Somewhere beyond the store, a branch struck the wall with a soft slap.

“My name is Nora Calloway,” he said.

“I know.”

He turned. “You know my name.”

“I know what this place is.”

He went back to the counter and poured water into a second cup. “People know enough. Most of them stop there. Some of them go a little farther.”

Nora did not sit. Her eyes moved over the shelf of tokens, the map, the old lantern. “The man in the photograph.”

“You’re looking for him.”

“I’m looking for what happened.”

Elias wrapped both hands around the cup. “That’s a different thing.”

“In the note,” she said, “it says do not let her board.”

“Yes.”

“Who is her?”

The rain made a sudden noise against the roof, as if the wind had shifted. Elias set the cup down carefully.

“I’m not going to say I know,” he said. “I’m not going to say I don’t either. But I’ll tell you this: if someone wrote that note in 1974 and hid it in the wall of the depot, they wanted it to be found. Not yesterday. Not today. When it could still matter.”

Nora’s mouth tightened. “My mother’s name was Margaret.”

Elias looked at her again.

“Margaret Vale,” she said. “She was one of the people in the photograph.”

The room seemed to narrow. The tick of the clock sounded louder, though it had not changed. Elias had not heard that name since a long time ago, and the remembering of it came slowly, the way an old signal light comes into focus after dark.

“Your mother,” he said.

“Yes.”

“And this?” He tapped the schedule. “Is it yours?”

“It was in the wall of the house she used to live in.”

“Which house?”

“The one at the end of Miller Road.”

Elias closed his eyes for a moment. The house at the end of Miller Road had stood empty for twenty years, its windows boarded after the family moved away. The last thing Elias remembered of it was the porch, the paint peeling in long white ribbons, and the sound of a child’s laughter on a warm evening. He opened his eyes.

“She came back,” Nora said. “A month ago. She wanted to know something. She had a lot of questions, and she was afraid of asking them.”

“About what?”

“About the train.”

Elias lifted his cup again. “There is no train.”

“I know.”

“There hasn’t been for thirty years.”

“She was looking for the old records,” Nora said. “The passenger manifests. The accident reports. Everything.”

“The company burned those years ago.”

“No.” Nora’s voice was quiet but firm. “One man saved them.”

The clock ticked.

Outside, the rain had softened to a fine mist. The mountains were almost gone. Elias looked at the photograph, then at the schedule, then at Nora Calloway. He had learned a great many things on the Mountain Line: which whistle meant danger, which whistle meant welcome, which whistle meant nothing but a man’s boredom. He had learned how to read a track by the way the grass grew along its edges, how to know a passenger was lying by the way he held a ticket. But he had not learned, in all those years, what to do with a dead woman’s name appearing in the present.

“My mother died last winter,” Nora said.

Elias set the cup down. “I’m sorry.”

“She died suddenly. Heart failure, they said. But before that, she had been digging.”

“For what?”

“For the truth.”

He nodded slowly. “What truth?”

“The one about the October 17 train. The one that never arrived.”

The words landed in the shop like a dropped tool, ringing briefly before falling silent. Elias looked at the window. The depot platform lay empty beyond the glass, dark under the eaves. The old waiting room was locked, and the office at the end of the line was a ruin. But the tunnel mouth at the far end of the spur was still visible, a black opening in the hillside, half hidden by brambles.

“The train did not arrive?” he said.

“No. The schedule says it should have.”

“The records say the same thing.”

Nora’s jaw tightened. “There were passengers. There was a woman named Margaret. There was a child. There was a conductor named Samuel Reed.”

Elias felt the name settle in him. Samuel Reed. He had never known the man by more than that, but the name fit the old cap, the old handwriting, the careful pencil marks on the schedule. He had been a passenger on that train, years before Elias joined the crew. He had been a quiet man, always seated near the door, with a coat that smelled of tobacco and camphor. People had liked him because he liked children, and because he never once complained about the mountain fog or the long stretches of empty track.

“He died in the fire at the mill,” Elias said.

“Fire? No one talks about it that way now. It was an accident.”

“A lot of things are called accidents until someone asks the right question.”

Nora looked at the photograph. “I need you to tell me what you know.”

“I know things the way a conductor knows things. I know what I saw, what I heard, what I couldn’t explain.” He reached for the map on the wall and unrolled it across the counter. “I know the line. I know every cut, every switch, every place where the track narrows. I know which sections the rain makes slick and which ones hold the old rust in the ties. I know the sounds the mountain makes when the weather is changing.”

“That’s not enough.”

“It may be.”

The bell rang again, but this time it was a man who came in. He wore a wet rain coat and a sheriff’s badge that had begun to tarnish at the edges. His name, Elias knew, was Dale Merrick, though no one in town called him that often. He was a large man, but tired, with a face that looked as if it had been washed too many times in cold water.

“Elias,” he said.

“Sheriff.”

Merrick’s eyes went to Nora. “I believe you’re the woman who called about the old depot.”

“I didn’t call.”

“I got your number from the store.”

Elias glanced at Nora. “You gave him your number?”

“Someone did.”

Merrick pulled a chair from the shelf of maps and sat down without being invited. “You found something in the wall?”

Nora nodded. “A photograph and a note.”

Merrick leaned forward. “A note saying what?”

Elias held up the schedule. “That this is what it says.”

Merrick’s face changed. Not much, but enough. The corner of his mouth tightened. His eyes moved from the paper to Nora, then to the photograph.

“We’ve been looking for that photograph,” he said.

Elias looked at him. “You have?”

“Two days ago. Someone broke into the old depot.”

Nora’s hand moved toward her coat. “The wall was open when I got there. They had been in before me.”

Merrick nodded slowly. “Yes.”

“The police were here this morning,” Nora said.

“They’re still here.”

“Then why didn’t you say anything when I came in?”

Merrick’s jaw worked. “I was going to tell you. I just needed to see who you were first.”

Elias studied the sheriff for a moment. There was something in his face that had not been there before, some hardness that Elias associated with bad news held too long. It made him think of a brake that had been set too early, not enough to stop the train, only to shake it.

“What happened this morning?” Elias asked.

Merrick looked at Nora.

She met his gaze. “My mother’s house.”

Elias’s pulse slowed, though his thoughts quickened.

“What happened?”

“They found the body.”
