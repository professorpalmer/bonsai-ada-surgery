# Chapter One: The Shell of Veyra

The city of Veyra breathed.

Not metaphorically. Every few hours, with a slow rolling heave that rattled the windows in their iron frames and sent the kettle in Maro's kitchen spilling into the fire, the whole city rose and dipped. The turtle beneath them—the great creature the old maps called Orun—had swallowed a mouthful of ocean water and was now coughing it back up in a long, shuddering exhale. Rain the size of coins struck the rooftops. The harbor bells began to ring, as they always did at the first breath, to tell the fishers which docks would flood.

Maro wiped the kettle dry and stepped to the window. Below, the town sprawled across the curve of the shell like a colony of barnacles on a ship's hull. White stone towers climbed toward the sky from the central ridge, and between them ran the walkways of rope and timber where children chased each other in the salt wind. At the city's edge, the rim of the shell dropped away to black water, and below the rim, so far down the dark water that most people only saw it when the light was right, something enormous was moving in the deep.

They never said what it was. Orun's shadow, the elders called it. The reason the city could not sail past its eastern rocks. The reason the tide came up in Veyra and not three leagues out in the open sea.

"Morning, Maro." Old Tessu sat at the end of the walkway, mending a net with eyes like polished pebbles. "Smells like the young breath today. The whole city's going to be wet by noon."

"Three days to the Festival of the First Shell, Tessu." Maro shrugged off his coat. "By then the whole of the city will be wet whether Orun breathes or not."

Tessu smiled at that. He was seventy, maybe eighty—nobody remembered exactly, which was another thing about the city: time moved differently under the shell. "You'll be in the processions, then. What will your family carry?"

Maro's jaw tightened. "My father's old lantern. The one he carried the night the rim broke."

It was a small thing to say, and it landed small. But everyone in Veyra knew the story of the rim breaking. Three years ago, a storm had come up from the south, the kind the maps called a *thunderfall*, and the waves had clawed at the city's eastern rim the way fingers claw at a wound. Two hundred feet of stone had come loose and gone down into the deep. The sea had rushed in to fill the gap, and the city had been drowning, slowly, for two days, until the tide turned and the fishers dragged the people out with nets meant for herring.

The elders had rebuilt the rim. Or so they said.

Maro didn't believe them. He was a rim-watcher, one of the few in Veyra whose work was to stand at the edge of the world and watch the water. He'd been watching for two years now, and he saw it: the broken stone did not match the new stone. The old pieces were the color of river mud, worn and pitted by salt. The new pieces were pale and smooth, and they sat in the wall like false teeth. Someone was hiding the shape of the scar.

He should have said it. He had said it, in his head, every single day for two years.

"You're thinking too loud," Tessu said. "It'll come up in your shoulders by night."

Maro walked on without answering. The Festival was three days away, and that meant three days of preparation, and preparation meant the Guild of Carriers. He ducked through the arch at the guild hall and into the smell of tallow and old rope.

"Maro." The guildmaster, a broad woman named Cael, didn't look up from the ledger she was writing in. "You're early. The new lanterns go on the rim tonight."

"I heard the rim's being worked on."

Cael's pen stopped. "It is being worked on. That's all."

"It's not the old stone, Cael."

Her pen started again, and she did not look up. "The stone doesn't matter, Maro. The lanterns do. When the Festival comes, every house on the rim will be lit. If someone climbs the broken stretch after dark, they'll know it's ours. They'll know we hold the line."

"And if the line isn't there?"

"Then we'll find a new line." She closed the ledger. "Bring your lantern. The one your father carried. I want the old flame on the rim, not a new one. Some things you don't replace, boy. You just pass on."

Maro stood there for a long moment. Outside, the city heaved again, and a wave of cold wind came through the door. Somewhere far below the rim, in the black water where no light reached, something vast turned over in its sleep.

He took the lantern from the shelf. It was small, dented, and the glass had a crack that ran like a frozen river. His father had carried it the night the rim broke, and his father had been dead by the time the water stopped.

"Come," Cael said, softer now. "Three days, Maro. Then the whole city will be watching the rim again."

He walked out into the wind, up the ridge toward the center of the shell, and for the first time in two years, he didn't look down at the water.

The city breathed, and he let it.
