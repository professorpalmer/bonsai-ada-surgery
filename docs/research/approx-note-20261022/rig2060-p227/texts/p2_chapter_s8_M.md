Chapter One: The Last Departure

Harlan Pike had spent thirty-one years listening to steel, so when the sound came through his window that February morning, he knew before the kettle had boiled. It was the mountain line, the long low complaint of the Crest and Alpine Railroad, the last train out of Pine Hollow. Or rather, the kind of train that would have been the last, if the line still ran.

The town slept below him in a bowl of black rock and blue pines, a place so small the mailman knew which houses were sick by the way the porch lights stayed on. Harlan lived above the old station, in a cabin the railroad had built for the conductor when the company believed the rails might live forever. The station was now a museum that had no one to run it, a shell with a ticket window full of snow and a sign that read CREST AND ALPINE, EST. 1887, the letters faded like a memory.

He dressed slowly, the way he always did, shirt first, socks second, coat after. His hands were steady, though his knees were not, and he had learned long ago that a man who could no longer climb a ladder could still read the rails. He had read them for a career. He knew every switch, every grade, every place where the track bent where the mountain squeezed it against the rock. He knew which ties had settled and which had split, which sleepers held a nail and which held only rust.

At the door, his dog, a gray mutt named Whistle, pressed against his shin. The animal had been old long before Harlan retired, and now he moved more like a rumor than a dog.

“Leave me,” Harlan said, though he already knew the dog would follow. “It’s just cold.”

Whistle’s ears lifted. That was not just cold.

Down the lane, the snow had a fresh sheen. No one had been out since the storm, except maybe the mailman, and he did not come to Harlan’s end of the station road. The air smelled of ice and the black pine resin that always rose when the sun hit the slope. Harlan pulled his cap low. The old conductor’s hat was a thing of habit; he no longer wore it on duty, but the wool still sat like a second skull against his forehead.

He had not meant to go to the spur. That was what would later make the sheriff look at him oddly, the way men look at a dog who has brought in a dead rabbit. Harlan told himself he was only walking to the water pump to fill the jug. He always filled it in the morning, before the ice got cruel in the lines. The spur lay a quarter mile beyond the station, a short dead end where the track had once been used to ship pine logs from the mountain mill. Now the mill was a pile of blackened beams and the town called it the wreck, though it had not been a wreck so much as a thing that had simply given up.

The snow on the rails was undisturbed. That was the first wrong thing.

Harlan had been a conductor enough years to know that snow did not lie. It fell where gravity allowed and settled where the air was still. A clean rail meant no one had walked it. A clean rail meant no one had carried anything across it. He stopped. Whistle sat a few paces behind him, panting through the wool of his muzzle. The mountain was very quiet, too quiet. Even the wind had gone still, as if the whole place were holding its breath.

Then he saw her.

She was lying on the shoulder of the spur, not on the track, where the snow would have been deeper and colder. She wore a green coat with a long black scarf, and her face was turned away from the light. She was not very old, maybe thirty, maybe younger, with a shock of dark hair frozen into her cheek. There was blood on the snow beside her, but not much, and it was already stiffening into dark threads.

Harlan knelt, then stood, then knelt again. His hands had not trembled in decades. He had seen men die on the line before, the kind of death the company called an accident and the families called a betrayal. He knew the look of a body that had fallen, the way a neck could break at an angle that spoke of a stumble or a step taken too far. This body had not fallen. The arms were folded, the head was turned as if she had been laid down, and in her right hand she clutched something small and bright.

It was a key.

Not a house key, not a car key, but a brass railway key with a long shank and a round bow engraved with the Crest and Alpine monogram. Harlan knew the shape. It was the key to the switch lockbox, the box that hung on the post near the water tower, where a conductor kept the token that let a train through. The company had retired the old system twenty years ago, replaced it with electronics and a man at a depot in town who thought he understood signal boxes. Yet the key still existed, because men kept old keys the way other men kept knives.

The token itself was nowhere to be found.

The whistle from Whistle came again, sharper this time, and Harlan realized he had been standing so long that his boots had sunk to the ankles. He looked toward the station. The sky had changed. A thin band of sun lay across the roof of the old depot, and the snow was beginning to turn gold. If he wanted to know whether anyone had been there before him, he would have to look before the day erased it.

He went to the nearest switch, the one at the spur where the main line curved away into the mountain. The lever was closed. The lock was locked. The lockbox on the post was empty. A man who did not know the line would have seen a dead woman and a broken lock. Harlan saw a place where someone had been careful, and where someone else had not.

The door to the station banged.

Sheriff Dana Calloway came up the walkway with snow on her shoulders and a coffee cup in her hand. She was a small woman with a hard face and a habit of speaking as if every sentence were a bill being paid.

“Harlan,” she said. “I saw you out there. You’re not going to tell me it’s a dog or a fox, are you?”

He did not. He pointed.

She went out without another word. By the time she reached the body, her face had gone flat. She looked at the snow, the rail, the key, and then at Harlan.

“Who is she?”

“No name that I can see. No badge. No husband on the porch.”

“You’re the only one on this side of town who knows the old line. What do you see?”

Harlan looked at the snow around her. The print of her coat was a clean patch, but behind it, in the deeper drift, there were marks. Not footprints exactly. Drag marks. Two of them, spaced where a body would be pulled if the person carrying it had a bad knee. One ended at the edge of the spur. The other stopped just short of the tree line.

“Carried,” Harlan said. “Not far.”

Dana crouched. “You’re sure?”

“I’ve carried men down that spur when the line froze. You know what a bad knee sounds like in the snow.”

She looked at him for a long moment. There was something in her eyes that was not unkindness, but it was not trust either. Trust had not been given to him since he retired. The railroad had not trusted him either, when they had cut his route and told him the age had come for men who liked the smell of grease and diesel.

“Call it an accident for now,” she said. “Maybe she was out hiking, maybe she got into it with someone. I’ll get the state people. You can call your daughter if you want. She’ll come.”

“Her name is not on the body,” Harlan said.

“No. It isn’t.”

Dana took out her radio. “Dispatch, it’s Calloway. We’ve got a female, unknown, at the old Pine Hollow spur. Cause looks suspicious. Send the medical examiner and, if you’ve got them, the state homicide.” She paused. “And a body bag. It’s cold.”

When she switched off the radio, the snow fell again. It came down slow and quiet, covering the drag marks like a man covering a mouth.

The state would take over. That was how it always worked in a place this small. The people who came from the city had gloves that were too thin for snow and questions that were too clean. They would ask Harlan to identify the line, to explain the switches, to tell them which way the wind had blown the snow. They would say that the key was evidence and that he should not touch anything else. They would not ask him about the token, because a token was not a thing that belonged in a court unless you were trying to prove who had been allowed to pass.

But Harlan had not touched anything else. He had only looked.

At the water tower, the lockbox hung with its chain loose. Inside, where the token should have been, there was a small rectangle of frost. A token was brass, and brass held warmth. If it had been removed in the morning, the cold would have taken its shape and left it behind like a coin in the air. Someone had taken it recently. Not after the body was laid, because the drag marks stopped before the tower. Before. The body had been brought to the spur after the token was gone. That meant the person carrying her had known the line well enough to take the key and know where to put the token, and well enough to choose this place where the snow would hide a secret and a body.

Harlan looked toward the tree line. The drag marks stopped there, but the trees were thick with snow. A person could have walked back with the key and not left a trace if the wind had been at their back. Or they could have hidden in the snow and watched until Harlan came.

Whistle whimpered.

He bent again, not for the body, but for the key. It was still in the dead woman’s hand, but now that he looked, the snow beneath the hand had a thin line where it had been lifted. Someone had placed the key there, or she had been holding it long enough that it had grown to her fingers. Either way, it was not random.

The key was the Crest and Alpine key. The last of a line that had no train.

He put it in his pocket without thinking, then took it out and put it back on the snow, the way a conductor would put a token on a post. If he wanted the town to understand what had happened, he would have to be the one to carry the weight of it.

From the station window, he saw a figure appear on the mountain road.

It was not the mailman. The mailman did not have a long black coat, and he did not walk with the patience of a man who had already decided to wait.
