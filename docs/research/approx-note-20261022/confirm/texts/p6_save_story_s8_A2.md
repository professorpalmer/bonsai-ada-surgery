# The Small Red Sun

The morning bell on the Meridian did not ring. It hummed — a low, patient C that traveled up through the deck plates and into the soles of Marta's boots before it reached her ears. She walked to the galley inside that vibration, apron already tied, because on a ship of four thousand souls the cooks began before the sun. On the Meridian, the sun was a painted disc the size of a thumbnail on the dome of the observation bay, rotating once every twenty-four hours for the comfort of people who had never once seen the real one.

The galley was a long room of brushed steel and blue light. She checked the rehydration tanks: protein, starch, the grey paste that had fed this ship for one hundred and ninety-six years. She checked the yeast cultures. She checked the small white flower on the counter, a hydroponic lily that no one had ever named.

Then she checked the thing she was not supposed to check: the seed-vault access log, still flagged red on Bay Seven. The garden at the heart of the ship — the place children were taught, in the first year, that plants grew — was dying. The spore counts had climbed for three years. Chief Agri, old Soren, had told her the fungus would take Bay Seven by winter. Then Bay Five. Then, eventually, everything.

"Breakfast is ready in the canteen, Cook," said the morning voice over the comms. "Ration cycle two begins in forty-eight hours."

"Already on it," Marta said, to no one in particular.

---

Her secret kept itself in Bay Three, behind a panel marked *Water Reclamation Auxiliary*, in a space the size of a coffin. There were three pots in that space. A basil plant, no taller than her hand. A green pepper that had forgotten how to ripen. And one tomato plant, no bigger than a fist, with a leaf curling at its edges and — hanging from a stem thin as thread — a single green sphere the color of wet moss.

It had taken four hundred and twelve days.

She kept the journal beside the pots. The paper was thin as onion skin; the ink was rust. The first cook on the Meridian had written in it on his first shift:

*Tomato, Roma variety. Seeds recovered from the Ark. If a crewman asks you to describe it, tell him it is a small red sun, because that is what he will see when he bites in.*

Her grandmother had kept the journal. Her mother had kept it. Now Marta did, and the journal's first recipe was not a recipe at all. It was a memory of a meal eaten on a world with rain:

*Tomato, sliced. No oil — there is no oil on the ship, only salt. Basil, a few leaves, torn by hand so they give up their scent. A pinch of salt. That is all. That was everything.*

Every morning, after the tanks were checked and the flower was misted, Marta watered the plant with reclamation water — water that had once been the ship's breath. The basil smelled green and sharp and alive, and she let herself stand in that smell for one minute, which was more than the regulations allowed for anything not rated essential.

In the canteen, the crew ate the grey paste and did not complain. That was the thing about the Meridian. With no horizon and no future to argue about, no one had anything left to lose, so they ate what was given them with the quiet dignity of people who understood the meal was not the point. The meal was proof the ship was still a home.

That was what Marta had learned after her mother died — of a cough the medbay could not name, a cough that had been in the family for two generations. She had learned it from the other cooks, from the journal, from the long slow work of feeding four thousand people meals that tasted like nothing, aboard a ship that had never once been a home.

But the meal and the proof were different things, and Marta had come to suspect it, in the way you suspect a word is coming on the tip of your tongue. The proof could be grey paste. The meal had to be something else. She could not say what. She only knew, the way the first cook must have known, that she had to make it.

---

On the first day of cycle two, Soren came to her station with his face the color of the paste. He did not look at her when he spoke.

"Bay Seven is gone," he said. "Fungus took it overnight. We have Bay Five for a month. Maybe two. After that we run on paste for the rest of the crossing."

"The crossing," Marta said. "Two hundred and twelve years, Soren. The AI says the star is stable."

"The AI says the star is stable. It does not say the star is warm."

He left. Somewhere above, a child was crying over a toy that would never be fixed, because there had been no spare parts for a hundred years.

Marta looked at the tanks. She looked at the yeast. She looked at the small white flower and thought of the basil in her coffin-sized room, and the pepper, and the green thing hanging from its thread of a stem, no bigger than a thimble — the only real food left on the Meridian, and it was not even ripe.

She went to the canteen and made bread. Not the flat grey cakes the mills printed out, but bread — the ship's flour, her hands, a recipe that was not in the journal. Her mother had hummed it in her sleep. It was not a recipe so much as a song, and Marta sang it while she kneaded, low, off-key, to the hum of the deck. The bread came out of the oven golden and round and smelling of something no one aboard the Meridian had a word for.

She served it with the grey paste. She gave the bread to everyone — four thousand loaves' worth, small as eggs, torn and passed hand to hand in the canteen. The crew ate it and the paste, and some of them closed their eyes, and some of them did not, and a few of them wept without understanding why. The canteen smelled of bread, and the bread smelled of *something*, and for the first time in a long time, something on the ship had not been grey.

Marta stood at her station and thought: not enough. A meal is not a proof with a crust on it.

She went back to Bay Three. She took the basil. She took the pepper. She left the tomato, still green, still growing. Not yet. Not while it was alive.

---

On the fourth day of cycle two, she took it.

The tomato was still green, still the size of a thimble, still the only unmade thing on the ship. She cut it on the counter with her best knife, and inside it was red — not the dull red of the paste's protein layer, but a red like a wound, like the painted sun in the observation bay, like the word she had not yet found for rain.

She did not cook it. Cooking would have been a lie, the way the grey paste was a lie with a flavor. She sliced it thin. She tore a few leaves of basil by hand. She set a pinch of salt at the edge of the plate. Then she set the plate in the middle of the canteen table and stood beside it.

The canteen had gone quiet on its own, the way it always did when a cook brought out something that was not from the tanks.

"What is that?" a woman asked. She was young, third generation, one of the ones who had never seen a real sky.

"This," Marta said, "is a tomato."

Silence. Then the woman laughed, the way people laugh at a word they cannot place. "A tomato. What does it taste like?"

Marta looked at the small red sun in her palm, and at the journal open on the counter behind her, and at the bread still warm on the table, and she thought she had the answer, and then she thought maybe the answer was the point, not the taste.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I've never seen rain. I've never been to the ground. The first cook said it tastes like a small red sun, but I can't tell you if he was telling the truth or just being poetic. So I'm not going to tell you either."

She offered the plate.

The woman took a slice with both hands, like something sacred and fragile and new, and ate it. She closed her eyes. She opened them. Her face was doing something Marta could not name.

"It's not like anything else," the woman said. "It's like... like something that used to be somewhere. Like it missed."

"Maybe," Marta said. "That's what home is, I think. A thing you missed before you knew what it was."

The canteen was quiet. Around them, four thousand people had stopped eating. Not because they were hungry, or full, but because the ship had hummed along at the same low C since before any of them was born, and for one minute, in a canteen on a generation ship with no horizon and no rain and no ground, something had happened that did not hum at all. It tasted.

Soren stood at the back of the room, grey-faced, and Marta saw him press his hand to his mouth, and she understood, at last, what she had made. Not a meal. Not proof. A thread. The way the first cook had written in his rust-colored ink: *That is all. That was everything.* The thread that held one meal to the next, one cook to the next, this ship to the world it was still, after all, going to.

After that, other cooks began to ask questions. What was a tomato? How did it grow? Could we keep the seeds? Marta answered what she could. She could not grow a field on the Meridian, not with the fungus in the walls. But she could keep the basil. She could start a second pepper. She could write in the journal, in fresh ink, what she had learned: that the small red sun was not a memory of Earth, but a memory of Earth's possibility, and that the difference mattered less than the tending.

Years later, when a new cook asked her the question the first cook had asked himself, Marta would set a small green sphere on the counter, no bigger than a thimble, and say: it will be ready soon. The ship would hum its low, patient C. And on the painted sun above, the thumbnail sun would turn, once, as if to say: soon.
