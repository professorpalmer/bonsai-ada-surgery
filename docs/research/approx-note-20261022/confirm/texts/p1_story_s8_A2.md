It arrived in the mailbag on the ferry, tucked between a notice from the harbor authority and a damp packet of tea. He opened it by the kitchen stove, with the sea wind trying to get in.

"My name is Nora," it began. "I am not here yet. I am coming. Please keep the light on."

Elias read it twice. He was sixty-eight, had kept Greymouth Light for thirty-one years, and had long since stopped believing in messages from the sea. He thought of his daughter, long gone to the mainland. He thought of pranks, of the students who sometimes rowed out to mock the old lighthouse. He put the letter in the logbook drawer, beside the weather entries, and went up to polish the lens.

The next letter arrived three days later. "The light was bright tonight," it said. "I could almost see it. My mother says the island is gray, but your lamp is yellow. Can you tell me a story? I am afraid of the dark." Elias stared at the sentence. He had no mother in the letter. He had no daughter. He should have thrown it in the fire. Instead, by the next ferry, he had written a story about a fisherman who lost his boat but found a star. He signed only "E."

Nora replied: "Thank you, E. I read it three times. The fisherman's name was good. I hope my name will be good too."

That was when he first understood the impossible thing: the girl was not pretending to be someone else. She was speaking of herself before herself.

The letters continued with a regularity that frightened him. They came with the ferry, always on blue paper, sometimes with a small drawing: a wave, a bird, a circle of light. They told him things he could not know. "There is a crack in the west window," Nora wrote. "The wind sounds like a voice when it passes through it. Please mend it." Elias went up that morning. "Yes," she answered. "I am not yet. But I will be. Please do not be afraid. You are keeping a light for me."

He wrote to Nora: "I am afraid the light will be turned off. Will you still be coming?"

Her answer came quickly. "Then you must make it stay."

Elias did not know how to make a lighthouse stay. He was a man who cleaned, trimmed, and wound the clockwork. But he began to write letters to the harbor authority. That winter, a storm came. The kind of storm the island did not forget. The sea rose to the gallery steps. The ferry line was closed. ## final text

**The Unwritten Light**

On the last autumn before Greymouth Light was to be automated, Elias Wren received a letter from a girl who did not exist yet.

It arrived in the ferry mailbag, tucked between a notice from the harbor authority and a damp packet of tea. The envelope was blue, unmarked, and addressed in small pencil letters: *Mr. Wren, Keeper, Greymouth Light.* The handwriting was neat and slanting, like a child’s. Elias opened it by the kitchen stove while the sea wind pressed its cold nose against the door.

“My name is Nora,” it began. “I am not here yet. I am coming. Please keep the light on.”

He read it twice, then a third time, because the third time allowed him to pretend he had misunderstood. Elias was sixty-eight years old. He had kept Greymouth Light for thirty-one years. He knew every creak of the tower, every salt stain on the wall, every way the fog could lie to a man on the water. He did not know anything about a girl named Nora.

He thought of pranks. He thought of students rowing out from the mainland to mock the old lighthouse before it was sold for parts. He placed the letter in the drawer of the logbook, beneath the weather entries, and climbed the one hundred and thirty-two steps to polish the lens.

Three days later, another letter came.

“The light was bright tonight,” it said. “I could almost see it. My mother says the island is gray, but your lamp is yellow. Can you tell me a story? I am afraid of the dark.”

Elias stared at the page. There was no mother in the letter. There was no daughter in his cottage, no niece, no grandchild. He should have burned it. Instead, when the ferry came again, he sat at his table and wrote a story about a fisherman who lost his boat but found a star. He signed it only *E.*

Nora’s reply was quick.

“Thank you, E. I read it three times. The fisherman’s name was good. I hope my name will be good too.”

That was when the first true cold settled in Elias. The girl was not pretending to be anyone else. She was speaking of herself before herself.

The letters continued with a regularity that frightened him. They always came on blue paper, sometimes with a small drawing: a wave, a bird, a circle of light. They told him things he could not know.

“There is a crack in the west window,” Nora wrote. “The wind sounds like a voice when it passes through it. Please mend it.”

Elias went up the next morning. The crack was there, narrow and fresh, and when the wind moved through it, it made a sound like a word just before it was said.

“My mother is learning to sew,” Nora wrote. “She hums while she works. She does not know I am writing.”

Elias looked out from the gallery at the harbor. No ship was visible. He began to think the island was larger than he had known, a place that held more time than the mainland.

He wrote back. He told her about the lighthouse: how the lamp had been trimmed, how the lens turned, how the gulls wheeled in silver against the gray. He told her about the keeper before him, who had carved his initials into the railing and never lived to see his daughter grow up. He asked her, in a letter that felt foolish even as he folded it, if she was real.

“Yes,” she answered. “I am not yet. But I will be. Please do not be afraid. You are keeping a light for me.”

The harbor authority sent its official notice in November. The lighthouse would be automated in the spring. Elias read the paper at his kitchen table and felt the floor tilt beneath him. He had planned to leave after the winter, to go to his brother’s house in the city, where there would be no stairs and no foghorn and no one to ask for a story. But the letters made leaving feel like a breaking.

He wrote to Nora: “I am afraid the light will be turned off. Will you still be coming?”

Her answer came quickly. “Then you must make it stay.”

Elias did not know how to make a lighthouse stay. He was a man who cleaned, trimmed, and wound the clockwork. But he began to write letters to the harbor authority. He wrote about the island’s history, about the ships saved, about the fog lines and the rocks. He wrote with a new patience, as if his words were also a beam of light. He attached photographs. He asked for a meeting. They sent a young engineer who came by ferry and looked around the tower with skeptical eyes. Elias showed him every record, every repair, every storm log. He also showed him, carefully, the blue letters.

The engineer frowned. “These are charming.”

He did not believe them. Elias did not need him to. The delay came in the form of a report on structural concerns. The lighthouse would not be automated that spring.

That winter, a storm came. The kind of storm the island did not forget. The sea rose to the gallery steps. The ferry line was closed. Elias climbed the stairs with a lantern, though the lamp was burning bright. On the second landing he stopped, because a letter had appeared in his coat pocket, not in the mailbag but inside the wool, folded in the shape of a wave.

“The light is not enough,” it said. “There is a boat. Please go down.”

Elias knew he should not answer a ghost with his body. But the storm was knocking at the tower, and his legs went to the door. The rain was horizontal. The landing stage was black. He saw a shape in the surf, a rowboat thrown against the rocks, and he ran down the outside stairs, one hand on the railing, the other toward the sea.

The woman in the boat was heavily pregnant. The sea had torn the oars from her hands. Her face was pale, but her eyes were steady. She did not know his name. She said only, “I’m sorry. I was lost.”

Elias got her into the keeper’s house. He dried her by the stove. The radio crackled with the harbor office, but no vessel could come. By nightfall the woman was in labor. Elias, who had once pulled a sailor from a wreck and carried a wounded bird down to the village, did what was needed. He held her hands. He called for the midwife’s bag. He did not pray, because he was not sure who to ask.

When the baby came, she was small and red and screaming with a strength that seemed older than the house.

The letter in Elias’s pocket had changed.

“Thank you,” it said. “I am here.”

The woman’s name was Mara. She told him she had been following the light. She said she had always dreamed of this island, of a lamp turning in the dark, and that when the storm came she had known she had to row toward it. She did not explain the rest. Elias did not ask. He had learned, in his years of keeping, that some things were not made clearer by explanation.

Nora was born on the third day. She had the steady gaze of someone who had been watching the world before she was in it. Elias held her while the sea still crashed beyond the glass.

Mara looked at him. “Will you help us?”

He said, “I have been helping you all along.”

After the fever passed, the letters stopped. For a while Elias thought he had imagined them, or that they had been a kind of madness brought on by isolation and age. But he kept them in the logbook drawer, under the weather entries, and when Mara left the island in the spring with her child, he put a box of them in her hands.

“For when she is old enough,” he said.

Mara looked at the blue papers. “You received these before she was born?”

“Yes.”

She held them for a long time. “She has told me about a letter,” she said finally. “She says she wrote it before she existed. She says it was because someone needed to keep the light on.”

Elias did not smile. He was not ready for the joke, if that was the joke. He stood at the gallery window and watched the ferry take them out of sight. The light turned behind him, patient and white.

Years later, when Elias’s knees would no longer carry him up the full spiral, the lighthouse was automated at last. The harbor authority sent a man to remove the old fixtures. Elias was allowed to keep the logbook. The tower grew quiet. The glass lens stopped its slow turning, and the island became a place of visitors, photographs, and guided tours.

One October morning, a woman stood on the landing stage.

She was tall, with sea-colored hair and a coat darkened by rain. She carried a leather folder and the look of someone returning to a promise.

“Elias Wren?” she said.

He nodded.

“I’m Nora.”

He looked past her at the tower. The old lamp was dark, but in the gray afternoon a thin line of sun crossed the gallery window and held for a moment.

“You don’t need to come up,” he said. “It’s not the same.”

“I know.” She opened the folder. “I have something to show you. I’ve had these letters all my life. My mother gave them to me when I was small. You wrote to me before I was born.”

“I received letters from you before you were born.”

She laughed, a startled sound. “That is what I thought. I have carried the question like a stone. I don’t know how I wrote them. I was not there. I was not real. But I remember the light.”

Elias looked at her face and saw, for a second, the slanting hand that had asked for a story.

“I never understood how,” he said.

“I don’t either.” Nora closed the folder. “But the light was always on. I could feel it from the other side.”

He went to the door of the cottage and opened it. Behind him, on a shelf by the window, sat a small oil lamp, no larger than a lantern, the kind his father’s keeper had once used in bad weather. It was not enough for ships. It had never been meant for ships. It was only for the unwritten things, the lives not yet named, the voices waiting for a hand to answer.

Elias lit it.

The flame caught, and for a moment the whole island seemed to lean closer, listening.
