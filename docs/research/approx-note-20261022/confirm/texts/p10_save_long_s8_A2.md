# Two Ovens on Gull Street

The first thing you could smell on Gull Street was the sea. The second, if the wind came off the harbour and the tide was out, was yeast. For as long as the street had known either of them, the two smells had been arguing with one another, much as the people who made them had been arguing with one another — more gently, and for a much shorter time.

Miri Voss worked out of Salt & Rye, the old chandler's shop at the corner of Gull Street and the quay, its windows facing the water so that customers could see the boats going out before they decided on their bread. Three doors down, Thomas Bracken's bakehouse had stood on the corner for sixty years, its sign warped by salt air, its door a brass bell so green you could taste the patina. Thomas's oven was the town's oldest: a stone hearth his grandfather had built in 1921 out of the stone hauled up from the old harbour wall, which Thomas still fed with driftwood and patience. Miri's ovens were electric, stainless, and had a display of numbers. That was not a neutral fact. The town discussed it the way sailors discuss weather: seriously, and often, in the bar.

Miri was thirty-five. She had come from the city twelve years ago, after a fire had taken the bakery where she had been training — a fire she never spoke of, and which Thomas always brought up, politely, in the way a man tests the depth of a wound. Thomas was seventy-three. He had married a baker's daughter, lost her in a winter storm the year after Miri opened her shop, and had not since been able to raise his hands above a certain height without his joints complaining. He had never said he was angry at Miri. He had never said he was not.

The rivalry had a shape the town liked. Every year the Saltmere Guild held the Tide Festival in June, and every June the two bakers entered a loaf. For twelve years Thomas's bread had taken the Guild's single blue ribbon, and Miri's had come second eleven times. People drove in from towns across the coast specifically to argue. Children learned to read from the results pinned to the board above the pub's taps.

The year Miri came to Saltmere she had been twenty-three, she had flour in her hair, and she had made the terrible mistake of standing in the bakehouse doorway with a tray of cardamom buns and saying, in a voice that could have frozen a fish, "I think I'd like to buy your corner."

Thomas had looked at her through the steam-fogged window and said, "It's not for sale."

Then, because he was not a cruel man, and because the buns were, in fact, very good, "Come back tomorrow. Bring something you can eat with one hand."

She came back the next day.

The argument they had in May was the worst of the twelve years. They were standing across from one another outside the Guild hall, which had just announced that this year's festival would be judged by a panel from the National Bakers' Guild — three master bakers, a food writer, and the Guild's keeper, a woman called Elin Marsh, whom it was said could tell a loaf's region of origin by the smell of its crust alone.

"The same recipe," Miri said. "Seventeen years now, Thomas. That rye from '78. I'd recognise it in a blindfold."

"Your recipe changes every year," Thomas said. "No one can taste why. You're always trying to be somewhere else."

"I'm trying to be good. You're trying to be safe."

Thomas's jaw worked. "My grandfather built that oven from the stone they pulled up out of the old harbour wall. It has seen three storms that would have killed your little machines."

"My machines," she said, and for once she sounded almost frightened, "have made the best bread in this town, in order, for a season, a month, a week. And you call me machines."

"Because you are." He said it quietly. "A machine that's moving."

He walked away. The bell over his door did not ring; he had taken the mechanism out the year before, which Miri had always taken as a personal insult, though she knew it was because his hands ached in the cold.

What neither of them said, though both of them knew the other would hear, was that Miri had begun to understand that she had come to Saltmere to be somewhere safe.

The dough for the festival loaf was shaped and cold in its proofing basket: her rye with sea buckthorn and wild fennel, the most ambitious bake of her life, the one she'd spent three months perfecting. He showed her.

The stone oven was not a machine. It had moods. She could feel it in her palms.

"You see," he said. "The stone is like a tide. Push too early and the crust seizes. Too late and the crumb goes open. The middle has a width of a second."

"The middle," she said.

"Everyone has a middle. Yours is just wider than mine."

She almost laughed. It came out as a cough. "What are you doing, Thomas?"

"I'm trying not to."

She looked at him for a moment. His hands — she had always thought of them as steady, the hands of a man who had measured the same dough for fifty years — were not steady in the firelight. They trembled, fine and continuous, the way the sea trembles against a window glass.

"When did you start?" she asked.

"A while back."

"Since the winter?"

He did not answer, and the not-answering was the answer.

She put her loaf in the corner and said, "I've been trying to remember something for years, too."

He looked at her.

"The fire. In the city. For months after, I woke up trying to remember the recipe for a bread I'd never baked. Every day it was a different loaf. Thomas was quiet for a long time. The fire ticked and settled.

"My wife," he said, "baked a sea rye with caraway and honey. She did it for thirty years, and when she died the whole town asked for it. The fishmonger on the quay said it tasted like her. "And?"

"I keep coming back to my own hands. What I make is good. It's mine. It isn't hers. I can't close the gap."

"Then stop trying to close it. Change what you're measuring."

"My grandfather built that oven from the stone they pulled up out of the old harbour wall."

"Right. And she baked on a flat iron in a tin. The oven is in her bread, and your hands are in it too. You can't bake her out of it, Thomas. You can only bake her in."

It was not a good sentence. It was the truest thing either of them had said in twelve years, and they both knew it.

They baked through the night.

She did the shaping — her sea buckthorn rye, the loaf she had come to save, folded over his fire and given a crust with none of her usual neatness: deeper, wilder, cracked open down the middle like a river seen from a great height. "The distances are in the margin," he said. "I can't see them anymore."

She put on her glasses. They stood shoulder to shoulder over the counter, reading the hand of a woman neither of them had ever met, and he read to her as she read to him, one line at a time, until the recipe was no longer a memory but a set of hands.

When the tin was empty and the dough was cold again, Miri said, "We should bake one more."

"That's the third one."

"It is the point," she said. "Your base. Her caraway. My fennel. Salt from the quay, and the sea in the steam. A bread for the town now, and for the town that was. Let's bake the answer."

He did the kneading; she did the scoring. Or the other way round. It did not matter. What mattered was that both their hands were in the same dough, and that the dough no longer had to carry all the memory by itself.

By dawn there were three loaves on the counter: her sea buckthorn, dark with the jammy sweetness of the fruit; his caraway rye, honeyed, with a sound like a small bell when you tapped it; and the third, between them, the colour of the sea at dusk, scored in a pattern that was neither hers nor his and therefore both.

Thomas broke off a piece of the caraway rye and put it in her hand. She bit into it and stopped.

"It's hers," she said.

"It isn't hers," he said, and his voice was very small, but it was not an argument anymore. It was a truce, or a beginning. "It's both of them."

The festival had moved into the harbour itself: lights strung across the quay, boats moored with their names painted in peeling letters, and two baking carts set side by side — Miri's stainless van with the words *Salt & Rye* in hand-painted blue, and Thomas's old cart with *BRACKEN* faded to a ghost along its flank.
