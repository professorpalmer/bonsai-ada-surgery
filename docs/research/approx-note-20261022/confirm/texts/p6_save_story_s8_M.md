The Hesper had been cooking, in her own way, for four hundred and twelve years.

She had no flame, no wood, no open oven — those things had died with the first hull, along with every recipe that could not be written down. What she had was a galley at her heart: a small room with a single induction stove, a set of copper pots polished to a dull shine, and a recipe book. The book was the same book it had been at launch. The hand that wrote the first page was dead four centuries ago.

"Head cook" was the oldest title on the Hesper, older than the captain's chair, older than the first child born in the void. Maren had held it for six years. She was thirty-one. Her grandmother had held the title for thirty-one years before her. The Kessler line ran unbroken as far as the ship's records could see.

Most of the ship's meals were grown in the agricultural rings: pale, efficient, unremarkable. Algae loaves. Grain porridge. Protein prints that could be flavored to taste like anything, which was their own kind of horror. In the galley, Maren cooked things no one needed.

The tomato plant in Bay 7 was the exception.

It lived in a sealed grow bay the size of a closet, under a lamp that mimicked a sun no living person had ever seen. One plant. Two leaves a day in its youth. Maren spoke to it in the low voice she used for everything on the ship, and it grew — slow, green, patient. She fed it water the ship had spent two centuries desalinating. She fed it salt. She fed it light.

On the Hesper, salt was a kind of relic. The ship carried it in a ceramic jar from the launch ceremony — a pinch of the sea from a world no one could remember. Every meal Maren made ended with a single grain of that salt, dropped from her thumb. The ship's chefs would never understand what that cost. The ship's AI could not understand at all.

Arrival was in nine days.

News had spread through the ship the way it always did: first in the dining rings, then in the corridors, then in the sleepless hours at 3 a.m. when the lights dimmed and the ship's hum was all that was left of the day. Aurora was coming. Not a name for a destination, but a name for a promise. For four hundred years the Hesper had carried a world in her belly, and now the hull was opening.

The crew gathered every day in the atrium, and the captain made his speeches, and the children asked questions the adults had stopped asking. Maren stood at the back of the crowd with her hands at her sides, and she felt the same thing she had always felt in a room full of people she was supposed to feed: that she was the only one of them still hungry.

On the sixth day, the captain asked her to come to the bridge.

He was a quiet man, with the flat patience of someone who had been told the same thing about the future a thousand times and had learned to be grateful for it. He stood by the viewport, looking at a star that had a name and a distance and a soil composition, and he said, "We have the numbers."

Maren waited.

"The soil is good. The water is good. The air, after scrubbing, is good." He turned to her. "We don't have enough to feed two thousand people through a decade. The agricultural rings will not come up to speed fast enough. The first generation after landing will be thin."

Maren thought about thin. She thought about the pale, efficient, unremarkable meals of the agricultural rings. She thought about the protein prints that could be flavored to taste like anything, which was their own kind of horror.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked.

The captain looked at her for a long time. "I want you to cook," he said. "That's all I've ever been able to ask of you."

That night Maren went to Bay 7 and stood a long while in front of the plant. It was not ready. Two more weeks. She could feel it the way you feel a person breathing in a dark room.

She did not tell anyone. She told no one on the ship that Bay 7 was not sealed, or that the plant inside it had been, for four years, eating something it had never been told to eat — a handful of compost the captain had found in the old survey drones, the only organic matter the Hesper had ever carried, the first dirt of a world that was about to begin.

On the ninth day, the hull opened.

Maren had spent the previous night writing a new page in the recipe book. The pen was the same pen. The ink was the same ink. She wrote:

Tomato, first harvest.
Water: clean, warm.
Light: what you have.
Salt: what you can afford to keep.
Seed: one, saved.
Hunger: whatever remains.
Yield: enough to start.

When the atrium doors opened and the light came in — real light, not lamp light, not star light, but the light of a morning that had not yet decided what it was going to be — Maren stood at the back of the crowd with the plant in her arms.

The captain's face changed. The children's faces changed. For a moment, no one spoke.

Then a girl of maybe ten years old reached out and touched a leaf.

"Maren," the captain said. "What is that?"

"It's a tomato," Maren said. "It's the first of the first kind. It will be ready in two weeks. Maybe three." She looked at the light. "I need soil."

"We have soil," the captain said. "We have a lot of it."

Maren nodded. She had expected to feel something — joy, or triumph, or the relief of a job finally done. Instead she felt the thing she always felt, the thing that kept her up at 3 a.m. when the lights dimmed: hunger. Not for food. For the meal that would come next.

She planted the tomato in the first row of the first garden, in the first soil of the first world, with the pinch of sea salt from a world no one could remember, and she whispered to it the way she always did:

"Grow."

And for four hundred and twelve years, the Hesper had been cooking, in her own way, for everyone who would ever eat.

The first tomato ripened on a morning that looked, to no one, like any other morning on a new world. It was small and imperfect and red in the way that only something true can be red.

Maren cut it in half. She gave it to a child.

The child closed her mouth, and for a long moment there was nothing else in the world but the taste.

Then Maren went back to the kitchen, and she began to plan dinner.

# The Last Tomato

The Hesper had been cooking, in her own way, for four hundred and twelve years.

She had no flame, no wood, no open oven — those things had died with the first hull, along with every recipe that could not be written down. What she had was a galley at her heart: a small room with a single induction stove, a set of copper pots polished to a dull shine, and a recipe book. The book was the same book it had been at launch. The hand that wrote the first page was dead four centuries ago.

"Head cook" was the oldest title on the Hesper, older than the captain's chair, older than the first child born in the void. Maren had held it for six years. She was thirty-one. Her grandmother had held the title for thirty-one years before her, and the Kessler line ran unbroken as far as the ship's records could see.

Most of the ship's meals were grown in the agricultural rings: pale, efficient, unremarkable. Algae loaves. Grain porridge. Protein prints that could be flavored to taste like anything, which was their own kind of horror. In the galley, Maren cooked things no one needed — and everything else needed.

Every seventh day, a small crowd gathered in the atrium and waited for the galley doors to open. Maren made things that took hours: a broth that had been simmering since before the children were awake, bread that rose in a warm corner of the bay and filled the ship with its smell. The people who came were the people on the ship who had the least to eat. That was not by accident. That had always been how it worked.

The tomato plant in Bay 7 was the exception.

It lived in a sealed grow bay the size of a closet, under a lamp that mimicked a sun no living person had ever seen. One plant. Two leaves a day in its youth. Maren spoke to it in the low voice she used for everything on the ship, and it grew — slow, green, patient. She fed it water the ship had spent two centuries desalinating. She fed it salt. She fed it light.

On the Hesper, salt was a kind of relic. The ship carried it in a ceramic jar from the launch ceremony — a pinch of the sea from a world no one could remember. Every meal Maren made ended with a single grain of that salt, dropped from her thumb. The people who made the protein prints would never understand what that cost. The ship's computer could not understand at all.

Her grandmother had died in her sleep, in the old quarters of the agricultural rings, and she had left Maren the copper ladle and an instruction that had taken years to understand: *the book is not a record. It is a promise.* A promise is not something you keep by reading. A promise is something you keep by doing.

Arrival was in nine days.

News had spread through the ship the way it always did: first in the dining rings, then in the corridors, then in the sleepless hours at 3 a.m. when the lights dimmed and the ship's hum was all that was left of the day. Aurora was coming. Not a name for a destination, but a name for a promise. For four hundred years the Hesper had carried a world in her belly, and now the hull was opening.

The ship's computer had been calculating the landing since launch. Now, in the final days, it had gone quiet. Maren understood what quiet meant from a machine that had never spoken once. It meant that the math was done.

The crew gathered every day in the atrium, and the captain made his speeches, and the children asked questions the adults had stopped asking. Some of the adults had stopped coming. They said they were tired, or that the atrium was too bright. Maren left the hearth meal on the table anyway. The bread would rise. The broth would keep. If no one came, the printers would take it and make it into something else, and no one would taste it at all.

Maren stood at the back of the crowd with her hands at her sides, and she felt the same thing she had always felt in a room full of people she was supposed to feed: that she was the only one of them still hungry.

On the sixth day, the captain asked her to come to the bridge.

He was a quiet man, with the flat patience of someone who had been told the same thing about the future a thousand times and had learned to be grateful for it. He stood by the viewport, looking at a star that had a name and a distance and a soil composition, and he said, "We have the numbers."

Maren waited.

"The soil is good. The water is good. The air, after scrubbing, is good." He turned to her. "We don't have enough to feed two thousand people through a decade. The agricultural rings will not come up to speed fast enough. The first generation after landing will be thin."

Maren thought about *thin*. She thought about the pale, efficient, unremarkable meals of the agricultural rings. She thought about the protein prints that could be flavored to taste like anything, which was their own kind of horror.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked.

The captain looked at her for a long time. "I want you to cook," he said. "That's all I've ever been able to ask of you. I know what a hungry ship is. I have been one. Cook for them like that, Maren. Cook for them like they'll be here forever."

That night Maren went to Bay 7 and stood a long while in front of the plant. It was not ready. Two more weeks. She could feel it the way you feel a person breathing in a dark room.

She did not tell anyone what Bay 7 held, or that the plant inside it had been, for four years, eating something it had never been told to eat — a handful of compost from the old survey probes, the only organic matter the Hesper had ever carried, the first dirt of a world that was about to begin.

Two nights later, Maren sat at the galley table and wrote a new page in the recipe book. The pen was the same pen. The ink was the same ink.

> *Tomato, first harvest.*
> *Water: clean, warm.*
> *Light: what you have.*
> *Salt: what you can afford to keep.*
> *Seed: one, saved.*
> *Hunger: whatever remains.*
> *Yield: enough to start.*

On the ninth day, the hull opened.

When the atrium doors opened and the light came in — real light, not lamp light, not star light, but the light of a morning that had not yet decided what it was going to be — Maren stood at the back of the crowd with the plant in her arms.

"Maren," the captain said. "What is that?"

"It's a tomato," she said. "It will be red in a month, maybe two." She looked at the light. "I need soil."

"We have soil," the captain said. "We have a lot of it."

Maren nodded. She had expected to feel something — joy, or triumph, or the relief of a job finally done. Instead she felt the thing she always felt, the thing that kept her up at 3 a.m. when the lights dimmed: hunger. Not for food. For the meal that would come next.

She planted the tomato in the first row of the first garden, in the first soil of the first world, with the pinch of sea salt from a world no one could remember, and she whispered to it the way she always did: *Grow.*

And for four hundred and twelve years, the Hesper had been cooking, in her own way, for everyone who would ever eat.

The first tomato ripened on a morning that looked, to no one, like any other morning on a new world. It was small and imperfect and red in the way that only something true can be red.

Maren cut it in half. She gave it to a child.

The child closed her mouth, and for a long moment there was nothing else in the world but the taste.

By the first winter, the garden had a name. The children called it the Hesper's table — not after the ship, but after the galley, which was still open, which still smelled of salt and steam and four centuries of copper. Maren came out every morning with her hands in the dirt. She came back every evening with something to cook. The plant from the old bay had become four plants, and four had become a row, and the row was learning, the way everything on the new world was learning, to be bigger than the day it was planted.

Then Maren went back to the kitchen, and she began to plan dinner.
