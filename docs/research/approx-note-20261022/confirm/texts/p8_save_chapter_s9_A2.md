The city of Ondo had no foundation.

Not a cellar, not bedrock, not even the bottom of a well where the earth finally stopped. Every street, every market square, every rooftop bell hung on a single, enormous thing: the back of a sea turtle the size of a district. The old names for him varied. The streets ran along the ridges between the armor plates, their edges worn smooth by ten thousand generations of feet. There was no underground, no bed, no "beneath." To a child of Ondo, the word *below* meant the water, and the water was a thing you stared at from the edge and never fell into.

On the morning of the sixty-first tide, Mara Vane was on her knees on the second ridge, polishing the largest plate the city had ever seen. The plate was the color of old ivory, and at its center the skin of a scar rose like a closed eye. Her master, old Fen, had told her that this scar was where the last ship had tried to nail a mast to Omo's back, and that the nail had never stopped singing.

"Mara, put down the polish."

She looked up. Fen stood at the edge of the ridge, his beard braided with rope, his hands red with the ink he used to read the shell's patterns.

"What is it, Master?"

"The Hush."

The Hush was a word that children used for the silence before a storm. But Fen's face was not the face of a man describing weather. He pointed to the water below the city's rim, a black line that stretched in every direction, and Mara saw that the waves had stopped breaking against the turtle's flanks. There was no foam. There was no crash. There was only a stillness so complete it felt pressed against her ears, and in that stillness, something was moving far below the city—a shadow so large it blocked the light, and something else: a slow, rising hum that she felt in her teeth before she heard it.

"Omo is diving," Fen said.

Every forty years, or so, the turtle would dive. The city of Ondo would tip and shake, and every rope and chain would be tested, and then the tide would carry them back up to the surface, and the old ones would say, "He only dreamed." But Omo had been diving every forty years for four hundred years, and none of them had ever once come back from one of the dives with the shell changed.

Except this time.

As Mara watched, the hum rose, and the plates beneath her knees began to warm, and the scar in the center of the largest plate—the scar that had been closed for four centuries—opened a crack, no wider than a finger.

"Fen."

# Chapter 1: The City Without a Foundation

The city of Ondor had no foundation.

Not a cellar. Not bedrock. Not even the bottom of a well where the earth would finally stop and you could press your back against it and be sure the world held. Everything in Ondor rested on a single, immense thing: the carapace of a sea turtle the size of a quarter city, older than every dynasty that had ever pitched a tent on his shell. The fishermen called him Omo. The poets called him the Patient Deep. Everyone who lived on his back knew the true thing, which was that he was a creature with a mind and a long memory, and that none of them had any say in what he chose to do.

The streets ran along the raised seams between his armor plates, their edges worn smooth by ten thousand generations of feet. The houses were built of coral, driftwood, and tarred rope, their eaves weighted with chain that bit into the shell so nothing would lift off in a storm. Nothing here could be dug down. There was no below, no basement, no ground. For a child of Ondor, the word *under* simply meant the water, and the water was a thing you stared at from the rim and never went into.

At dawn the city woke the way any island wakes: with bells. The bells were lashed to the ridge-ribs of the shell, and when the tide rose, they swayed and rang together in a low, rolling peal that the children called the Sea-Singing. Three thousand families lived on the shell. The poor lived at the water's edge, where the houses were small and the doors were lashed shut against spray. The rich lived near the center, where the air lay still and the rope-bells hummed all day. Everyone knew which direction was down: toward the sea.

On the morning of the sixty-first tide, Mara Vane was on her knees on the second ridge, polishing the greatest plate the city had ever seen. It was the color of old ivory, and at its center the scar rose like a closed eye. Her master, old Fen, had told her the scar was where the last ship had tried to drive a mast into Omo's back, and that the nail had never stopped ringing.

"Mara, put the polish down."

She looked up. Fen stood at the rim, his beard braided with rope, his hands stained with the ink he used to read the shell's patterns.

"What is it, Master?"

"The Hush."

The Hush was what children called the silence before a storm. But Fen's face was not the face of a man describing weather. He pointed to the water beneath the city's edge, a black line running out in every direction, and Mara saw that the waves had stopped breaking against the turtle's flanks. No foam. No crash. Only a stillness so complete it seemed to press against her ears, and in that stillness something was moving far below the city. A shadow. A shadow so wide it stole the light, and with it, a slow rising hum she felt in her teeth before her ears found it.

"Omo is diving."

Every forty years the turtle dove. The city would tilt and groan, every rope and chain would be tested, and then the tide would carry them back up to the surface and the elders would say, *He only dreamed*. Omo had dove every forty years for four centuries, and no one had ever come home from a dive with the shell changed.

Except this time.

As Mara watched, the hum rose, and the plates beneath her knees began to warm. The scar at the center of the great plate — the scar that had been shut for four hundred years — split open along a hairline crack, no wider than a finger.

From the crack, something rose.

A hand. Pale, webbed, with fingers far too long for a turtle. It touched the plate, and Mara saw, with a coldness that stopped her breath, that it was not reaching up from Omo. It was reaching up from under him. As if something else were beneath the city, and it had been waiting a very long time to be touched.

"Fen."

The old man had already stepped back from the rim, his face the color of bone. He looked at the hand. He looked at the water. And he said the words every child of Ondor heard once in a life — the only words in the world with no other meaning, no excuse, no way to pretend they were a joke.

"The deep is waking."

Below the city, the sea began to glow.
