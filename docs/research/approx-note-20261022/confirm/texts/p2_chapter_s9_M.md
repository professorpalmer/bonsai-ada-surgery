# Chapter One  
## The Conductor’s Last Mile

The rain in Copper Creek had a memory.

It came down the Kettle Range at the same hour each spring, soft and patient, slipping through the pines and finding every crack in the old gravel road that wound toward the river. It knew where the stone walls leaned, where the creek swollen over its banks, and which house in town kept its windows open because the owner had never learned to live in winter. Elias Crane knew it too. He had known it long enough to feel the change in the air before the first drops touched the porch boards.

At seventy-four, he still rose at five, though the need for early rising had long since been reduced from a job requirement to a habit. For thirty-two years he had walked the Copper Ridge Line in the dark, wearing a cap with a brass badge that caught the headlamp glare, his eyes trained on the curve of the rails, the stand of the switches, the distant flicker of signals. He had conducted passengers from the mountain town of Copper Creek to the lowland city of Ashford, and he had learned the language of trains in the same way other men learned the language of the land: by sound, by pressure, by the small wrongness that came before a disaster.

He stood now in the kitchen of the old depot house, where the walls were still paneled in worn cedar and the floor creaked with the memory of feet that had carried parcels, tickets, and news. A cup of coffee cooled in a chipped blue mug. On the table beside it lay a folded newspaper with a headline about water rights and a letter from his daughter in Denver asking if he still dreamed about the old 4:17. He had not answered that one.

The dog, a mutt of no particular breed named Biscuit, lifted his head from the hearth and listened.

Elias listened too.

Outside, the rain was doing something it had not done in years. It was not falling evenly. The sound came in long, dragging swells, as if the mountain were exhaling through a mouthful of stone. Then, faintly beneath it, a different sound: a soft scraping, like gravel being dragged across gravel.

His head turned toward the window before his mind had finished deciding to be afraid.

Below the house, past the kitchen garden and the broken gate, the old cut yawned in the hillside. It was a shallow trench of dirt and crushed rock where the Copper Ridge Line had once followed the bend in the river. Most people in town called it the Old Cut, as if that explained everything. They did not see it the way Elias saw it. To him it was not a hole in the mountainside. It was a line of tracks with the rails pulled, the ties split, the ballast turned over and left to rot. It was the place where the train had died thirty years before he retired, and the place where a dozen people had learned that the mountain kept what it took.

There was something red in the cut.

Not red like the autumn maples. Not red like the old paint on the water tank. It was a wet, dark red, the color of something that had been there a while and was still trying to move.

Elias crossed the kitchen and pulled back the curtain. The shape was below the eaves, half hidden by the slope, but the rain had washed the dirt away from it. A coat. A boot. The pale angle of an arm.

He did not run. He took his coat from the hook by the door, the old conductor’s coat with the brass buttons polished bright by decades of thumbs, and he went down the porch steps. The mud sucked at his boots. Biscuit followed, low and uneasy, his tail tucked against his leg.

Elias stopped at the edge of the cut and looked down.

The dead man lay on his side, face turned partly toward the old river bed. His coat was dark green, soaked and heavy. His hair was gray and matted to his forehead. He wore a wool hat, though the morning was not cold enough for it, and one boot had been removed. The other remained on his foot, black leather, the heel worn flat.

Elias felt the old familiar tightening in his chest, the same one he had felt a hundred times before he ever had the right to wear a uniform. The body was placed there. Not fallen there. Placed.

He went down.

The cut was narrow and slick with runoff. He kept his hand on a loose root as he passed the place where the rails had once been, where the sleepers still jutted from the earth like broken teeth. The man’s face was visible enough. Elias leaned closer, shielding his eyes from the gray sky.

Middle age, maybe fifty. A square jaw, a broken nose, a small scar above the left eyebrow. The lips were blue at the edges. His hands were closed into fists.

Elias crouched.

The left hand was clenched tight. He tried to open it. The fingers would not give. He touched the knuckles. Cold. He waited a moment. The fingers stiffened further, not from life but from the final certainty of death.

“Hold still,” he said softly.

Biscuit whined and backed up the slope.

Elias pried the fingers one by one, not with force, but with the patient pressure of a man who had once opened the doors of a crowded passenger car in the dark. The fist opened slowly, revealing a small brass object resting in the palm.

It was a token.

Not the round paper fare tokens from the ticket window. This was heavier, thicker, the kind a conductor carried to validate tickets, stamped with an engraving so fine it had once made Elias’s thumb ache when he rubbed it with too much soap. The face of it was dull, but the reverse caught the weak light.

There was a number: 4-17.

Below it, a date: March 4, 1987.

Elias stared at it.

The number of a train that no longer existed. The date of a day that had not been a day since he had been younger and the mountain had not yet taught him to keep its silence.

His hand, which had been steady enough to pry open a dead man’s fist, trembled.

“Mr. Crane?”

The voice came from above the cut. A woman’s voice, sharp and close.

Elias looked up.

Sheriff Dana Whitfield stood at the top of the slope, one hand on her flashlight, the other holding the radio at her shoulder. She was not wearing her coat. Rain ran down her neck and the collar of her uniform shirt. Her eyes moved from him to the cut, and something in her face tightened.

“I saw a shape in the rain,” Elias said. “I came to look.”

Dana descended quickly, careful with the mud. Biscuit had followed at a distance. The sheriff stopped at the body, and Elias saw her glance at the brass token in his hand.

“Jesus,” she murmured.

“Who is it?” Elias asked, though he did not want to know.

“Name’s Colton Vane,” she said. “He was seen leaving the mine office about three hours ago. No one else saw him after that.”

Elias turned back to the dead man. Colton Vane. The name meant nothing to him, and yet it fit. He had heard it in the general store the week before. Vane had come into town from Ashford with a surveyor’s case and a smile that did not reach his eyes. He had been asking about the old tunnel, the abandoned mine, the stretch of the Copper Ridge Line that the county had promised to fence off and leave alone. People in Copper Creek did not like questions about the mine. The mine had been closed for twenty years, but it had not stopped being there. It sat beneath the town like a tooth under the gums, waiting to hurt.

Dana crouched beside the body. “No signs of struggle,” she said. “At first look. His throat is closed, but there’s no cut. Maybe a heart attack.”

“Maybe.”

She looked at him. “You sound like you know more than you’re saying.”

“I’m a retired conductor,” Elias said. “I read ground.”

Dana gave a short laugh, but there was no humor in it. “Explain.”

Elias did not answer at once. He looked down the cut, at the slope, at the old riverbed where the water had been pulled back by the rain. The body lay on the shoulder of the cut, not on the track bed. That was wrong. If a man had fallen from the slope, the angle of the cut and the way the earth had sloughed would have carried him lower, not left him there. If he had been dragged, there should be scratches. There were none. If he had been placed, someone had come from above and walked away without disturbing the surface.

Elias pointed to the mud below the body. “Look here.”

Dana followed his finger.

In the soft earth, barely visible, were marks. Not boot prints, not exactly. They were smears, shallow grooves, the impressions of something dragged or set down with care. One of them ended at the body’s hip. Another began near the top of the cut.

“Someone carried him down,” she said.

“Or laid him out,” Elias said. “The way you lay a thing so it looks like it belongs.”

Dana’s face changed. She reached for the radio, then stopped.

“Hold on.”

“Who?” Elias asked.

“Who what?”

“You asked me if I knew more. I’m asking you.”

She looked at him for a moment, then back at the body. “Colton Vane’s been in Copper Creek for two days. Bought a room at the Mill Inn. Asked about the old rail line. Asked about the mine. Asked who owned the cut. People got nervous.”

“Nervous is a small word for what I’ve seen.”

“Nervous is a big word for what a lot of people in town are.” She touched her radio again. “I need to call it in. Medical, and maybe a coroner if you’ll forgive me.”

Elias nodded, but his eyes were still on the token in his hand.

4-17.

March 4, 1987.

He had not thought of that day in thirty years. Not since the night the 4:17 had come around the Black Pine curve with its lamps burning and the conductor’s cab empty. Not since the fire that had taken the engine before the brakes could stop it. Not since he had stood in the mud at the bottom of the cut and listened to the men talk about what had caused it. The official report had blamed the switch. The unofficial ones had blamed a dozen things, and none of them had satisfied him.

He had been thirty-four that night. The train had been his responsibility. The curve had been his responsibility. The silence after had been his responsibility too.

“You’re quiet,” Dana said.

“I’m thinking.”

“About what?”

He looked at the dead man’s face again. The scar above his eyebrow. The square jaw. The way the gray hair had been combed back despite the rain. “About the fact that this man is holding a conductor’s token from a train that doesn’t exist.”

Dana frowned. “A train?”

“The 4-17.”

“The 4:17? There’s still a 4:17 bus out of town.”

“No.” Elias almost smiled. “Not on the Copper Ridge. Not since the old line.”

Dana studied the token in his hand. “Where did you get it?”

“Out of the dead man’s fist.”

She was quiet for a second. Then she said, very carefully, “Did you check his pocket?”

“He has pockets. They’re wet.”

“Check them.”

Elias leaned down and reached into the inner pocket of Vane’s coat. The cloth was slick. He felt a folded paper. A wallet. A small metal key with a leather loop. The paper first.

He unfolded it.

It was a ticket.

Not paper fare, but a passenger order, printed on stiff stock with a faint blue rule down the side. The ink had bled at the edges, but the words were still sharp.

**COPPER RIDGE LINE**  
**One Fare**  
**Reserved: E. Crane**  
**From: Copper Creek**  
**To: Ashford**  
**Time: 4:17**  
**Do not sell.**

Elias looked up.

Dana’s face had gone pale.

“Your name?” she said.

“I know.”

She stared at him. “That’s impossible.”

“Not to me.”

The rain fell harder. The old cut gurgled with water. Somewhere above them, a crow began to cry, thin and persistent, and then stopped.

Elias turned the ticket over. On the reverse, in a small hand that had once used a pen or perhaps a pencil, were two words written in the same blue ink.

**For the last conductor.**

He looked at the brass token again. 4-17. March 4, 1987. The day of the fire. The day he had been a young man on the last train before the line was closed. The day he had not been able to save it.

Or maybe he had, and did not know it.

“Mr. Crane,” Dana said, and her voice had lost its authority. “How do you know what this is?”

He wanted to lie. He wanted to say, I don’t. I’m an old man and this is a trick. He wanted to say, The dead man must have found it in an attic, or stolen it, or been part of some old joke I never heard about. But the truth had been sitting in his hands all along, cold and heavy and brass.

“I know because I was there,” he said. “When the 4:17 burned.”

Dana did not move.

Above them, the mountain stood wet and patient. Below, the river moved in the dark with the sound of something turning over in sleep. And in Elias Crane’s hand, the token and the ticket waited, as if they had been carried for thirty years only to arrive at this moment, in this cut, in this rain, with a dead man’s fist opening like a door.
