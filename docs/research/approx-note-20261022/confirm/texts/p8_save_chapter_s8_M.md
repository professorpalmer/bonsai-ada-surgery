**Chapter One: The First Bell**

The city of Velmara woke with its mother.

Every morning, before the first gull cried out, before the bakers lit the coals in their stone ovens, before the fishermen climbed up from the tide-caves, the whole city stirred at the same low rumble that rose up through the cobblestones and through the soles of everyone's boots. It was a sound older than the oldest stone in Velmara: the heartbeat of Orem, the turtle the size of a mountain, who had been crossing the deep sea of the world for longer than anyone could say.

His shell was the ground. The city was built on it.

In the morning, the shell plates looked like a vast field of slate and moss, and between them ran the deep fissures that the city had learned to live with. Velmara had been built in the cracks and along the ridges, not on top of the plate. The houses were wedged between the ridges, the bridges were built over the fissures, and the great cathedral, built of coral and bone, arched across a fissure so wide it swallowed its own shadow. The people called it the Mouth. When the sea mist rose up from there at dawn, it smelled of brine and iron, and the priests said it was Orem breathing in his sleep.

At the front of the shell, the city was new: bright stone, new roofs, the bell towers still warm to the touch. At the rear, it was old beyond reckoning. There the moss grew thick in the cracks, the houses sank slowly into the softening shell, and the oldest streets ran into the Moss Quarter, where the walls were soft and the windows were overgrown, and the elders said that one day, when the moss reached the roof, the whole of the city would be swallowed into the shell. Nobody believed this. No one wanted to live there, but everyone was afraid of it.

I was twelve that day, and I was the first to be awake. Not the first in the whole city — the first in the whole of the Lower Ward. I had been born in the city, so the only thing I knew about Orem's body was what the city was: the ridges, the fissures, the way the shell curved and rose and curved again, the way the whole world swayed like a sleeping animal. I had never seen him outside the city. No one had ever seen him. The sea around us was called the Dark, and no one had ever paddled out into it.

My job was the bell.

It was not a large bell, not by Velmara standards. It was the size of a barrel, hung from a crane of old coral in the square of the Lower Ward, and every morning at first light I climbed the rope ladder, turned the great iron wheel, and let it swing. The bell was not to be heard in the city. The bell was to be heard by Orem. Or so the elders said.

"He wakes to it," my father used to tell me, before the fever took him. "Not the city wakes to him. He wakes to the bell."

My father had been a plate-watcher, one of the men who climbed the ridges with their ears pressed to the shell to listen for changes in Orem's gait. He had come back from his last walk quieter than usual, and from then on he had stopped walking the ridges and started fixing things instead. I had watched him climb the rope ladder and turn the wheel, and I had learned the climb from him, rung after rung. I thought it was the only job in the city worth doing. I am still not sure I was wrong.

I had not believed him about the waking. I believe it now.

Today was the day of the first turning. Every year, at the first light of the spring, Orem changed his course. For one thousand years, the city's priests had said, he had been walking toward the north star. For one thousand years, the city had grown in that direction — the new districts built at the front of his shell, the old districts sinking slowly into the moss at the rear. Today, the priests said, he would turn south. Today, the whole of Velmara would begin to move in a new direction, and I would be the one to sound the bell.

The rope ladder was slick with mist. I climbed carefully, the bell swinging gently behind me, and at the top I could see the whole of the Lower Ward in the grey light — the roofs, the chimneys, the little gardens where my mother used to grow sea-cress in the warm cracks of the shell plates. Everything was quiet. The whole city was quiet. Even the gulls were silent.

I turned the wheel.

The first swing was small. The bell rang, and the sound went out across the square and up and over the cathedral, and I felt it in my teeth before I heard it. The second swing was bigger. The bell rang again, and this time I heard the city wake — the doors opening, the women at the wells, the fishermen calling each other up from their tide-caves. The whole of Velmara stirred.

The third swing.

And then I heard it.

Not the heartbeat. Not the low rumble that everyone knew. Something else. Something new. A sound deep below the heartbeat, as if something inside the turtle was turning over. A creak. A groan. The whole of the city shuddered. The rope ladder shook. The bell swung.

I stopped.

Below me, in the Lower Ward, the gulls began to cry. Not the normal cry. A loud, sharp cry, the cry birds make when they see something coming. All of them, at once, taking to the sky.

The mist from the Mouth was rising faster now, and it was not grey. It was red.

I turned the wheel again. The bell swung, and I pulled the rope, and it rang.

And below me, for the first time in my life, the city of Velmara held its breath.
