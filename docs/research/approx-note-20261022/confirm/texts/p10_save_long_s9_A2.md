"Come in," she said.

## IV. Dawn

Nora's bakery at dawn was a small warm place. The stone floor held the heat of the oven. The shelves were lined with jars — flours, salt, honey, rye — and the wood oven was a big thing, older than Nora, older than her mother, older than the street. Its mouth was open, the fire low and steady.

Nora set the jar of Old Brine in a bowl of warm water. Elias stood behind her and watched her hands — the way she touched the surface, the way she tilted the bowl to watch the bubbles, the way she knew by feel what her eyes could not see.

"She's tired," Nora said. "Livia named her. I think she got old and stubborn, the way she was."

"How long does it take?"

"A day, sometimes two. You don't push her. You just keep her warm."

"That's a strange way to run a business."

"That's a strange way to bake."

He laughed. It was the first time either of them had laughed in the bakery, and the sound of it was so strange in the little warm room that they both stopped.

They worked. Nora made her rye, the salted rye with the molasses edge, and as she did it she felt for the thing she had never had — the recipe on the blackened card, the one she had built from memory over thirty years. She knew the feel of it: how the dough should hold, how it should smell, how it should crack when it came out of the oven. She had never had the card. She had made it from memory, and memory, she had learned, was a recipe too.

Elias worked his brioche — the sea-salt brioche with the glaze that shone like a wet oyster. He worked fast, with the precision of a man trained in city kitchens, and Nora watched him fold and turn and press, and she saw the difference between his hands and hers: his were precise, hers were patient. Both were right.

As they worked, they talked. They had never talked before. They talked about the town, the ferry, the breakwater, the price of flour, the weather. They talked about the bread, the fire, the cold, and the way the sea came in at night. And slowly, without either of them meaning to, they talked about the letter.

It came out of Nora the way things do — a slip, a half-sentence. "My grandmother wrote a letter," she said, not looking up from the dough. "She wrote it twenty years ago. She never sent it."

Elias stopped folding. "For who?"

Nora looked at him. She had the look again — tired, and proud, and not quite herself.

"Your grandmother," she said.

The room went quiet. The fire cracked. Old Brine began to bubble.

"I found it after she died," Nora said. "In a tin box under the counter. I've carried it for twenty years." She went to the counter and opened the tin. The letter was in there, yellow with age, Livia's shaking handwriting, crossed out and begun again. "I never sent it. I don't know why. I was too angry, or too ashamed. I never could tell which."

She looked at Elias.

"But I think it's time."

Elias took the letter. He read it. The words were short, and honest, and the paper was soft from being handled.

"Thank you," he said, quietly.

They put it in an envelope. They addressed it. They did not send it that day. But it was no longer in a tin box under a counter. It was in Elias's hand, and it would go to the city, to Cécile's apartment, to a woman who had not known a letter was coming.

At seven, Old Brine woke. It bubbled and it smelled sweet, and it was ready.

Nora looked at it and smiled. "She's up."

"That's what you call her."

"Old Brine."

"Of course."

They went back to work.

## V. First Loaf

The day of the First Loaf came up bright and cold, the kind of morning that made the harbor look like a bowl of water. The town came early, the way it always did, and the hall was full by nine. The long table was set. The sea wall was dry. And the corner by the door, where the old community oven had stood for a hundred years, was bare.

Nora and Elias stood side by side with two loaves. Nora's salted rye, dark and dense, with the molasses edge and the crack that came like a shell. Elias's brioche, golden and flaky, with the glaze that shone like a wet oyster. Both had been baked in the wood oven on Gull Street, by the fire that had kept them warm all night. Both had crossed the street.

The hall went quiet when they set the loaves on the table.

The town ate. The fishermen and the fishwives took Nora's rye first, and the tourists and the young people took Elias's brioche. And then, slowly, without anyone asking, everyone took both. Rye on one side, brioche on the other. Salt and sweet. Dark and gold. The table filled, the queue filled, and the hall filled with people who had been hungry for thirty-five years and had not known it.

There was no judge. There was no verdict. The First Loaf had always been decided by the queue, and the queue, for the first time in memory, was both of them.

Nora looked at Elias. Elias looked at Nora. They both smiled.

The First Loaf was held again the following year, and the year after, and the year after that. Two bakeries. One table. Two loaves.

And in the city, in an apartment on a street that smelled of the sea, Cécile Marchand opened a letter. The handwriting was shaking, and the words were crossed out and begun again, and the letter was honest. She read it twice. She read it a third time. She went to the window and looked out at the water and thought about the hall, and the bread, and the friend she had thought was gone.

She wrote a letter back.

It reached Wren's Cove a week later. It was short and it was kind. *I remember. I was wrong. The card was never yours. The bread was never yours. You were my friend. — C.*

Nora read it the morning it arrived. She read it a dozen times. She put it in the tin box beside the other letter and closed the lid.

The bakery was warm. The oven was warm. Old Brine was awake.

And the two bakers on Gull Street stood at the counter and sold the bread, and the line was long, and the town was full.
