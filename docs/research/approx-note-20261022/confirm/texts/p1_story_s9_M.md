He was seventy years old, and the island had a way of making his bones feel borrowed. The sea came in through the door of the tower in a low voice, as if it were asking permission to stay. He had been its keeper for forty years, and before that he had been a boy who ran errands for the man who came before him. In all that time, no one had ever sent him a letter by air or by post, except the government, the supply company, and one widow from the mainland who had written after her husband drowned.
Then, on a Tuesday in November, a letter arrived. It was in the brass slot, small and folded twice, with no stamp and no name. The address was written in his own handwriting: Mr. Elias Vane, Keeper, Gull Island Light, before the road was cut and after the house was burned. He had never burned a house on that island.
Inside, the page was thin as onion skin, and the writing was small, careful, and strangely adult.

Dear Mr. Vane,
My name is Nora. I am not yet born, but I am already afraid of fog. Please keep the light clean.
Nora.

He read it three times before the wind changed and made the paper sound like a held breath. He put it in his coat pocket and went back to polishing the lens. The light turned overhead, a white wheel in the dark, and he thought: someone is trying to frighten me. But the island was a single stone above the sea, reached by a boat that only came twice a month, and no boat had come since October. If a letter could be carried in the fog, then the fog must have a hand.

The next letter came on a Wednesday. It smelled of rain, though no rain had fallen in a week. It said: The light must not stutter on the night of the 17th. When the moon is hidden, the ship called the Cresswill will come from the west, and its captain will not see you until he is too late. Make the lamp burn bright.
Elias wrote the date on the wall with chalk. The 17th was the night of the equinox gale, and the Cresswill was a trawler he had known well. He had seen it pass with the lantern low and the crew singing. He told himself the letter was a dream or a trick of the old mind. That night, he climbed higher than he had in years and added oil to the lamp. The wind began at dusk, then climbed the cliffs like a black dog. At midnight, he saw the Cresswill through a gap in the fog, its mast bending, its crew silent. He swung the lantern from the gallery and ran the beam from west to east, west to east, until his shoulder screamed. When morning came, the ship was beached in the cove, all hands alive. The letter in his pocket was soaked at the edge, as if it had been in the sea.

From that time on, Elias kept a notebook on the small table beneath the lamp room window. Each page held a letter from Nora. She wrote only once a month, usually on the first Tuesday, and never once gave a place where she lived. She wrote: The sea is loud inside. The woman who carries me has a name I almost know. She is waiting for a man to come home. Tell him I am not yet here, but I have learned the shape of his boat.
Elias did not know which man she meant, yet every letter made the island less alone. He began to answer the letters in his mind. He said: I hear you. Keep your light. I am here.

In the spring, the boat came with a young woman named Mara and her sister, both pale with sea sickness. Mara was the daughter of a fisherman Elias had pulled from the ice in 1911, and he had never met her before. She looked at the tower, at the lamp, at the old man with salt in his beard, and she said, I heard a man was keeping the light. The supply boat had no room for her luggage, so she stayed until the next crossing.
While she mended nets in the kitchen, Elias noticed a letter in his coat pocket. It had no date. It said: She is here. Do not tell her. She will ask questions if the light is dark, and dark is a dangerous answer.
Mara asked him that evening if the lighthouse had ever saved anyone. He said it had. She asked if he had ever saved a child. He did not answer.

She stayed three weeks. On the last night, a storm rose from the south, and the supply boat was forced back. Elias climbed the steps with his head lamp while Mara held the door, and together they watched the sea become a black roof over the island. At two in the morning, the lamp began to cough. The wick was wet, the oil line clogged, the great lens filling with a thin mist that no cloth could wipe away. Elias worked until his fingers bled. He could hear the Cresswill out beyond the wind, not a ship he had expected, but another, smaller, with a woman in the wheelhouse whose voice came over the speaking tube and begged for a light.
A letter slipped from his coat as he ran. It was dated the next morning.
The lamp must not fail. If it fails, I am lost. If it holds, I come.

He did not know how to say what Nora was, and he did not need to. He took the oil from the last canister and the cloth from his sleeve. He climbed into the lamp room alone while Mara stayed below with the radio. The wind tore at the door. The light died once, and the dark came in with the sound of a closing eye. Elias lit a lantern with his thumb and matched the flame to the wick. The lens shivered. The beam returned, weak at first, then strong, then strong enough to push the sea back against its own face.
He did not sleep. He did not eat. He wiped the glass with his shirt and spoke to the light as a father speaks to a horse in a storm. At dawn, the smaller ship was beached in the cove, and Mara ran to the door and cried out, Elias, there is a child alive on that boat.
The woman from the wheelhouse was the captain of a fishing skiff. She had not been Mara. She was a stranger from the mainland, a woman who had come to Gull Island to visit a grave she did not know was hers. She carried a newborn in a wool blanket, born in the storm, with salt in the hair and no breath in the mouth until the lighthouse beam crossed the cove.
Elias looked at the child and felt a name come to him without permission: Nora.

The letters did not stop, but they changed. Nora wrote in a larger hand, and later she sent postcards from a school in the mainland town, though Elias never learned how she got there. He wrote back to no one, but he kept the pages in the tower and read them each morning. In one, Nora wrote: I am seven now, and I can see the light from the hill. It is not a man anymore, but I know he was there before I was. In another, she wrote: Please do not die alone. He kept that one on the table for years.
Mara returned the next spring with a husband and two children, and after that, the island had visitors every season. The supply boat came more often. The light was still tended by Elias, though his knees no longer climbed as easily. One winter, when the first automatic lamp was installed in the lower room, Nora wrote: I am old enough to remember you, and I am not yet old enough to be forgiven for forgetting. He put the letter in the fire and watched it become a small orange flower.
The last letter came in 1956, the year Elias died. It said: Thank you for keeping the light when no one knew I was coming. I have kept it in my heart ever since.
The island was not forgotten. It simply learned to turn its beam for those who had never arrived.

After that, the letters remained folded in the tower, waiting for no one, but the light kept turning bright. Some lights are not meant to be explained, only kept before dawn arrives.
