The first thing you learn in the galley of the *Sowing* is that the ship has a heartbeat and the galley keeps time with it. Every six hours the gravity plating shifts by a tenth of a g, and the stockpots of broth go from sloshing to still. You can set the hour by how the soup moves. I have set it this way for twenty-two years, so long I have been setting it. My mother did it, and her mother before her. The gene records trace the name Vasko back seven generations to the ship's original cook, and before that the record is a gap, a torn page, a silence where a name should be.

My name is Ell, and I am the galley officer. The title is engraved on the plaque by the service door, in letters dulled to brass: FIRST OF NAME, COOK. I read it every morning the way other people read the star charts. The plaque is scored with the edges of a thousand trays. I like the scoring. It is the ship's handwriting, and I have learned that I am part of the ship.

Below deck, three levels down, the gardens grow under amber lamps that imitate a sun I have never seen. I walk those aisles at dawn, checking the vines. Tomatoes the size of a fist, cucumbers heavy on the stem, peppers turning from green to the red of a wound. We eat what the lights grow. But what we cook comes from the vault.

The Seed Vault is in the ship's belly, and it is the oldest thing on board. It holds what the Sowing took with it when she left Earth: four thousand seeds, a bulb of garlic, a jar of peppercorns, a tin of yeast. Four thousand years of rain and sun, sealed in a metal case. I have counted the garlic bulbs. There are nine left. I have told myself that was fine for another century.

Every galley officer keeps one book. Mine is bound in a material that has not been made anywhere for a thousand years, and its pages are soft as skin. Four hundred thirty pages of recipes, transcribed by hand by the first cook from a tablet that no longer exists. The book has a name, which is a kindness I invented for it: *The Kitchen*. I have cooked from its pages for as long as I have been alive. I can tell you that a chicken soup must simmer an hour and a half, that a bread dough is right when it springs back, that a stew is not finished until the bones begin to loosen. I cannot tell you what Earth smelled like. No one can. The Sowing was launched in the year I was not born, and no one who remembers Earth is left aboard.

We are close now. The course to Proxima Centauri is set, the arrival window opens in nineteen days, and the reactors are in their final third. The ship has been dying, in the way that only a ship can die: quietly, with every system still working. The crew has been on edge for months. Children are drawing ships with wings. The captain has ordered a feast for the day we enter orbit, and she has looked at me over the galley door and said, "Cook what you can. I don't care what it costs."

I have been thinking about that ever since. Cost. What does it cost on a ship where every calorie is measured in joules, every gram in a ledger?

I have a job: feed four thousand people one meal, and make them remember something. I pulled the jar of peppercorns. I pulled the tin of yeast. A crop that had once grown in a field I have no name for.

I told the botanist that the saffron was for the feast. I did not tell her it was the last of it.

For nineteen days I cooked. I cooked the garlic bread with the tin of yeast. I cooked the chicken soup with the peppercorns. I cooked the saffron into a broth so yellow it looked like late afternoon. I made it for the crew in small batches, a taster's portion, because a feast on a generation ship is not a dinner; it is a ceremony. People stood in the galley and ate and were quiet. And a home is not a place. A home is a recipe: what you feed the living, and what you carry when you leave.

I opened my eyes. In the dark of the airlock, in the cold of the ship, the garlic bulb was already white, and it was already growing.

# The Last Recipe

The first thing you learn in the galley of the *Sowing* is that the ship has a heartbeat, and the galley keeps time with it. Every six hours the gravity plates shift a tenth of a g, and the stockpots of broth go from sloshing to still. You can set the hour by the way the soup moves, and I have set it that way for twenty-two years, long enough to have set it myself. My mother set it that way, and her mother before her. The family record goes back seven generations to the original cook of the *Sowing*, and before that is a gap — a torn page, a silence where a name should be.

My name is Ell, and I am the galley officer. The title is stamped in the brass plate by the service door, the letters worn to a dull gold: FIRST OF NAME, COOK. I read it every morning the way other people read the star charts. The plate is scored with the edges of a thousand trays. I like the scoring. It is the ship's handwriting, and I have learned to be part of the ship.

Three decks below, the gardens grow under amber lamps that imitate a sun I have never seen. I walk those aisles at dawn and check the vines — tomatoes the size of fists, cucumbers heavy on the stem, peppers turning from green to the red of a small wound. We eat what the lights grow. But what we cook comes from the vault.

The Seed Vault is in the belly of the ship, and it is the oldest thing aboard. It holds what the *Sowing* carried when she left Earth: four thousand seeds, a bulb of garlic, a jar of peppercorns, a tin of yeast. Four hundred years of rain and sun, sealed in a metal case. I have counted the garlic. There are nine bulbs left. I told myself that was fine for another century.

Every galley officer keeps one book. Mine is bound in a material that has not been made anywhere in a thousand years, and its pages are soft as skin. Four hundred and thirty pages of recipes, copied by hand from a tablet that no longer exists. I have a name for the book, and I made it up myself: *The Kitchen*. I have cooked from its pages for as long as I can remember. I can tell you that chicken soup must simmer an hour and a half, that dough is ready when it springs back, that a stew is not finished until the bone begins to loosen. I cannot tell you what Earth smelled like. No one can. The *Sowing* left Earth in a year I was never born to, and there is no one left on this ship who remembers it.

The story they give me is that the first cook's name was lost in the first crossing, in a storm that was not quite a storm. My mother told me about it, the way mothers do, carefully, as if the telling might crack it open. The vault took a hit, she said, and whole crates went down with it. That is why the book has a torn page. That is why the garlic is nine and not twelve. I do not know if she was lying. On a generation ship, stories are weighed too, measured against the calories they cost.

We are close now. The course to Proxima is set, the arrival window opens in nineteen days, and the reactors are in their final third. The ship is dying in the way ships die — quietly, with every system still working. The crew has been on edge for months. Children are drawing ships with wings. The captain came into the galley once with a tray of coffee she never finished and said, "Cook what you have. I don't care what it costs."

I have been thinking about cost ever since. Cost is a word that means nothing on a ship where every calorie is a joule and every gram is a line in the ledger.

I went to the vault the next day. I stood before the garlic case and pressed my forehead to the cold metal and counted my breaths. Nine bulbs. Nine bulbs and three grams of something else in a drawer I had not opened since my mother's hands were too weak to work, a small packet of dried saffron — flowers that had once been picked in a field I do not have a name for. I took the peppercorns. I took the yeast. I took the garlic. I did not tell the botanist what I had taken. I did not have to. We both knew, in the way people on this ship know things, that the vault had been emptied of its last.

For nineteen days I cooked. I cooked the garlic bread with the tin of yeast. I cooked the chicken soup with the peppercorns. I cooked the saffron into a broth so yellow it looked like late afternoon through a porthole. I served it in small portions, because a feast on a generation ship is not a meal. It is a ceremony. People stood in the galley and ate and were quiet. The girl who draws ships with wings, the captain's daughter, asked me once why the soup tasted like memory. I told her it did, because it was.

On the morning we enter orbit, the whole crew gathers in the great hall, and the table is too short for all of them, so we eat in shifts and no one minds. The bread comes out golden and cracked, and the saffron broth is served in the bowls my mother made, the only ones left in the galley, which fit my hands the way a recipe fits a cook: exactly, and only once. There were those who remembered the first kitchen and those who had eaten only from mine, and I watched the old ones close their eyes over their bowls and watched the young ones lift them. The captain stood with a glass of water in her hand and said, "To Earth," and four thousand people said it back, and it sounded like rain.

The girl stood beside me with her bowl. She was thirteen, and for the whole year she had been asking me questions I had no time to answer.

"Will the food be good there?" she asked.

I looked at her. Behind me the galley hummed, and the ship beat against me, and I had set the hour by its soup for twenty-two years. I looked at the saffron in her bowl — three grams from a field I had never seen.

"I don't know," I said. "That's the first time I have said it about a dish."

She thought about it. She ate.

That night, after the feast, I took the last bulb of garlic and put it in the soil we would carry to the landing. One bulb, one pot, one promise. I carried the pot to the airlock with my arms low, the way you carry anything you have kept too long and finally used. I set the pot in the dark. I closed my eyes. The ship beat against me, the gardens breathed under their amber light, and for the first time in my life I thought: this is not a ship. This is a home. And a home is not a place. A home is a recipe — what you feed the living, and what you carry when you leave.

I opened my eyes. In the dark of the airlock, in the cold of the hull, the garlic bulb was already white, and already growing.
