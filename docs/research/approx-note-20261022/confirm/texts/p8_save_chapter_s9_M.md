# Chapter One: The City on the Shell

The first thing Kael learned to do was feel the hum.

It rose through the soles of her feet, through the floorboards of the chandlers' row, through the bedframe, and up into her teeth, a vibration so old and so constant that most children never learned its name. They simply grew up with it, the way one grows up with breathing. Only the eldest of the Tideborn, those who kept the Old Ledger in the Hall of Ripples, called it what it truly was: the heartbeat of something vast and slow beneath them.

They did not call the thing a sea turtle. The word was too small, too common, a fisher's slang for a crab the size of a dinner plate. They called it the Mother, or, in the formal liturgy of the first bell, the World-Shell, and they spoke of her as if she were a god, because on the scale of the sky and the salt, she was one.

Kael was eleven, and she had never seen a shore that was not part of her. The Mother's back stretched out beneath the city of Caelthar, a vast disc of pale, pitted shell rising from the endless sea, its rim flared like the walls of a fortress that no mason could have raised. For three hundred years—three hundred years of storms that never broke, of cities that rose and fell and were swallowed by the same horizon—Caelthar had sat upon that back like a gnat that had mistaken a mountain for a table.

The city rose in terraces from the shell's central swell. At the lowest level, the salters and rope-makers worked their shops in the shadows of the great flared rim, where the tide licked at the outer edge and the Mother's breathing fogged the windows every hour of the day. Above them, the weavers' quarter wound upward in spirals, its hanging laundry snapping in the salt wind like a thousand white flags. Higher still, the bell-towers of the Tideborn hung their bronze and their brass, and at the very top, on the knotted ridge where the shell's skin thinned and the light went green, stood the House of the Old Ledger, watching the sea in every direction, as though it expected the ocean to answer when it spoke.

No one had ever seen the sea's bottom. No one had ever seen the sea's far side. The Mother simply went, east and west and south and north, and Caelthar rode with her, and the people who lived there had built their whole world around a single, unanswerable question: what was she carrying them toward?

The answer, the elders said, was that she was carrying them toward nothing at all. They were simply where they had always been.

Kael did not believe this. She had no reason to, either, except that she could feel the hum changing.

It had started three days before her birthday, when the tide was low and the whole city creaked as the Mother rolled slightly in her sleep, a motion so slight that no one in the lower terraces noticed but the children. Children noticed. They pressed their palms flat against the floor and held their breath, and they heard it, the great slow turn of something under the city, and they whispered that the Mother was restless.

Now, on the morning of her eleventh year, Kael stood at the edge of the weavers' quarter with the hum running up through the stone, through the railing, into her wrist. She pressed her fingers to the old rope that tied the walkway to the shell, and the rope vibrated against her skin like a plucked string.

She felt it wrong.

Not faster. Not weaker. Just… off. The way a song is off when one instrument plays a half-tone too low. You can't point at the mistake and name it. You can only stand there, in the middle of the crowd, while everyone around you hums along with the rest, and you realize, with a slow and private dread, that you are the only one who hears the crack.

"Kael! The ledger's boy is on the rim again."

She turned. Denna was running down the spiral stairs with a basket of rope over her arm, her hair escaping from its braid in three wild threads. Denna was the same age as Kael, though she carried herself like someone who had been born a year earlier and had never forgiven the world for it.

"The ledger's boy," Denna said, as though this were a crime. "He's been on the rim since dawn, looking into the water, and Old Maren says he's talking to it."

"Talking to what?"

"To the sea. To the Mother. To the water, whatever you want to call it. She said he said he'd heard something, under the hum. A voice, maybe. Or a name."

Kael's stomach went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind.

"Which boy?"

Denna stopped, and she looked at her, and for a moment the rope basket slid off her arm and caught on a post.

"You're not the only one," she said quietly. "He's been coming to the rim every night for a week. You've seen him. You've always seen him. You just didn't know what to do with it."

Kael did know what to do with it. She had known since the first morning she pressed her palm to the floor and felt the hum come back at her, not as a vibration but as a shape, a long slow arc that almost — almost — resolved into a word.

She looked down at the water, gray and endless and moving with the slow patience of something that did not need to hurry.

"Tell Old Maren," Kael said, "to come up to the House. Tonight. Bring the ledger."

"And if she says no?"

Kael watched the ripple move out from beneath the rim, where the tide broke against the Mother's edge and the water slid off the shell in long white sheets, and she thought of the hum, of the half-tone, of the voice that was almost a name and not quite.

"Then I'll bring it myself," she said, "and I'll read it to the sea."

Above her, in the highest tower, the first bell of the day began to sound, a deep bronze note that rolled out across the water and across the shell and into the hum beneath everything. For a moment, all the bells rang together, and the hum rose with them, and the city of Caelthar sat on the back of the thing that carried it, and the thing that carried it went on, as it had always gone on, east and west and south and north, carrying the city and the city's small and stubborn questions into the endless, green-lit dark.

And under the shell, far below the tide, something that had been sleeping a very long time turned over in its dreams, and opened its eyes.
