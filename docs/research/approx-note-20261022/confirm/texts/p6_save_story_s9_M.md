The announcement came on a Tuesday.

Or what the ship's calendar still called Tuesday — the *Meridian* ran on Earth's seven-day cycle, which was another way of saying the crew still remembered, in a small stubborn way, that the world they'd left once turned on that rhythm. The message went out to all decks at once: the planet was confirmed. K-186f. Water. Nitrogen. Soil.

Yara stood in a corridor full of people with her ladle in her hand. She had never seen the ship full of people. They crowded onto Garden Deck, faces tilted up at the observation glass, at a blue dot that in one hundred and eighty days would be a world.

Captain Ito assigned her the landing feast. Two thousand three hundred and four seats. One meal. She would cook.

And that, Yara understood with a cold certainty in her stomach, was a trap.

The hydroponic towers had been drained for the landing burn. The algae vats would run through the descent and the first year on the surface, but the garden deck — the only place on the ship where anything still grew in dirt — belonged to her. To her alone.

She had six tomato vines, eight pots of basil, a single head of garlic, two onions, a sack of rice her mother had kept, and a flour mill that had seen three generations of bread. She had, in short, a kitchen. She had, in short, everything a feast needed except the salt.

That night she opened the tin and held it to her ear. The sea inside was silent.

The next morning she climbed to the observation deck and found Tomás in the chair he had chosen for the view. He was eighty-two, the oldest cook's apprentice Yara had ever met, and the only person on the ship who had ever told her stories of the sea.

"You're cooking the feast," he said. Not a question.

"I'm cooking the feast," she agreed.

"Good. And what are you going to salt it with?"

Yara almost smiled. "That's what I wanted to ask you."

Tomás turned to look at her. "The sea has no salt," he said. "The salt is *in* the sea. The sea is just what the sea is. When you taste the sea, you're not tasting the salt. You're tasting everything the salt has ever touched. Every ship that ever sailed it. Every child who ever swam in it. You have to feed them first."

That night Yara stood alone in the galley and started.

She made the broth first. Tomatoes, crushed by hand in a bowl she had no right to use, skins and all. Onions next, cooked low and slow until nothing was left but amber. Basil, whole leaves, dropped in at the very end, and the steam carrying their smell up the galley shaft like a small green ghost.

Lena, twenty years old and never having cooked anything that did not come out of a vat, watched from the doorway.

"Who was Elena?" she asked.

"The first. Before the voyage. Elena cooked the first meal on this ship. She sealed this," Yara tapped the tin, "and told the next cook to give it to the people when they land. So they'll remember."

"Remember what?"

Yara was quiet for a moment. "Where we came from. And where we were going."

The night before the feast, Yara stood alone at the counter and spooned a little broth into a cup. She ate it without salt. It was good. It was tomato and basil and water. It was not home.

Home, she thought, was a pinch of salt at the end of a meal. Home was the thing that finished a sentence.

The mess hall had not been this full in years. Yara ladled the soup into bowls and set the bread on the tables, still warm, cracked open so the steam rose into the cool air. She passed the salt — Elena's salt, freshly ground on the stone. She said nothing about where it came from. She only said, "Eat."

A long silence followed. Not the silence of the ship. A different silence. One with a pulse.

An old woman across the table closed her eyes. "I was born on the *Meridian*," she said. "I have never seen the sea. But I can taste it."

Yara nodded. That was what she had been hoping for. And afraid of.

At the end of the table, Tomás was not eating. He held his bowl in both hands and his shoulders shook, and when he looked up at Yara his eyes were wet.

"Where did you get this?" he asked. "The salt."

"The tin," Yara said. "From Elena. From the first cook on this ship."

"Then the first cook knew," Tomás said. "She knew we would need this."

Yara had no answer for that. She simply nodded.

And then the ship began to slow.

One hundred and eighty days later, Yara stood in the shuttle bay in a suit that felt too large. The tin was in her pocket. Next to it, a cutting from the tomato vine wrapped in damp cloth, a cutting of basil, a cutting of garlic. She had brought the garden with her.

The planet received them under a morning sky the color of nothing they had ever seen before. Blue, but not quite blue. It held a green in it that the artificial sky of the *Meridian* had never managed.

They made camp on a shelf of red soil. Yara built a fire. Not from the algae vats, not from the protein looms. A fire, of dry sticks and a spark from the shuttle's tool kit. She set a pot over it and put in tomatoes, onions, basil, the rice from the sack, and the bread she had baked on the landing deck.

Tomás stood beside the fire. "What are we having?"

"Soup," Yara said.

He looked at the tin in her pocket.

Yara took the salt out and ground it on the stone. One pinch, a small dark thing against the steam. Into the pot.

"Where did it come from?" he asked again.

"From the first cook," Yara said. "A hundred and sixty-seven years ago, on a ship that is now a world. She sealed it in a tin and told the next cook to give it to the people when they were ready."

Tomás looked at the fire, at the pot, at the sky.

"Ready for what?"

Yara was quiet for a long time. The fire crackled. The steam rose. Behind them, the *Meridian* hung in the sky, black and patient.

"For this," she said.

And the first meal on the new world tasted, at the very end, of the sea.
