Title: The Light Before Lark

Ewan had kept the Sallow Point light for thirty-one years, long enough to know the sea's moods and his own failing. He climbed the hundred steps each evening, polished the great lens, trimmed the wick, wrote in the log. He lived alone since his wife, Anne, had died in the winter he was forty. The island was small; below the cliff were cottages, a chapel, a school, and the harbor where the supply boat lay in spring. In winter, the sea cut the island off. He had learned to be patient with silence.

Then the letter came.

It was in the crate of tinned meat from the mainland, tucked under a broken plate and a bundle of wire. The envelope was cream, soft at the folds, with his name written in a neat hand: Keeper Ewan, Sallow Point. There was no postmark. He opened it on the kitchen table while the stove ticked.

"Dear Ewan," it said, "I am writing from a room I have not yet entered. I am not yet born. My name is Lark. I am only a possibility, but I am already thinking of your lamp."

The rest was strange and quiet. It told him that if the north pane cracked in the next gale, he should use the old blue glass, not the new one, because the old would hold a hairline fracture. It told him that when the wind smelled of iron, he must not open the service door, because the sea would climb the stair like a cat. It told him to leave the light burning through the long night, though no ship would come. At the bottom: "Keep me bright."

Ewan read it twice. He laughed once, a dry sound in the empty kitchen. The next day, a gale split the sky and the north pane cracked with a sound like a bone. He took the old blue glass from the storage shelf, the one that had once belonged to the lighthouse's first keeper, and set it in the frame. The storm hammered the tower until the stones groaned. The glass held. When dawn came, the crack had stopped at the edge of the old pane, as if the storm had been turned aside.

He should have dismissed it. He did not.

The next letter appeared in the oil can, folded around a rusted nail. It said: "Today the cat will knock the inkwell into the sea. Tomorrow the supply man will ask for salt. In three days a woman with flour in her hair will come to the shore with a bruise on her shoulder. Her name is Mara. She is carrying me."

Ewan looked out the window. The island cat, a white tabby called Bane, sat on the sill and looked at him with an expression of profound indifference. That morning the cat knocked the inkwell off the sill; it clattered to the rocks below and was gone. That afternoon the supply boat arrived, and its man asked for salt. The third day, a woman came to the landing in a grey coat. She had flour in her hair and a bruise on her shoulder, though Ewan did not see it until she lowered her head. She was from the village below, but Ewan had not seen her before. He asked if she needed help. She said only that she had come to wait for her sister, who was on the mainland.

Ewan went up to the light. He had not meant to be watchful, but the letters made him watchful in a new way. He began to keep a second notebook, not for weather but for promises: the cracked pane, the iron-scent wind, the woman with the bruise. Each thing happened. The letters came more often. Sometimes they were in a child's uneven handwriting; sometimes they were elegant, as if written by a woman with practiced fingers. The paper always smelled faintly of rain and lamp oil.

"My mother is afraid of the sea," one said. "She was a sailor's daughter. She will not forgive herself if I am not born. Please do not let the light fail when the fog comes off the Sound. She must cross at half past ten, or the boat will not leave. I am still only a breath in her. Please make the breath live."

Ewan began to ask questions of the village, carefully. The woman with flour in her hair was Mara Holt, the baker's daughter. Her husband had died in a shipwreck the previous year. She was with child. The midwife's sister lived on the island, but the midwife herself was on the mainland and could not come. The baby, they said, was expected in late spring. Ewan did not know that the baby would be named Lark. He did not know that a girl who would not exist for another month would be writing to him in a lighthouse where he had not yet learned to love her.

The worst letter came in February.

"Dear Ewan," it said, "there will be a storm on the night of the full moon. The foghorn will fail. The lamp will gutter because the wick is fouled. Mara will be in the channel, small boat, black coat, white hood. She will not see the mainland lights. I am in her hands. If the light goes out, she will be swept to the rocks. If she is swept, I will not be born. Clean the wick. Polish the lens. Do not sleep."

Ewan cleaned the wick the next evening, though he did not yet know the storm's name. He polished the lens until his arms ached. He took the spare wick, the spare glass, the oil, and the lamp oil, and laid them in a row on the floor. He told no one. He did not know whether he was obeying a letter or preparing for the sea. The sea, he had learned, always prepared itself.

On the night of the full moon, the storm came from the west with a voice like a man shouting into a tunnel. The foghorn on the mainland gave one long note, then nothing. The tower shook. The lamp guttered, and a thin thread of smoke climbed the glass. Ewan climbed the stairs with the service lamp in one hand and the wick picker in the other. The wind threw itself against the gallery. Below, in the black channel, something small moved.

He thought of Lark. The name had no body yet, no face, no first cry. It was a word made of future, a small bright thing that had not yet fallen into the world. He thought of Mara, who had come to the shore with a bruise on her shoulder, and he thought of Anne, who had died in a room where the light was not bright enough. He set the spare wick into the lamp. The flame caught, then steadied, then bloomed into the great lens. The beam swept out over the sea, cutting the fog into ribbons of gold.

Below, a small boat appeared between two islands. It was heading for the rocks. Ewan took the signal flag and ran to the gallery window, though the wind nearly tore it from his hands. He waved the flag in the old keeper's code: turn, turn, danger. The boat's light flickered. For a long minute, nothing happened. Then the boat swung away from the rocks, slow as a hand withdrawing from a wound.

Ewan stayed at the window until the boat disappeared toward the mainland. His hands would not stop trembling. The storm passed before dawn. The foghorn did not sound again all day. No one knew how close the channel had come to becoming a grave. In the village, Mara said the light had guided her, though she did not know which light. She went to the island and stood for a while under the lighthouse, looking up. Ewan did not meet her. He was afraid of what his face would say.

In April, the midwife's sister delivered Mara's child in the cottage near the chapel. It was a girl. She had a thin red line along one cheek and a voice like a small bell. When Ewan was brought to see her, he held the baby as if she were made of lamp glass. The mother slept in the next chair, her head bowed. The midwife's sister said the child had the old family's stubbornness.

That night, Ewan climbed the hundred steps to the light. He expected no letter. There was one, tucked under the door of the watch room. The handwriting was unsteady, as if written by fingers not yet strong.

"Dear Ewan," it said, "I am here. I am small and warm and afraid of the dark. Thank you for keeping the light. I do not know you, but I know your hand on my life. When I grow, I will remember."

He read it until the words blurred. Outside, the lamp turned in the night, patient and bright. He had kept a light for ships, for storms, for the sea's many hungry edges. Now, he thought, he had also kept a child before she was born. He did not know if that made him a keeper or a father. He only knew that the beam went out over the water, and in the black below, something small and living had finally arrived.
