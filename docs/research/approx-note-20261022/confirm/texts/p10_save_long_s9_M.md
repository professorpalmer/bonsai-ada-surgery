# The Lamplighter's Loaf

Brine Hollow woke before its people did. At three in the morning the harbor was only a rumor of grey water, the gulls were asleep in the fish sheds, and the only honest light on Marrow Lane was the pair of bakery windows at its end — one on each side of the salt line.

Clara Marchetti was up first. She had been up since one, when the cold in the kitchen takes on a sound, like a spoon tapping the side of a bowl of ice. She filled the water bucket, checked the cellar, and lifted the lid off Old Brine.

Old Brine was a sourdough culture two hundred years old, the size of a fist and the color of the sea after a storm. It lived in a stoneware jar with a lid her mother had carved, and every morning she fed it a cup of flour and a cup of water, and it rose on her palms like a promise. In the years she had kept it she had never once seen it die, and the town treated this as a local legend, the way one treats weather. Clara accepted it the way she accepted everything: without a word, eyes on the work.

Across the lane, Dorian Bell's ovens were already humming. He had not been a dawn person by nature; he had trained for six years at a hotel in Lisbon, won a trophy nobody outside the kitchen had ever seen, and come home to Brine Hollow in his thirties to take over a failing bakery and turn it into something the town had not seen in decades. His pastries were lamination: ninety-six thin sheets of butter folded into a croissant that, when broken, split into a thousand shivering flakes. The Marchetti loaves were something else — dark, slow, crust the color of wet slate, crumb that smelled of the cellar and the sea.

Both bakeries claimed the same street. Both claimed the town. Every morning at eight, when the first loaf left a counter, the owner of the other shop stood across the lane and watched, and the town watched both.

That was the rule, even though no one had written it down: whoever sold the first loaf on market day held the square. For four hundred years the square had belonged to the Marchetti side, and for the past ten years Dorian Bell had been very busy trying to take it back.

The rivalry had a beginning, and Clara was the first to say so out loud, which was why she lost arguments and won mornings.

Her family had been in Brine Hollow since before the lighthouse had a beam. The first Marchetti woman — or at least the first on the record — had been baking bread for the fishermen before the boats had names. Her grandmother's ledger, a water-damaged book in the cellar, listed customers by boat name, and at the bottom, in a hand gone weak with age, a line read: *the bells will take care of their own.*

The Bell family had arrived two generations after the Marchettis, with a shipwreck in the family tree and a debt in the books. The old Bell was a fishmonger, and when the fish went south, his son put a name on a flour sack and bought the lane.

Dorian had not inherited the bakery. He had bought it back.

He announced it the way people announce the weather in the paper: a sharp sentence, no apologies. "This town is going to need better bread," he told the evening paper. "And I'm going to bake it."

Clara had not answered. She was already kneading.

The Tide Festival was three weeks away, and the town had already decided what it wanted.

The festival fell on the harbor wall at the first full tide of the season, and its centerpiece had been, since before Clara's grandmother, a bake-off. The judges were the town council, plus Mrs. Fenn, who had not lived in Brine Hollow for forty years and still judged every contest, every pie, every meringue, the way a magistrate judges a bench. The prize was a copy of the golden whisk of 1899 that hung in town hall, and, more important, the right to name the festival loaf.

The festival loaf was the thing Brine Hollow sold to the tourists all summer — a loaf the whole town knew by name. The Marchetti side had called it Salt Crust for sixty years. Dorian had been saying for two years that the name was a "nostalgia product" and that the real name should be the one his great-grandfather had written in the old ledger: *Lamplighter's Loaf.*

Clara had a word for Dorian's pastry: architecture. Dorian had a word for Clara's bread: a brick. They said it in the square, at the counters, once, across the lane.

As the festival drew near, the lane changed. The Marchetti side of the salt line smelled of long ferment; the Bell side smelled of butter. The streetlights between the two shops began to burn out — first one, then the next, and by the second week there was a dark place in the middle of Marrow Lane, and the town said this was a sign.

Clara's helper was a boy of nineteen named Tom, who had learned more about the sea from the cellar than any fisherman. Dorian's apprentice was June, who could count the layers of a croissant by touch and had opinions about both bakeries and the tide chart. The two of them passed each other in the lane every morning, and by the second week they were no longer passing. They were stopping.

On the night before the festival, the storm came off the west like something angry with the coast.

The wind hit the lane at midnight. The power went out on the Bell side first — a blue flicker in the freezer window, a long beep, nothing. Then the Marchetti side went, and the town was dark except for the ovens.

Dorian's ovens were electric. They were not running. His laminated dough had to rest at four degrees, and it had already begun to warm. He had forty minutes before the butter began to weep.

Clara's ovens were wood. She fired them at two, and the old ovens took the cold out of the cellar and gave back heat the way they had for eighty years.

She stood at the end of the lane a long time, watching his window. Then she went across.

"Your freezer's dead," she said. It was not a question.

"The butter's still good," Dorian said from the dark. "The dough isn't."

She looked at the freezer. She looked at him. He stood in the middle of the bakery in one sock, holding a croissant tray in front of him like a shield.

"Bring the butter in," she said. "I've got a cellar."

The cellar door opened onto a stone room with a wall of shelves, and on the shelves jars of flour, a barrel of salt from the salt pans, a loaf of bread still cooling. Old Brine slept in its jar in the dark, and the room was warm, because a wood oven holds heat the way a person holds a grudge.

Dorian put his butter in the barrel. He set his dough on the stone. "It needs cold," he said. "I can manage cold here."

"It's not cold," Clara said. "It's cool. There's a difference."

He looked at the thermometer on the wall. Twelve degrees. He looked at Clara. "Close enough for a legend," he said.

Clara laughed. It surprised her. She had not laughed in the lane since the day her mother died.

They worked until four. They spoke little. When they did, it was about the dough, the salt, the tide.

"Your grandmother's recipe," Dorian said at three-thirty, when the dough went into the second fold. "The Lamplighter's Loaf. It's in the ledger. It's a real recipe."

Clara wiped her hands on her apron. "It's a recipe for a loaf I've been baking for sixty years, under a different name."

"It's not just a different name," Dorian said. "It's a different loaf. Your Salt Crust is sourdough. The Lamplighter's Loaf is sourdough and butter. It's a different thing."

Clara looked at him. For the first time in ten years she thought he might be right.

In the morning Dorian asked Clara to go with him to Mrs. Fenn.

Mrs. Fenn lived at the top of the lane, in a house that had been a chapel before the war, with a door knocker shaped like a croissant, which Dorian had chosen himself. He had been a little embarrassed to tell Clara that, but he had not been embarrassed to knock.

Mrs. Fenn was eighty-nine, or a little more, and had not walked down the lane in twenty years. She opened the door to them both and said, "I knew you'd come together. The street's been trying to get you to for a long time."

"About the festival," Dorian said.

"About the loaf," Mrs. Fenn said. She sat them down in a chair that was more rug than chair and asked for two cups of tea. "You want the Lamplighter's Loaf recipe. Both of you want it. One of you wants it for the name, and one of you wants it for the truth. Sit down."

Clara sat. Dorian sat. Mrs. Fenn went to a shelf in the corner and brought down a book older than both families.

It was the original ledger — the 1792 ledger — the one the town had kept in town hall since before the war. In it, the recipe for the Lamplighter's Loaf was written in two hands, in two inks. The two hands were not the Marchetti hand and the Bell hand. They were two other hands, and they had written the recipe together, and they had argued over it for the rest of their lives.

"It was a split," Mrs. Fenn said. "The Marchetti family kept the sourdough. The Bell family kept the butter. Each family had half the loaf, and each family thought it had the whole loaf, and the two families had been arguing about it for two hundred years, and the loaf had been missing the other half the whole time."

Clara looked at Dorian. Dorian looked at Clara.

"So," Dorian said slowly, "the Lamplighter's Loaf isn't a Marchetti loaf, and it isn't a Bell loaf. It's a Marchetti-and-Bell loaf."

Mrs. Fenn smiled. "It's a Brine Hollow loaf. It always was. The two families were meant to bake it together, every year, on the festival. They stopped in 1911, after the war. The war took the Bell grandmother, and the Marchetti grandmother took the recipe down to the cellar, and the two families have been arguing about it ever since."

Clara put her hands flat on the table. "The old culture," she said. "It isn't two hundred years old."

"It is," Mrs. Fenn said. "It's been in the cellar since before the Bell family came."

Mrs. Fenn nodded. "Your grandmother knew. She kept the sourdough alive. And Dorian's great-grandfather kept the butter. The two families were supposed to meet in the cellar, every year, on the night before the festival. They stopped in 1911. They never came back."

Clara looked at the cellar door. "Old Brine," she said. "It's been waiting."

Dorian looked at the ledger. "The butter," he said. "So has that."

The next morning the storm was over and the sky was the color of wet slate, which Clara had always known was the right color for bread. The lane was wet, the salt line darker than usual, and the town had gathered on the harbor wall where the festival would be held. The square was full. Both bakeries were open. The first thing out of either oven that morning was not a loaf at all, but a promise.

Clara fed Old Brine before she did anything else. She filled the bucket, checked the cellar, and lifted the lid off the jar. The culture rose on her palms like a promise, and she folded it into the dough, and the dough took it in, and the dough was alive. It was not a Marchetti dough, and it was not a Bell dough. It was a Brine Hollow dough.

Dorian stood at his counter with his hands in the flour and his butter cold and his dough ready. He looked across the lane at Clara's oven, and Clara's oven was burning, and the heat between them was visible, and the town could smell it and taste it and see it, and the town did not know what it was, but the town was not going to ask.

They baked together. Not at the same counter, not in the same oven, but on the same lane, in the same morning, in the same town. Clara shaped the sourdough and Dorian folded the butter, and the two of them did not speak, and did not need to. The dough spoke for them.

The loaf that came out of the oven at ten-fifteen was a Marchetti-and-Bell loaf. It was a Brine Hollow loaf. It was the Lamplighter's Loaf. It had a crust the color of wet slate and a crumb that smelled of the cellar and the sea. It had a hundred and nine layers of butter folded into it and a sourdough that had been alive for two hundred years. It had a name that had been missing for a hundred and one.

The judges took it to the harbor wall. It went onto a white tablecloth. The loaf was not white. It was the color of the sea after a storm.

The first judge was Mayor Calloway. He cut the loaf, and the crust broke, and the steam rose, and the steam smelled of the cellar and the sea, and the mayor closed his eyes. He took a bite. He swallowed. He opened his eyes and looked at Clara, and Clara looked at Dorian, and Dorian looked back, and no one spoke.

The second judge was the council librarian, who had a reputation for being difficult and for being right. She cut a second piece. She chewed. She set the piece down and said, "It's not a brick."

The third judge was Mrs. Fenn. She cut a piece without asking permission, which was in character, and ate it in three bites and said nothing for a full minute, which from Mrs. Fenn was the equivalent of a standing ovation.

The town said nothing.

"It's not a tie," she went on. "It's a loaf. A loaf isn't a tie. A loaf is a thing. A thing isn't a tie. A thing is a thing, and this is the first Brine Hollow loaf in a hundred and one years, and it's called the Lamplighter's Loaf, and it goes in the record book under both names, Marchetti and Bell, because both names are right, and both names are the town."

The mayor put the golden whisk in Mrs. Fenn's hands, and Mrs. Fenn held it out between them, between Clara and Dorian, and they took it together, and the town clapped the way a town claps — late, and all at once, and a little too hard.

The square stayed Marchetti's, the way squares do. But the next morning, when the first loaf left a counter at eight, the owner of the other shop did not stand across the lane and watch. He was already at the counter on his own side, and the two loaves went out the same minute, and the town went home the way it had been going home for four hundred years, and the salt line held, and the dark place in the middle of Marrow Lane stayed dark, and nobody said anything about it, because some things the street is not trying to get you to.

A year later, at the next Tide Festival, there was a plaque on the harbor wall under the golden whisk, and it read: *The Lamplighter's Loaf — Marchetti sourdough, Bell butter, baked together on the night before, as the lamps came down.* It was the only plaque in Brine Hollow with two names on it, and it was the only one that was still wet with salt.

Clara still woke at one. Dorian still fired his ovens at two. Old Brine still rose on Clara's palms like a promise, and the butter still held its shape in the barrel, and every year, on the night before the festival, the cellar door opened onto a warm stone room, and two pairs of hands worked in the dark, and the loaf that came out was a Marchetti-and-Bell loaf, a Brine Hollow loaf, the Lamplighter's Loaf, and it had the town waiting for it, and it had the name, and the name was right, and the name was the town, and the town was the name.

The gulls were asleep. The tide came in. And in the middle of Marrow Lane, where the salt line ran from shop to shop, the light from the two ovens fell on the wet stones and made one long glow, neither Marchetti nor Bell but the street itself, the street itself, the street itself.
