Chapter One

The mountain woke in pieces. First the light, pale and reluctant, slipped through the pines and lay in a long gray sheet along the old rail bed. Then the wind, a thin complaint that moved from the summit into the valley and ruffled the windowpane at my kitchen. I stood there in my socks, coffee going cold in my hands, watching the town of Harrow's Hollow collect itself in the mist.

I had lived here long enough to know that the mountains did not announce weather; they simply changed the temperature of a room. For thirty-two years I had worked the Silver Peak line, conductor, whistle, timetable, the little brass bell at the end of the handrail that used to say, "You're late, or you're dead." I retired four winters ago, after a heart stopped in the tunnel and started again with a long, embarrassed pause. Since then I had kept my days small: mending fences, walking the old track to the cemetery, answering letters that mostly wanted a signature.

The phone rang at six-fifteen, a sound I had come to think of as a small, bright blade.

I answered on the third ring. Sheriff Lila Marrow's voice came through rougher than I liked.

"Elias. It's a body."

"Where?"

"Old rail yard. The one past the bridge. They found a man in the caboose."

My throat closed. The caboose had been empty for a decade, stripped of paint and rusted into a kind of architecture. No one kept anything there but crows.

"I'm on my way," I said.

"You don't have to come."

"I know."

"You're the only one who can tell me what that caboose means."

That was not true. I had been the only one who could tell her many things, mostly why the rails were too cold, or which switch was lying, or which passenger would lie before the whistle blew. But the caboose meant something else now.

I pulled on my coat and went out into the cold that smelled of wet stone and pine resin. The mountain lay behind me, all ridgeline and fog, and the valley filled with a pale, patient light.

The road to the old rail yard was narrow, flanked by blackberry bushes that had never learned respect. I drove slow, the way the mountain demanded, past the mill and the church and the row of houses where the shutters were always half open. At the end of the street, where the gravel gave way to a gravelly kind of nothing, there was a line of police cars, and behind them a small crowd that had already learned the particular stillness of a place where something had gone wrong.

Sheriff Marrow was waiting by the caboose. She was a compact woman, with a face that had stopped pretending to be surprised by much. When she saw me she gave a nod that did not contain much more than fact.

"You're early," she said.

"The mountain is fast," I replied. "You are the only one who would have called."

She stepped back and gestured to the caboose.

The caboose sat on a short spur, its red paint long faded to the color of dried blood. It was a small building on wheels, once a conductor's office, now a monument to something no one had decided to remember. The door hung open. Inside, a single chair faced the window, and on the floor, with one hand extended toward the door, lay a man.

He was not old. That was the first thing I noticed. Maybe forty-five. Clean-shaven, with dark hair gone gray at the temples. He wore a wool overcoat that had not been tailored for him, and the leather of his shoes was scuffed in a way that spoke of long walking, not city streets. His eyes were open, but they looked at nothing, and his mouth held a faint expression of surprise, as if the cold had found him before he could ask what had happened.

I stood at the threshold for a moment, letting my eyes adjust to the darkness inside. The caboose had no light. No table. No desk. Only the chair, the window, the floor, and the body. The window looked out over the old yard, where the rails ran off into shadow and the mountain stood close enough to touch.

"He's not a passenger," I said.

"No," Marrow answered. "He isn't."

"Who is he?"

"We don't know yet. No identification. No wallet. No phone."

I looked down at the man's extended hand. Between the fingers, half-hidden by a fold of the overcoat, was something small and bright. A brass key? No, it was a bell, the kind I had carried at the end of my handrail for thirty-two years. A conductor's signal bell, the size of a walnut, its surface worn smooth by years of use.

I reached out and touched it.

The metal was cold, but the engraving on the bottom was clear: S.P.R. Silver Peak Railway. Below that, a date. 1968.

Marrow watched my hand as I lifted it.

"Where did that come from?"

I turned the bell in my fingers. It had a small crack running through the handle, a repair made with wire so old it had gone silver-black. I had seen that repair. I had made it.

"There are three bells like it in existence," I said. "Two in the museum. One in my house."

Marrow's face tightened. "You made that?"

"I fixed that. Forty years ago. It was mine."

The silence inside the caboose seemed to thicken. The wind moved outside and lifted the door, just a whisper, and then stillness returned.

Marrow set the bell on the floor beside the body.

"Then we have a problem."

I nodded slowly. "A problem."

They brought in the coroner, and I was asked to leave the scene. I went, but not before I noticed three things. First, the chair had been moved. The wheel marks on the floor were faint, but they ran from the back of the caboose to the window, then stopped. Second, there were two sets of shoe prints on the floor, both small, both leading to the door and then back. Third, on the sill of the window, a single drop of something dark and wet had dried into a crescent, the size of a coin.

Marrow met me at the door.

"You saw something," she said.

"I see a lot," I replied. "But I don't know what it means."

She was not satisfied. "Who is this man?"

"A passenger who never got off the train."

Marrow laughed once, a short, hard sound. "That's not an answer."

"It's the beginning of one."

We walked to the police car. The crowd had grown, mostly old men who had worked the line and women who had watched their sons and husbands grow up on the mountain. They stood with their coats tight and their faces turned toward the caboose, but no one spoke. The old rail yard was a place of memory, and memories in small towns are like rails: they carry weight and show every crack.

Marrow got behind the wheel and did not turn on the engine.

"I need you to tell me something," she said.

"Tell me."

"Do you trust yourself anymore?"

I considered the question. I had been a conductor. I had made a living by knowing where I was, when I was there, and what was expected of me. I had learned to read the mountain by the way the fog moved, by the creak of the rails, by the sound a train made when it was about to break a rule. I had also learned that the heart is a poor instrument for warning, and that a man can be very wrong and very sure of himself at the same time.

"I don't know," I said.

"Good. That's the only answer I can use."

She turned on the engine and pulled away from the caboose. As we moved along the spur, I saw the mountain again, the pines and the fog and the thin line of the old tunnel mouth where the tracks disappeared into the hillside. I had driven through that tunnel ten thousand times. I knew its every echo. I knew the places where the water ran and the places where the rock had fallen and the places where the light died. I also knew that no one had used that tunnel in years, and that the rails beyond it were broken.

Yet something about the day felt changed, as if the mountain had moved an inch closer, or the silence had learned a new word.

At the station, which was now a post office and a general store, we found a man with a name that made both of us stop.

He was sitting in the waiting room, a thin man with a wool hat and a face like a question no one wanted to ask. He had the same gray hair as the dead man. The same eyes. The same hands, folded on his knee, with the nails trimmed short and the knuckles swollen in the way of a man who had worked with tools.

His name was Thomas Vane. He was the son of the mayor of Harrow's Hollow. He had been missing since two days ago.

Marrow looked at him. He looked back.

"Elias," Thomas said. "You came."

"I came," I answered.

"That's a good thing. I have a question for you, and I think you're the only one who can answer it."

"What question?"

Thomas leaned forward.

"Who killed my father?"

The waiting room was quiet except for the tick of the clock and the soft cough of the heater. Outside, the mountain wind moved through the pines and lifted the door of the general store, then let it fall shut.

Marrow set her hand on Thomas's shoulder.

"That's not something you can ask here," she said.

Thomas did not move.

"My father was found in the caboose," I said.

"Yes."

"He was not a passenger."

"No."

"I need you to tell me where you have been."

Thomas's face did not change. "I was at my cabin. I was alone."

"Since when?"

"Since Friday."

"Friday," Marrow said. "That's two days."

"Yes."

"And where is your father?"

The question hung in the air. Thomas looked at me, and for the first time I saw something move behind his eyes, a small, sharp fear.

"He was going to the rail yard," Thomas said. "He said he had to see the old timetable. He said it was the only thing left."

I looked at Marrow. She looked at me.

"The timetable," I said.

Thomas nodded.

"Where did he say it was?"

"I don't know. He said it was in the caboose. He said he had looked for it his whole life."

I thought of the empty chair, the window, the dark drop on the sill. I thought of the bell in my hand, the one I had carried for thirty-two years, the one I had kept in a drawer at home because I could not throw it away. I thought of the man on the floor, his hand extended toward the door, as if asking to be taken somewhere else.

I looked at Thomas.

"Did your father ever tell you about the last train?"

Thomas's breath stopped.

"What do you mean?"

I turned to Marrow.

"The train that never came in."

Marrow's face hardened.

"Let's go back to the caboose."

We drove out in silence. The mountain fog had lifted, but the pines were still wet, and the air was cold enough to make the words I spoke feel sharp on my tongue. The rail yard looked smaller now, or perhaps I had simply stopped believing that it could hold anything but the past.

When we reached the caboose, the door was open again.

Marrow went inside first. I followed, but this time I did not touch the floor. I let my eyes move slowly, the way a conductor watches a platform: first the people, then the tracks, then the things that do not belong. The chair was still in place. The body had been covered with a black cloth, and the bell was gone. On the floor, in the same place where it had lain, was a single sheet of paper.

I knelt.

It was a timetable. Old, brittle, the ink faded to a pale blue. The dates ran from 1968 to 1972. The route was the Silver Peak line, but the stations were wrong. There were names I did not recognize: Kettle Hollow, Pine Gap, Blackwater, and one at the end, in a different hand, underlined twice: The Last Stop.

At the bottom of the page, in small letters, was a sentence:

If you are reading this, you have missed the train.

I looked up.

Marrow was watching me, her face tight.

"Who wrote this?"

I did not answer. I was looking at the clock on the wall of the caboose, a round brass thing with a broken hand that had stopped at 11:15. The time I had first arrived. The time I had seen the body. The time the bell had been in my hand.

Outside, the wind stopped.

The mountain held its breath.

I picked up the timetable and turned it over. On the back, in a hand I recognized, was a single word, written in red ink:

Elias.

I stood there, the paper in my hand, while the caboose creaked around me and the fog returned, thin and patient, sliding up the rail bed and over the old tracks and into the place where the mountain kept its secrets.

Marrow said my name.

I did not hear it.

I was already listening for a whistle.
