# The Signal

The snow came down in flat, white sheets, as if the mountain had decided to erase everything it did not need. Arthur Voss stood in the kitchen of his small house on Larkspur Road and watched it settle on the window ledge, on the old iron fence, on the empty lot where the last of the apple trees had died. The town of Larch Hollow lay below him, a scattering of lights in the valley, and beyond it the Blue Spruce Range rose like a wall of black glass. For sixty-two years, Arthur had known that wall by name. He knew the passes, the cuts, the switchbacks, the places where the stone shifted in winter and the places where the snow would hide a body until spring. He knew them because, for thirty-four years, he had ridden them.

He had retired from the Larch Hollow Branch in the winter after his heart gave its first warning. The company had sent a doctor, and the doctor had sent him home. The Branch had become a private short line for tourists and old men with memory. The tracks still ran through the mountains, but only in the slowest season, and only when the ice held. Most nights the rails were as still as bones, and the mountain gave up its silence only to those who had learned to listen for it.

Arthur’s house had the smell of pipe smoke, coffee, and the old leather cap that hung by the door. On the shelf above the stove was a brass whistle, green at the edges, and a row of tickets from the first year of the Larch Hollow Branch, their red ink faded to the color of dried blood. He liked to believe the whistle still worked, though he had not blown it in three years. He liked to believe that because a man who had spent his life calling trains should not be afraid to be called by one.

The phone rang at 4:12 a.m.

He lifted his cup. The tea had gone cold. He let it ring three times, then picked up the receiver.

“Mr. Voss?”

The voice was Deputy Nell Harper’s, and it had the flat edge she used when she was trying not to be frightened.

“It’s Nell.”

Arthur sat back on his stool. “What is it?”

“We’ve found a body at the old signal cabin on the spur line.”

The cold tea in his stomach turned to stone.

“Ruthie Calloway?”

A pause. Then: “Yes. Ruthie Calloway.”

He knew the name. Ruthie Calloway had been the librarian’s daughter, or rather the daughter of the librarian’s first wife, before the man had sold the library to the town and left for the city. She had bought the old depot and tried to make it into a little museum of the Branch. She had filled the waiting room with brass lamps and old maps, and she had a way of looking at people as if she were waiting for them to confess something.

“How?” he asked.

“We don’t know. We thought it was a fall, but the snow tells a different story.”

“Is she warm?”

“No.”

Arthur was already standing. “I’m coming.”

He did not take his old conductor’s cap. He did not take his coat of leather that had once been black and was now the color of dried blood. He wore his gray wool coat, a knit cap, and the heavy boots that still remembered the sound of iron rails. He put on the small key ring that hung by the door, the one for houses and the town store, and he told himself he did not need the old railroad keys. He told himself that because a man who had spent his life carrying keys should not be afraid to carry one.

The drive up the spur line took less than ten minutes. Larch Hollow was quiet when the mountain people came to the dead. There were only a few cars at the old signal cabin: the sheriff’s pickup, two department trucks, and a small ambulance with its lights off. The men and women outside stood in clusters, their breath rising in the cold, their faces half-hidden by hats. Arthur saw Nell at the edge of the snow, her flashlight in her hand, and she turned when she heard the engine stop.

“Mr. Voss,” she said. “We needed to know if you knew her.”

“I knew her,” he said. “She was asking questions.”

“That’s what I wanted to know.”

The signal cabin stood behind the old water tower, a low stone building with a rusted lantern and a door of blackened wood. Snow lay thick on the roof, and the rails beside it were black lines under the white. The body was a dozen yards from the cabin, face up in the snow, arms at its sides, head turned toward the tree line. Arthur saw the gray parka, the red scarf, the pale face, and he understood that the snow had not been the only thing covering her.

Ruthie Calloway was young. Not young by the standards of the old line, where the mountains aged a person before the calendar did, but young enough that the thought of her lying there felt like a wound in the air.

He knelt beside her.

Her eyes were closed. Her lips were blue at the edges. There was a small dark bruise at her temple, the kind of mark a stone leaves on a river. No blood had come from it, or very little, and the snow around her head was undisturbed.

“You’re the one who used to work the trains,” Nell said from behind him.

“I was the conductor,” Arthur said. “For a long time.”

“She’s been missing for a day or two. People thought she’d gone to the county.”

“She was at the library yesterday,” Arthur said. “I saw her there. She had a file open.”

Nell’s flashlight moved across his face. “What file?”

He did not answer at once. The mountain was very still.

“The Ashford Express,” he said.

Nell’s face changed. “The derailment?”

“Yes.”

“Everyone says it was an accident.”

“Everyone says that about mountains.”

Nell looked toward the cabin. “We found her this way. No one else was seen.”

Arthur looked at the snow around the body.

The snow was fresh, but not too fresh. There were prints around the body: her boots, size seven, small and careful, and a man’s boots, size twelve, deep and heavy. The man’s prints went to the cabin door and stopped. They did not leave.

“That’s not right,” he murmured.

“What is it?”

He pointed to the cabin. “The door was not forced. The lock is old, but it would have held a man. If she went in, she came out by the door. If someone put her there, the prints do not match the path.”

Nell followed his gaze. “You mean someone carried her?”

“I mean someone placed her.”

Arthur went to the cabin and pushed the door open. The hinge screamed as if it had been waiting a long time to be heard. Inside, the cold was dry and old. A wood stove stood black with soot, and on the bench beside it hung a wool coat with no hat in the pocket. On the wall was a timetable, the Larch Hollow Branch, 1998, the last year the line ran the way it had once been meant to run. The dates were printed in blue, and the mountains behind the timetable looked like a painting of somewhere else.

There was a lever in the floor, rusted and still. A small hole where a signal rod had been. On the bench, folded into a small square, was a ticket.

Arthur picked it up.

It was not paper from a modern office. It was thin, brittle, and the ink was blue-black, the old style. He unfolded it with careful hands.

Ashford Express.
11:47 p.m.
Single.
Berth 17.
R. CALLOWAY.

The name of the train struck him before the date did. The Ashford Express had run once a year through the winter, from Blackpine to the old mountain pass, and it had not run for decades. Not since the night the rails had broken on the curve and the mail car had gone down into the snow.

He turned the ticket over.

On the back, in pencil, was a single word:

HALE.

Arthur set the ticket on the bench. His hands had not shaken, but something in him had. He knew the name. He knew the face that had been on the death certificate before the body was buried. Elias Hale. The engineer. The man who had never come home from the Ashford Express.

Nell appeared in the doorway. “What is it?”

“Her ticket,” he said. “It’s from the old line. It should not be here.”

“Ruthie collected old things.”

“This one is not an old thing.” He picked it up again. “This is a warning. Or a confession.”

“Whose?”

He looked at the ticket, at the pencil mark, at the small brass key that had been in the dead woman’s hand. The key was still in his pocket. He had taken it from Ruthie without thinking, the way one takes a thing from a body, as if the dead had no right to keep it. Now he held it up to the light.

The tag was small and worn. It read: C-17.

The C meant car. No. 17 was the mail car on the Ashford Express.

Nell’s flashlight trembled in her hand. “The mail car went off the track.”

“Yes.”

“And it was never found?”

“It was found. Empty. The mail was gone.”

A long silence fell between them, broken only by the tick of the old clock in the stove.

“Why would she have a key to a car that disappeared?” Nell asked.

“Because someone had put it in her hand,” Arthur said. “Or because she had found it and was going to say something.”

He looked at the ticket again. The name HALE. The engineer. The man they had buried with no questions answered. For thirty-six years, Arthur had told himself that the mountain had taken the train, that the rails had failed, that the snow had closed the case. He had told himself that because a man who had been on the train should not carry the secret of it.

But the ticket was in the dead woman’s hand. The key was in his pocket. And the name was written there in pencil, as if someone had wanted him to read it, or wanted him to be afraid.

He put the ticket in his coat. “Who else knew she was looking into this?”

Nell shook her head. “A few people. The librarian’s widow. The old stationmaster’s nephew. The man at the store, I think. But no one else.”

“Where are they now?”

“No one’s said where. They were all at the depot yesterday. A small gathering. People bring their old photographs.”

Arthur closed his eyes for a second. The old depot. The photographs. The faces in the snow.

He opened them.

“I’m going to have to talk to them.”

Nell looked at the body through the window, then at him. “You’re not the detective, Mr. Voss.”

“No,” he said. “But I know the line. And I know what the line does when someone lies on it.”

He turned to go, and his hand went into his pocket for his keys. He felt something there that was not a house key. A small brass shape. A tag. A number he had not seen before.

He pulled it out.

The tag read: C-17.

It was identical to the one he had taken from Ruthie’s hand.

Arthur stood very still in the old cabin, and the mountain outside, white and patient, began to sound like a train.
