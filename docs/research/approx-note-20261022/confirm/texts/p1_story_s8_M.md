## final text

Silas Wren had kept the Gull Point light for thirty years. The island was small enough to cross before the fog swallowed the path, but the sea was not small. It arrived each morning with the boats and left with the gulls. One night in October, a storm tore the mail bag from the supply boat and a single letter lay in the lamp room, dry as bone. No stamp. No postmark. No address. Only his name, written in a careful hand: Silas. He read it before he remembered to close the door against the rain, wind, and the cold.

The letter opened with a sentence that made Silas set his coffee cup down as if it had become hot. My name is Hazel, it said. I am not born yet. I write to you because the sea says you will keep my light. The words were neat, childlike in their spacing but sure in their pressure. There was no signature, only a drawing of a bird perched on a post, its wing broken and mending. Silas looked at the drawing and felt a strange pull, like a fishhook behind the ribs. He could almost hear the bird in darkness.

Silas checked the door again, though no one had entered. He checked the supply log, the keeper's log, the old brass list of visitors. Nothing. The island had no future, only weather and work. He should have sent the letter to the mainland, but his hands would not lift it from the desk. Instead he folded it along its old crease, put it in his coat pocket, and climbed the tower to check the lamp. The light swept the water, and for a moment he thought the sea was answering back. It was not an answer. He hoped it was.

The next letter came on a Tuesday, while the fog was so thick the cliff vanished at thirty paces. It sat on the same table, in the same neat hand. My mother is coming, it said. She has a scar on her left hand from a rope burn. She will ask you for the old lantern. Tell her it is waiting. Silas stared at the words. He had not invited any mother to the island. He had not invited anyone, not since his daughter left the village a year before with a man he did not know. That was all.

The third letter arrived with the spring supply. It carried a postmark that belonged to no place Silas could name. The date was three years ahead. My name is Hazel, it read. I am not born yet, but I am learning my letters. I have a blue eye and a red one. I like the sound of the lamp when it turns. I wonder if you will remember me. Silas sat at the desk and let the sentence sink into him. The island, which had taught him patience, was asked to keep faith with a face he could not see.

Still, Silas did not act on the words. He cleaned the tower, trimmed the grass, polished the brass until his reflection looked thinner each week. Then, because the letters kept coming, he began to make small changes. He kept the old lantern from the storehouse and hung it by the door. He planted a row of sea beans along the path. He wrote Hazel's name in the margin of the keeper's log, not as a joke, but as a promise. The name felt less strange each time he looked at it. It began to feel real to him now, too.

The supply boat brought a woman in the spring that had no right to matter to Silas. She was tall, with wind-burned cheeks and a scar on her left hand exactly where the letter had said it would be. She came to the island to repair the new electronic light, but she stayed three days longer than the repair needed. She asked for the old lantern, and when Silas handed it to her, she closed her fingers around the brass as if it were warm from a child's sleep. She smiled at him, and he felt afraid for a while.

Her name was Nora, and she did not know why the old lantern made her pause. She spoke of the mainland, of a workshop, of a daughter she had not yet met. Silas listened. He had learned to listen to the sea, but this was a different sound. That night, a letter waited on the table, and the words were plain. She will be your friend, it said. She will carry my name. When you are ready, tell her I remember you. Silas looked at the open door. Nora was in the small kitchen, washing the salt from her hands.

Silas did not tell her at once. He watched her carry the lantern to the boat, and he watched the way the light caught the water. He thought of his own loneliness, the long hours in the tower, the meals eaten at the same table. He thought of a child who might grow up on this rock, with salt in her hair and a keeper's name in her mouth. When she returned, he handed her a small card with three words. I remember you. Her eyes filled, but she said nothing. She only touched the card and held it then.

The next letter came only after winter, and it was thinner than the ones before. My mother is afraid of being forgotten, it said. She is afraid of the mainland. She is afraid of the child she has not yet made. Please do not tell her I am real. She will ask you to be happy, and you must not answer. Silas read it by the lamp while snow lay on the windows. He wanted to ask why she should be afraid. He wanted to ask what it meant to be a letter that had not yet learned to walk.

Summer came, and Nora came again. This time she brought a book of tide tables and a small tin box. She sat at Silas's table and asked why he kept an old lantern beside a modern light. He did not answer. He watched her hands, which trembled. A letter arrived with no envelope, a note folded into a square. My mother is safe, it said. I am still not born, but I am learning your voice. Silas closed the note and listened to the sea outside, and for the first time in years, it did not sound like a warning.

Years passed. Silas grew old in the way the island made him old: slowly, with salt in his beard and the tower in his bones. Nora left the island, but she returned each spring. She never spoke of the letters. She only asked if the lantern was still bright. One April morning, she told him she was pregnant. The sea was flat and silver. A letter lay on the table, and the handwriting was no longer neat. It was hurried, urgent, alive. I am born, it said. I am not yet myself. Please keep the light for me now, again.

The child was born in winter, and Nora named her Hazel. Silas held her only because the doctor said a strong hand would steady her. The baby opened one eye, blue as the deep sea, and the other red as a lighthouse flare. Silas saw the drawing from the first letter. He did not know whether to laugh or to weep. Nora looked at him with a face full of questions, and he understood that she had carried the mystery longer than he had. The island, he thought, is not a place. It is a promise made of light now.

Hazel grew into a girl who asked for the sea. She climbed the tower before she was safe, and she learned the names of the winds. Silas taught her to trim the wick, to polish the lens, to write in the log. He never told her about the letters. He did not need to. She already knew what it meant to be expected. When the mainland finally sent a crew to remove the old lamp, Silas handed them the lantern and the tin box. They carried the box to the mainland, but they left the lantern on the island still.

Twenty years later, Hazel returned to Gull Point as the first keeper after the lights went electric. She stood in the lamp room and found the lantern still shining. Inside the base was the tin box, and in the box were the letters. The first one had no date. The last one had a date that had not yet passed. She read them all before noon. My name is Hazel, it said. I am not born yet. She put the letters back, lit the lamp, and waited for the sea to bring the next one in the morning, once more.
