## Chapter One: The Last Car of the Summit Line

The mountain had been quiet for eleven months, and Elias Cane had learned to mistake that silence for sleep. His office was the second floor of the old depot in Cold Hollow, where the paint peeled from the beams in long ribbons of gray and the floorboards remembered every pair of boots that had ever passed over them. For thirty-four years he had walked the rails of the Summit Line, listening to the iron speak in a language most people had forgotten. Now, with his badge and his coat hanging on a hook by the window, he made coffee, read the morning paper, and watched the town from above the depot office.

Cold Hollow sat in a narrow valley where the pines leaned east toward the sun and the river cut a dark line through the meadows. The town had been born with the railway, and it had never fully recovered when the line closed. There were still a few crews who maintained the tracks for summer tourism, and the old cars sat along the dead-end siding like wooden horses waiting for a command no one had given. Elias knew them all by name. He knew which sleepers had split in winter and which bolts had begun to weep rust. He knew the exact place where the rails narrowed after the switch at the old freight yard. He even knew which bird sang first at dawn: the mountain bluejay, always before the fog lifted from the river.

The knock came at eight-fifteen, two seconds before he expected it.

Elias set down his cup and crossed the room. The door opened to the face of Sheriff Dan Kessler, though there was no one else with him. Dan was a broad man with a careful voice, the kind who asked questions that already assumed answers. He stood in the doorway, rain still in his hair, and did not come inside.

“You’re coming in,” he said.

Elias looked past him. The road up to the depot was empty, the mountain behind the station blank with white. “For what?”

“A body.”

The word landed in the small office with a heavy thud. Elias felt it in his knees before it reached his mind. He pulled his coat from the hook, buttoned it to the neck, and stepped into the doorway.

“The old siding,” Dan said.

Elias’s breath slowed. “Which car?”

“Car twelve.”

The baggage car. Elias had not set foot in that car since the day the last passenger train left Cold Hollow. He had stood on the platform in the rain, watching the black engine pull away, and felt something in his chest fold shut with a soft finality. He had told himself he would not go back to it. He had told himself many things after his retirement: that he had earned rest, that the mountain needed quieter men, that his wife’s death had taken enough from him. He had not told himself he would survive the silence.

He followed Dan down the hillside path, past the boarded-up stationmaster’s office and the rusted ticket booth. The depot’s front steps were slick. The river murmured below the valley, half-hidden by mist. When they reached the siding, the old cars stood in a line, dark and still. Car twelve was the last. Its door hung slightly open, as if someone had just stepped away.

Dan stopped at the edge of the track. “She’s inside.”

Elias nodded. He did not say her name because Dan had not given it yet, and in his experience a man named too quickly became a person before he had become a case. He stepped onto the ballast. The gravel shifted under his boots. The mountain air smelled of wet pine, cold iron, and something else—something faintly sweet and wrong, like crushed flowers left too long in a closed room.

Car twelve was smaller than the passenger cars, built for freight and mail, its windows narrow and dark. The door swung on its hinges with a groan that sounded almost human. Inside, the air was stale, filled with the smell of old oil and dust. In the center of the floor, sitting upright against the wall, was a woman.

Elias stopped.

Dan did not enter immediately. “You all right, Elias?”

Elias did not answer. He looked at the woman. She had been dressed in a red coat, dark hair pulled back in a loose knot. Her face was pale, the skin drawn tight over her cheekbones. Her eyes were closed. There was blood at the edge of the collar, not enough to alarm, just enough to be undeniable. A leather handbag lay open beside her, the clasp broken. A folded map spread on the floor as if she had been reading it when she died.

The first thing Elias noticed was not the blood.

It was the door latch.

He crossed to the door and touched it. The exterior handle was cold. The interior latch was a brass knob set into the frame, the kind that clicked down with a small, precise sound. He had used a hundred such latches in his career. He knew the difference between a door released by accident and one released deliberately. This one had been pulled up from inside, not pushed down from outside. There was no sign of forced entry, no scratch on the metal, no scuff along the threshold.

“Who is she?” Elias asked.

Dan followed him inside slowly, hands visible. “That’s what I’m trying to figure out. She’s not from Cold Hollow.”

“Name?”

“Clara Wren.”

Elias frowned. He had not heard the name in years. It surfaced from some distant memory, a woman’s voice in a station office, asking whether the evening train waited if the road was bad. “I knew a Clara Wren.”

“Stationmaster’s wife?”

Elias nodded. “Years ago. Before the line closed. She lived in the old station house.”

Dan looked at the body. “That’s not her.”

“Then who is?”

Before Dan could answer, Elias crouched beside the handbag. The leather was good quality, older than most modern goods. Inside were a purse, a comb, a folded scarf, and a small brass object no larger than a thumbnail. He picked it up. It was a key, or at least the head of a key, broken off at the stem. The top was engraved with a number and a symbol.

“Eleven,” Elias said. “And a mountain.”

Dan leaned over. “What does that mean?”

Elias held the key up to the dim light coming through the narrow window. “It’s a car key.”

The words hung in the cold air.

Car eleven had been the mail car. It had been the last car on the line, the one that carried everything from the mountain to the valley and back again. It had been decommissioned before the final passenger service. Elias had personally unscrewed its coupling bolts the winter it was removed from the siding. He had kept a spare set of keys in his office drawer, though he did not remember when. He had thought of them as relics, not tools.

Dan stared at him. “You know something.”

“I know what that key fits.” Elias looked at the broken stem. “This wasn’t cut. It snapped. Someone tried to force the lock from the outside, but the lock held. Then, after that, the door was opened from within.”

“So she let someone in,” Dan said.

“No.” Elias’s voice was quiet. “She let someone out.”

He turned back to the woman. His eyes moved from her coat to her shoes. They were plain dark shoes, the kind a woman in town might wear, but they had been laced strangely. The laces crossed and tied in a way Elias recognized at a glance. It was a trick conductors used to keep laces from working loose while standing on a running platform. The laces were tied from the outside, the knots pulled tight against the foot, the excess tucked inside the shoe. It was a habit born of long years on a moving train.

Clara Wren, if the name was true, had not worn that lacing.

Elias looked around the small car again. The floor was covered in fine dust. There were no footprints except the faint impression of a boot near the door, one step from the threshold and then nothing. No drag mark. No struggle. The handbag had been opened with care, as if the woman had set it down before sitting. The map on the floor was damp at one corner, not with rain, but with something darker.

He picked up the map. It showed the Summit Line, every switch and siding marked in faded ink. Beneath the route to Cold Hollow, in pencil, someone had written a single line: *Do not board after dark.*

Dan looked at it. “That’s odd.”

“Yes.” Elias folded the map carefully. “She was dead before she was found. The body hasn’t been moving for hours, but the floor is still too clean to be certain when she sat down. There’s no mud from the track, no wet marks from the door. She didn’t walk far to get here.”

“Then someone carried her?”

Elias shook his head slowly. “No. She was placed.”

The word fell heavier than the rain outside.

Dan’s face changed. For the first time since he had come up the hill, he looked less like a sheriff giving orders and more like a man trying to decide how much of what he saw he wanted to believe. “We should call it back in.”

“You’ll want to.” Elias looked toward the window. Beyond the glass, the mountain fog had begun to thin. In the pale light, the old siding seemed to stretch farther than it did in the dark. “But I’m not done.”

“Elias—”

“Don’t.” His voice was not raised, but it stopped Dan at the word. “I’m not done. Look at the floor again.”

Dan looked down. Elias pointed to the place where the boot impression had been. Near the heel, half-hidden by the dust, was a small dark smear. Not blood. Something waxy.

“Grease,” Elias said. “But not the kind they use on these old cars.”

Dan frowned. “How do you know?”

“Because the Summit Line used a red compound. This is black. Newer. Military, maybe, or imported for a different track.” Elias crouched and touched the smear with one fingertip. It was dry and gritty. “Someone brought this here.”

Outside, the wind shifted. The fog thinned along the river, revealing a line of bare branches along the far slope. For a moment Elias thought he saw movement, a shape passing between the trees, but the moment broke and the mountain became still again.

“Could someone have come up this path?” Dan asked. “With a vehicle?”

“Not a vehicle. This path is too steep, too muddy. But a person, yes. If they came from the valley road and walked through the pines, they could have reached the siding without being seen.” Elias stood. “But they didn’t come from the valley.”

“Then where?”

Elias looked toward the end of the siding, where the rails disappeared into the fog. “From the mountain.”

The line had not run up that far for years. The rails ended at a point called Blackcap, where the slope became too steep for the old engine and the tracks had been left to rust. No one went there anymore, not even the summer tourists. The area was marked with old warning signs and a chain-link fence that had collapsed in two places. If someone had come from Blackcap, they had walked the dead line for over a mile.

Dan’s eyes moved from Elias to the body to the fog beyond the window. “You’re saying she was brought in from the upper line.”

“I’m saying she was placed here from a direction that makes no sense for a suicide.” Elias straightened. “And I’m saying this key.” He held up the broken piece. “Fits a car that has not moved in twenty years.”

Dan took a breath. “If she’s not Clara Wren, who is she?”

Elias did not answer at once. He looked at the woman’s face one more time. There was a thin scar at the corner of her mouth, hidden by the angle of her head. A small mole near the ear. A ring on the hand that lay open on the floor, the band worn thin.

“She’s a passenger,” he said at last. “Not on a train I know.”

The words were quiet, but they carried.

Dan stepped closer. “What do you mean?”

Elias looked up. “A passenger who never showed up on the manifest.”

The depot phone rang once, a thin sound in the cold air. Dan reached for his radio before Elias could stop him. The static came back in a rush of white noise, then the voice of Deputy Ruiz, flat and urgent.

“Sheriff, you need to see this.”

Dan looked at Elias. “What?”

“I don’t know.”

A beat of silence. Then Ruiz’s voice, lower now. “It’s at the old tannery. Another one.”

The rain returned. It fell in a fine mist over the siding, over the rails, over the red coat of the woman in the last car. Elias turned his face to the window and watched the mountain as if the fog might reveal what it was trying to hide.

Somewhere above him, on the dead line that no train had touched in decades, a whistle sounded.

It was not the modern horn of the valley road, nor the shrill blast of a logging truck. It was the old Summit Line whistle, low and long, carried by the wind across the valley.

Dan went pale.

Elias closed his eyes for a second. He had heard that whistle a thousand times. He knew its pitch, its length, the way it faded against the peaks. He knew who had maintained it. He had maintained it himself.

When he opened his eyes, Dan was staring at him.

“Who did that?” he asked.

Elias picked up the broken key from the floor. It fit perfectly in his palm, as if it had been waiting for his hand.

“Someone,” he said, “who still has a train.”
