# The Divide

The town of Saltcove ran on a single straight street that split the world in two, and the divide on that street ran through the middle of the bakers.

On the north side, behind a shopfront painted the color of sea glass, was Rosa Marchetti's bakery, *La Forniera*. The sign was old, the letters chipped so that the FORNI looked like a question. Rosa had baked there for twenty-two years, and before her her mother, and before her her grandmother, who had brought an Italian recipe book to this gray little corner of the Maine coast in 1951 and said the flour would be enough, the salt would be enough, and the sea would forgive the rest. It had, mostly.

On the south side, directly opposite, separated by nothing more than a two-foot strip of cracked sidewalk and a copper kettle the town had put there in 1987 for no reason nobody could remember, stood *The Salt Fork*, Jonah Hale's bakery. Jonah's shop had been a chandler's shop before, a shipyard office before that. Jonah had renovated it in one fierce summer, all black iron and bare wood, and hung a single loaf of his famous rye on a nail outside as a sign. He had opened the door to his bakery in the autumn, and the town had turned up to the door as if to see whether the sea had finally swallowed the place.

Between the two shops, the divide, a line of weeds pushed up through the sidewalk cracks, was where the old bakers met. Not Rosa and Jonah. The old bakers — Margot, who sold oysters at the wharf, and Denny, who repaired boats and complained about his knees, and Mrs. Pell, who sold seed potatoes and knew who was marrying whom. They gathered on the divide after the morning deliveries and traded stories the way other people traded cards. Rosa never went there. She said the salt wind off the harbor would rot her dough, which was a lie, because her dough was never the first thing out the oven and never the last, and Jonah was right across the street, baking to the same wind.

The rivalry had no single origin, which is how all the good rivalries work. There was the summer Jonah posted a photograph of his deconstructed focaccia — shreds of rye, a smear of cultured butter, a crack of smoked salt on a black plate — to his phone and called it "an argument with the sourdough tradition." There was the day Rosa's mother, in the last winter of her life, had walked to the Salt Fork's counter, still in her cardigan, and asked for a plain loaf, and Jonah, young and sharp and hungry for attention, had made her wait while he explained, in the careful tone of a man showing off a trick, that his rye took thirty days to ferment and that her grandmother's bread would have been "cute." The old woman had asked for her plain loaf again and gone. She was gone within the year.

There were also the good years, the years of the bake-off at the harbor festival, when the two of them would argue in public over yeast ratios and crumb structure, and the whole town would line up the fence, and the judges — always the same two, always the one from the library and the one from the school board — would taste with closed eyes and nod at nothing.

The divide had held for eleven years. It was the thing Saltcove had.

The announcement came in the same week as the fog. A flyer taped to the bulletin board at the town hall, in a font so clean it looked machine-made: *The Saltcove Annual Harbor & Harvest Festival will be returning to the Main Street corridor in October. Special judges from Portland, New York, and Boston. The Grand Loaf Prize will be awarded. Sponsorship has been extended to all businesses on the divide.*

Sponsorship. Rosa read the word three times. Then she went to her shop and stood a while by the door, listening to the sea.

The festival had not been held on Main Street for eleven years, since the harbor moved to the new pier. Main Street had thinned out. Denny's boat repair was closed most of the summer. Mrs. Pell's seed potatoes were down to a single window box. The divide had gone quiet, and the weeds had grown taller, and the old bakers gathered there less often, or not at all, the way people do.

The festival, if it came back to Main Street, would bring people. It would bring the judges, and the cameras the judges brought with them, and the money the cameras brought with them, and it would bring the kind of attention that the developer on the hill had been looking for. Rosa knew the developer. Everyone knew the developer. The one who wanted to tear the old bakery row — the whole row, from the chandler's shop to the oyster house — and build a row of town houses with a restaurant out front. A restaurant with a view. A restaurant that would not need salt wind or sea fog or a single old woman in a cardigan to survive.

If the festival brought people to Main Street, the developer would either have to walk away, or he would have to move in faster.

Rosa did not know which. She knew only that the judges were coming, and the prize was five thousand dollars and a trophy, the Golden Whisk, which had been hanging above Rosa's mother's counter since 1974, and that Jonah was going to be there, baking to the same wind.

She made her rye that morning in the old way, the way her grandmother had shown her: flour from the mill in the valley, water from the well behind the shop, a single starter, no additions, no tricks, a long cold ferment overnight, and the morning it went into the oven at two-hundred-forty, just before the salt wind came off the harbor and made the whole town smell of brine and diesel and the cold iron of the water.

The rye came out of the oven at nine-forty. It had the dark crust, the tight crumb, the flavor of smoke and salt and the particular darkness that comes from a rye that has been baked on a stone that has seen forty years of fire. She sliced one and set it out on the counter where it was visible from the street. She did not slice any. She did not put it in the display case. She set it there, on the bare wood, and left it there all day. It dried. By the afternoon it had the texture of a boot in winter.

Jonah saw it.

He saw it because he stood on the opposite side of the street, at the window of the Salt Fork, and watched her for the whole morning, the way he had every morning since he opened his door, watching her oven and her hands and the way she moved around the shop, the way she did not move fast, and the way she did not move slow, and the way the whole thing was a kind of music that he could not make.

He saw her rye. He saw her slice it. He saw it dry. He saw her leave it out all day, on purpose, because he knew her, and he knew what she was doing, and he knew the trick of it: the dry rye, the one that shows you the crumb, the one that says *this is what it is, and it does not need to be anything else*, and the one that says *you are a stranger, and you do not know what you are doing*.

He made his own that morning. He made the rye that had made him famous, the one that had been in the magazine, the one with the thirty-day ferment and the smoked salt and the crack of butter that came out in a thousand tiny pieces. He made it exactly the way he had always made it. He made it so that it would be right. He made it so that it would be the best thing in the world. And he set it on his counter, on the bare wood, and left it there all day. It dried too.

They did not speak to each other all day. The divide was quiet. The old bakers were not there.

The flour problem came on a Tuesday.

The mill in the valley, the one that had supplied both bakeries for longer than either of them had been alive, the one that had a single grinder and a single scale and a single man named Ed, who had been grinding flour there since before both of them were born — the mill was closing. Ed had put a notice on his door: *Flour runs out October 15. Last order in. Goodbye.*

Rosa went to him on Wednesday. She had enough for two days. Jonah had enough for three. They needed enough for the week until the festival, and enough for the day after, and enough for the winter, because the mill was closing and there was no other mill within a hundred miles that would take a small order, and the big mills in the city would send it in boxes and in plastic and it would not be the same, and it would not be right, and it would not be the thing.

They went to the mill on a Thursday, and they stood in front of his door, and they did not look at each other. Ed came out. He was old, and thin, and his hands were dusted white with flour to the wrist, and he looked at them, and he looked at the street, and he looked at the sea, which was gray and still and full of fog.

"I can do one more run," he said. "Not two. One. You'll have to split it."

They looked at each other. They did not speak. They looked back at him. He nodded. He went inside.

They stood on the sidewalk for a long time. The fog was coming up off the water in a low white sheet, and the town was silent except for the sound of the water, and the sound of their breathing, and the sound of the weeds on the divide growing, or not growing, or doing what weeds do, which is to push through whatever is in their way.

They split the flour. Rosa got half. Jonah got half. They did not speak. They did not have to. They had made a deal, and the deal was a deal, and the flour was the flour, and the salt wind was the same for both of them, and the sea was the same for both of them, and the fog was the same for both of them.

That evening, Rosa stood at her counter and looked at her flour. Jonah stood at his counter and looked at his flour. They had the same flour. They had the same water. They had the same salt. They had the same hands, which were rough and dry and stained with the same pale yellow dust, and they had the same eyes, which were the color of the sea in winter, and the same breath, which was cold and quick and full of the smell of the place.

They were not the same. They had never been the same. One of them had been born in the town, and one of them had come from somewhere else and stayed because the flour was good and the salt was right and the sea was the thing you came to. One of them had learned to bake from a book, and one of them had learned to bake from a hand. One of them had the recipe, and one of them had the instinct, and they had been arguing about it for eleven years, and the argument had been the thing, and the thing had been the town, and the town had been the sea.

The storm came on the eighteenth.

The weather man had said it would be a front, a slow-moving thing that would bring wind from the northwest and rain that would not stop and a cold that would get into the walls and stay there all winter. The town had braced. The harbor closed. The boats were in. The old bakers stayed home.

The divide was quiet. The weeds were still growing.

The storm hit at two in the afternoon. It hit hard and fast, and the wind came off the harbor at ninety, and the rain came down at an angle, and the salt was in the air, and the whole street shook, and the two bakeries shook with it, and the divide shook with them, and the weeds pushed up through the cracks and came out the other side, and the old bakers, who had been on the divide that morning, were gone, and the copper kettle was gone, and the sign was still there, but the letters were not, and the FORNI was gone, and the question was gone, and the sea was the only thing left.

By six, the wind had let up. The rain had stopped. The street was wet and gray and full of water, and the two bakeries were standing, but not the way they had been standing before. Rosa's shop had lost its window. The display case was on its side. The rye, the one that had been out all day, the one that had been the argument and the warning and the thing that said *I am still here*, was on the street, in the mud, in the water, in the salt.

Jonah's shop had lost his sign. The rye on the nail outside was on the ground. The counter was cracked. The three-day ferment, the one that had been in the jar behind the shelf, the one that had been the thing, the thing that had made it famous, the thing that had been in the magazine and the argument and the thing that said *this is what it is*, was broken on the floor, and the culture was gone, and the water was in the jar, and the jar was full.

They stood on the divide. They did not speak. They looked at the street. They looked at the sea. They looked at the weeds.

They did not go home. They stood on the divide for an hour, and the street was still wet, and the sea was still gray, and the fog was coming up again, and the two of them were standing there, and the divide was quiet, and the old bakers were not there, and the copper kettle was gone, and the sign was gone, and the rye was in the mud, and the ferment was in the water, and the flour was half and half and the salt was the same and the sea was the thing.

They did not speak. They did not have to.

The festival was on the second Saturday in October. The fog had not lifted. The street was still wet. The two bakeries were still standing, but not the way they had been, and the display case was in the corner, and the sign was not there, and the rye was gone, and the ferment was gone, and the flour was half and half, and the salt was the same, and the sea was the thing.

They made a deal. They made it on the divide, on a Tuesday, and they did not speak. They made it because there was no other way, and the way was the thing, and the thing was the town, and the town was the sea.

They would exhibit together. They would put their breads on the same table, and the judges would taste both, and the town would come, and the cameras would come, and the money would come, and the developer would have to walk, or he would have to move in faster, and the festival would be the thing that would keep the bakery row standing, and the row would be the thing that would keep the town alive, and the town would be the thing that would keep the sea from swallowing it all.

It was not a truce. It was not a friendship. It was a deal, and the deal was a deal, and the flour was the flour, and the salt was the salt, and the sea was the thing.

The table was in the middle of the street. The fog was still there. The judges were three: the one from the library, the one from the school board, and the one from Portland, who had a face like a closed book and hands like a surgeon's.

They set the rye out first. Rosa's rye. It was the one she had made with the half the flour, and it was the one that had the dark crust and the tight crumb and the flavor of smoke and salt and the particular darkness that comes from a rye that has been baked on a stone that has seen forty years of fire. Jonah's rye was next. It was the one with the thirty-day ferment and the smoked salt and the crack of butter that came out in a thousand tiny pieces. It was the one that had made him famous, and the one that had been in the magazine, and the one that had been the argument.

The judges tasted. They tasted the first one, and they closed their eyes, and they nodded at nothing. They tasted the second one, and they closed their eyes, and they nodded at nothing. They tasted the third, which was the one Rosa had made from the half the flour, and they closed their eyes, and they nodded at nothing. They tasted the fourth, which was the one Jonah had made from the half the flour, and they closed their eyes, and they nodded at nothing.

They did not decide. They did not have to. The town decided. The town came.
