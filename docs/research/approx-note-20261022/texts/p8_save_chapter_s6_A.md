# Chapter One: The Sky Underfoot

The city of Veldrath woke with a shiver.

It began at the edges, where the oldest walls of basalt cracked along ancient fault lines, and it ran inward through the winding streets like a tremor in a sleeping giant. Children were the first to know. They stood in doorways with bare feet pressed against the cool flagstones, watching the cobblestones ripple under the morning light.

Then the bells rang.

In Veldrath, no bell meant anything on its own. A single chime was weather. Two, the tide. Three, something was coming up from the deep water below. The people of the Turtle City had learned, over a thousand years, to listen to their home before they spoke.

Maren listened from the top of her watchtower, her fingers white-knuckled on the railing. Below, the city sprawled across the vast, domed back of the creature in soft layers of stone and timber — terraces of green gardens spilling from the ridge of its spine, the black market crouched in the hollow where its shoulder blade curved, the silver-spired temples clinging to the curve of its neck like barnacles on a hull. Somewhere beneath her boots, something enormous shifted its weight, and the whole city groaned.

"You're doing it again," said old Brother Tessell, who had followed her up the spiral stairs without being invited. The priest of the Deep held his staff across his chest and studied her face with patient, milky eyes. "Listening to the old man."

"I'm listening to the city," Maren corrected. "They're the same thing."

"Are they, though?"

She didn't answer. She had learned, the summer before she was named keeper of the north watch, that some questions in Veldrath weren't meant to be answered. They were meant to be carried.

The turtle — they called it the Shaper, or the Elder, or simply *Him*, depending on the ward — had been walking the waters for longer than anyone could count. No one remembered the last city. No one remembered the one before that. The records in the Temple of Tides began, at the oldest margin, with a single line: *We are walking.* Everything else was built on top of that, layer upon layer, stone upon stone, until the city was indistinguishable from the beast that carried it.

The tide bell rang a third time.

Below, the black market was already stirring. Stalls of salted kelp and coiled rope unspooled from their moorings, merchants shouting to one another in the flat, singsong dialect of the shore-people, who lived along the leading edge of the city and who could feel the water change before any instrument could name it.

Maren descended.

She'd been the north watch's junior keeper for three years now, which in Veldrath meant she had the title of the most important position in the city and the rank of the least. The north watch oversaw the highest and oldest part of the city, the section nearest the Shaper's head, where the streets were narrow and the buildings leaned into the wind with a weariness that the masons insisted was "architectural."

She passed the bread ovens first, where the smell of burnt honey clung to the air. Then the children's quarter, where a game of marble-chasing echoed off the walls and the children were already back in their beds, because in Veldrath a child who didn't sleep early was a child who'd sleep in the tide. Then the fountain of the First Bell, where the water came up from a spring that no engineer had ever found and no priest had ever blessed, and which the city had simply decided, centuries ago, was a gift.

The deep water was coming up.

It came, as it always did, from the north — a cold, grey-green swell that rolled up beneath the city's leading edge and pressed against the belly of the world. The Shaper's shell shuddered. Somewhere in the middle distance, a wall the height of a temple cracked down the middle, and a groan rose up out of it like a sound made by something waking.

Maren reached the tower and looked down.

The city was already in motion. Not the frantic motion of panic — Veldrath had seen a hundred deep-water rises, and its people had learned that the water came for the city's comfort, not its destruction. But it was a motion nonetheless: doors being shut with a care that took time, gardens being drained with long, deliberate channels, the great chains of the north mooring being drawn tight against the piling-stones where they bit into the Shaper's skin.

"Keeper," said a voice at her shoulder.

It was Jorin, her co-keeper, a broad man with a scar across his jaw that had come, according to legend, from the last time the Shaper had turned in his sleep. Maren didn't look at him. She didn't have to. She knew his voice well enough.

"The third bell," he said. "We're rising."

She nodded. "How far?"

He pointed north, toward the edge of the city where the water was gathering in a slow, grey-green line. "Not far. But not near."

Maren turned back to the watch. Below, in the grey morning light, the city of Veldrath sat on the back of a thing too large for language, and the water was coming up, and the bells were still ringing.

She thought, briefly, about what the records said: *We are walking.*

She thought, briefly, that perhaps the walking was about to change.

And then the water broke against the leading edge of the city, and the bells went silent, and the city held its breath.
