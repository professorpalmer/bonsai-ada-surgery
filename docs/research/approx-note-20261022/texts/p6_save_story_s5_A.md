# Salt and the Long Night

The first rule a cook on the *Ark* is taught is not about heat. It is about salt.

"Salt is the only thing we carry from Earth that does not die," Grandmother Ito had told Mara, when Mara was still small enough to stand on a crate and stir the great pot. "Everything else rots. You cook around it."

Grandmother had been dead for nineteen years. The great pot was still there, a dented copper thing salvaged from the pre-departure museum, though now it mostly held rehydrated lentils. Mara had been the ship's cook for twenty-two years — her grandmother's title, her grandmother's uniform, her grandmother's recipes written in a notebook whose pages had been copied so many times the ink had gone grey. She was forty-seven. The *Ark* was three hundred and one years into its five-hundred-year voyage to a world called New Calypso.

Every day she cooked the same meals, from the same recipes, in the same galley on deck seven, where the hydroponics lights hummed their blue-green hymn. Every day the meals were a miracle, and every day she was a little less amazed.

The food on a generation ship is a kind of archaeology. The potatoes came from the seed vault, from a stock so old its DNA had been repaired by hand. The wheat grew in vertical towers under artificial suns. The chickens were small, grey, and bred not to lay a lot of eggs but to live to old age without sulk, so that meat would be a monthly luxury rather than a daily necessity. And the fish — the fish from the recirculating tanks were the ship's real pride, their silver scales the only thing on the *Ark* that had ever tasted like a river.

Mara's job, then, was to make everything else taste like somewhere. That was the trick her grandmother had understood. You could not cook the ocean, but you could cook the memory of it: a little smoked paprika over the fish, and suddenly a girl in Deck Nine remembered a summer in Lisbon, though no one alive had ever been to Lisbon. The *Ark* was a museum of a world it could no longer taste, and the galley was where the exhibit came alive.

On the 1,987th day of the voyage, the meal was the same as it had been on the 1,986th day. Braised chicken. Root vegetables. A bread made from the wheat tower's last good batch, with a crust dark as a wound. The crew of four hundred and twelve people ate at their tables, and the mess hall smelled of thyme and hot fat and the faintest sweetness of steam.

Mara was mid-shift, wiping down the counter, when the chime came — not the meal chime, not the alarm, but the long slow bell that rang only once in the ship's life. The bell of arrival.

She stood still for a long moment, holding a rag, listening to the bell fade. Around her, the crew looked up from their plates, and for a minute the whole mess hall was silent, four hundred and twelve people remembering that they were in a machine, in the dark, in the middle of a universe that had no name.

Then the door opened.

It was Dr. Osei, the ship's navigator, and he had on his face a look that was almost like a child's face — a little too wide, a little too bright.

"The readings held," he said. "We hold for a month. We land in a month."

The galley erupted. Mara felt something lift out of her chest, something she had not known was there — the thing that kept her moving from morning to night, the weight that had been sitting on her shoulders since the day she first put her hands in the flour and knew, with the certainty of a child, that she would never stop. She had carried that weight so long that she had mistaken it for her skeleton.

"Mara," Osei said, softer now, "what should we eat when we get out?"

She looked at her rag, at the counter, at the great copper pot.

"I don't know yet," she said. "Let me find out."

She put it in her pocket.

The technician thought about it. "I don't know," he said. "I've never had it from the ground."

Mara stood in the galley, alone, with the notebook in her hands, and she cried. Not the quiet, private crying of a woman who has been on a ship too long and misses a world she has never seen. The loud crying. The ugly crying. The crying of a woman who is forty-seven and is suddenly, again, a child, small enough to stand on a crate and stir the pot.

On the morning of the landing, the *Ark* came out of the cloud and the sky — a sky with no lights on it, no blue glow from the hydroponics, no metal walls, just a pale, enormous, blue nothing that went on forever — and Mara stood in the galley, alone, and she lit the great copper pot, and she began to cook.

"From a world I never saw," she said. "Cooked by a woman I did see. And eaten by people who are here now."

Lina nodded, very seriously, the way children do when they understand something too big to hold. Then she took a piece of bread and a piece of fish, and she ate.

And outside the galley window, the new world turned, and the light moved over the fields — the first real fields anyone on the *Ark* had ever seen — and the grass was green, and the wind was there, and the salt in the pot was still salt, and the long night was over.
