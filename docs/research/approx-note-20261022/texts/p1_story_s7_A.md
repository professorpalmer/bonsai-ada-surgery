The Light Not Yet Born

For thirty years, Elias had kept the lamp on Gullspire Head, and the island had learned his routine better than the weather did. He climbed the one hundred and twelve steps before sunrise, trimmed the flame, oiled the bearing, and wrote in the log that the sea had been calm, which was a lie, or the truth, depending on which shore you were on. The lighthouse stood on a black tongue of rock that cut into the Atlantic, and beyond it the sea was usually a dark sheet, moving with a slow, indifferent breath. Elias preferred it that way. The sea did not ask him to explain itself, and neither did the light, though men in port sometimes wrote to say they had found their way home because of it, and he would fold their thanks carefully into a drawer with the broken fuses and the spare wicks.

One October morning, when the fog came down the rocks like a slow white animal, Elias found a letter lying on the stone landing where the postboat sometimes left his groceries. The envelope was cream-colored and soft, with no stamp, no postmark, and no return address. The name on the front was written in a small, careful hand: The Keeper of Gullspire. He turned it over. On the back, in ink the color of bruised plum, were two words: From the future. He stood for a long time while the gull cried in the lamp room, and then he opened it because a man who lives alone does not leave a mystery unopened, even when the mystery smells of salt and candle smoke.

Dear Keeper, I know this will seem strange, or worse, like something that might be a joke. My name is Wren, though I do not yet have a body to say it with, or a face for the mirror, or a voice that can be heard in a room. I am not a ship, and I am not a ghost. I am only what I will be, which is not quite the same thing. I have been watching your light for as long as I have been able to watch, which is less time than you might think and more than I would like to admit. You always turn it at the same moment, though your hands grow slower each year. You leave the little brass cap open too long because you like to feel the flame breathe. You think you are lonely, but you are not only that. You are waiting for the sea to become a story again. Please do not be afraid of me. I only need you to know that the light is seen, even before I am born.

He read the letter twice, then set it on the narrow desk beneath the lens. His first instinct was to blame the wind, which had a way of getting into the lamp room and rearranging objects like a child’s toys. His second instinct was to blame himself, because Elias had been alone long enough to hear his own name in the creak of the railing. He tore off a piece of paper, wrote a reply, and addressed it to Wren, but he could not send it anywhere, so he placed it in the same drawer with the unsent notes from his wife before she died.

Another letter came the next morning, then two more the following week. They arrived without sound, sometimes on the windowsill, sometimes folded beneath the door, once pinned to his coat with a sprig of kelp that had no business being dry. Each one was signed Wren, and each one described something true. She knew the exact number of steps he counted when the stairs were wet. She knew which lightbulb in the service room had begun to flicker, and that he had been meaning to replace it since spring. She knew, too, that he still set out two cups of tea on some nights, though the second cup always went cold.

At first Elias told himself he was imagining things, because an old keeper on a rocky island had plenty of reason to imagine. But the letters grew tender. Wren spoke of the smell of the lamp oil, of the way the glass cooled against his palm, of the night he had once fallen asleep in the chair by the door and dreamed of his mother’s garden. She did not mock him for it. If anything, she sounded more sorrowful than he felt, as though the world before birth was a small and bright room, and she was trying very hard not to cry in it.

The last letter before the storm arrived on a Tuesday, when the sky was the color of a wet slate. It was shorter than the others, and the handwriting shook.

Dear Keeper, I can feel the weather changing. There will be a gale, and it will be bad. A boat is coming from the harbor, but it will be too late for the harbor. There is a woman on it who is carrying me, and I need you to be awake. If you are afraid, be afraid. I know fear. It is the only thing I have that is almost real. Keep the light bright. Keep the door unlocked. I am so close, and I do not yet know if I will be brave.

Elias read the letter until the words blurred. He had served on the coast long enough to know the difference between a sea that was angry and a sea that was merely loud. That night, he filled every spare oil drum, polished the lens until it looked like a second moon, and checked the ropes in the boathouse three times. He did not tell anyone, because there was no one, and he did not need to tell himself either. He only wanted the work to be solid, as if solidity could be sent back through the letters and arrive somewhere in the dark before the child who wrote them had learned to draw a breath.

The gale came before midnight. It hit the lighthouse with a sound like the world being torn open, and the light swept out over the water in wide, white arcs. Elias climbed the steps with his hand on the rail, feeling every vibration travel up his bones. Somewhere below, a rope snapped. The door blew against its hinges, though the storm had not yet found its way in. He reached the lamp room and set his hand on the lens, and for a moment the light did not feel like a machine at all but like a heart beating in the dark.

He saw the boat through the glass, a small dark shape struggling against a green sea. It was not the harbor ferry, but a smaller craft, with a figure bent over the helm and another seated behind the wheel, wrapped in oilskins that had turned black with rain. The woman in the stern was holding her side, and Elias knew, without hearing her, that she was in labor. The letter had warned him, but the warning had not prepared him for the shape of the thing: a human need, rising out of the storm like a hand reaching for the light.

His phone had died two days before, and the radio was dead with it, but he knew the rocks by heart. He sent down a flare with the signal mirror, though he had no idea if anyone below could see it, and then he climbed back to the lamp room and turned the beam by hand toward the low channel. The light cut through the spray. Once, twice, three times, he guided it toward the place where the sea had a narrow throat of safe water. Below, the boat turned, groaned, and came closer to the rocks, where a small ledge offered a handhold only a few feet from the water. Elias was at the door before the storm could push it shut again.

The woman in labor screamed as the boat struck the ledge, and Elias caught her shoulder before she could fall. The man at the helm was unconscious, his face split and bleeding, but the woman was alive, and the storm, which had been so cruel, seemed to pause for one second. The sea heaved. Elias held on. He pulled her from the boat by a single strong arm, and for a moment they were only two people against the water, and he thought of all the ships he had guided home, all the thanks he had folded into a drawer, all the years of being useful to the dead and the living alike.

By dawn the storm had exhausted itself, and the sea was only a rough gray thing. A boat from the mainland came with a doctor and a midwife, and they took the woman inside the lighthouse to the dry room where the lamps still burned low. Elias sat on the stone bench and watched them work. When the first cry came, thin and furious, it sounded like the sea had finally found a word for itself. The midwife held the baby up to the air, and for one strange second Elias could not decide if the room had grown brighter, or if he simply remembered what it felt like to need something before it was born.

Later that afternoon, a letter appeared on the desk. It was warm, and the handwriting was no longer shaking.

Dear Keeper, my name is Wren. I am here now. Thank you for keeping the light. I think I will be brave. Love, Wren.

Elias read it once, then placed it in the drawer with the others, where it lay for many years, though he never knew if the baby had ever seen the letter, or if the light had simply remembered.
