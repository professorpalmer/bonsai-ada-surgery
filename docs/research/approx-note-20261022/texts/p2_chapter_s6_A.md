# Chapter One: The Line

The fog came down off the Kettle Range at half past six like a curtain being drawn across the face of the world. Eddie Brandt watched it swallow the pines at the edge of the old rail yard, the way he'd watched it for thirty-one years, only now from the porch instead of from a conductor's cab with a handrail cold enough to bite.

He set down his coffee. The mug was chipped on the lip where a mug had broken, years ago, in a station that no longer existed. He drank it anyway.

Marrow Creek had not had a train in four years. Not since the company had pulled the last scheduled run and then, a year later, had torn up the ties and sold the right-of-way to a logging outfit. The ballast was still there, though. The steel was still there, for a half-mile or so near the old depot, which now housed a used-parts store called Hargrove's. The rest of the line had been swallowed by undergrowth and the slow, patient work of the mountains pushing back on everything human.

Eddie stood at the top of the porch, sixty-three years old, thin in the way a man gets when he stops filling his plate the way he used to. His hair was all white and combed back. His hands, still broad from a lifetime of gripping handrails and lever handles, rested on the railing. He wore a flannel shirt, navy, and jeans that had seen better decades. There was a railroad pin on his left lapel, a small brass thing shaped like a locomotive, and it caught the first gray light that managed to push through the fog.

He liked this hour. Before the town woke. Before the bakery on the square put out its first smell of cinnamon. Before the dogs started barking. The mountains at this time of year were a solid, dark mass above the tree line, and the creek below ran white and fast over the stones. It was the kind of quiet that made a man feel small and held at the same time.

He took another sip. Finished. Set the mug inside.

By seven, the bakery had opened. By eight, the church would ring for the 10 o'clock service, and the whole town of four hundred and ninety-three people would be moving through the square and the stores and the single traffic light at the junction with the highway, which had been green since the last administration and no one had the energy to fix it since.

Eddie had retired six months ago. Not because the company had told him to stop. Because his knees had finally filed a formal complaint, and his wife, Dorothy, had looked at the calendar of his final route—fourteen stations, eleven tunnels, and the long climb through Kettle Pass—and said, *You've earned the porch, Eddie.* So he had earned the porch. He wore it like a uniform now.

He was still making his way to the old depot to get the mail from the PO Box the town had set up for him there, when the phone rang.

It was not a ringing that he recognized from the last thirty years. It was the phone on the kitchen counter, a plastic thing with a cradle and a cord, and it had been silent for three months because he didn't have a number and nobody in town called him. Dorothy had taken his old line when he'd packed up the second bedroom. He'd been living off the cell, which he used sparingly.

He let it ring twice. On the third ring, he answered.

"Eddie."

It was Sheriff Marla Voss. She had a voice that carried the flat, practical accent of people who had been born in the valley floor and never climbed above it. "You know what I'm asking, and I'm asking because you'll come anyway. Don't make me repeat myself."

"I'm at the house. I'll be out in ten."

"Bring a flashlight. It's worse than I thought."

He put the phone down. He stood in the kitchen for a moment, listening to the refrigerator hum. Dorothy was at the table across from him, reading the *Marrow Creek Ledger* with a pair of reading glasses that pushed up onto her forehead. She did not look up. She set the paper down.

"You know," she said.

"I'm going."

She nodded. She looked, for the first time in six months, like a woman who had known something was coming that she hadn't had the words for.

Eddie pulled on his coat. The flashlight was in the drawer by the back door, behind a jar of nails he kept because he didn't trust anyone in town to leave the garage tools where they went. He had the pin on his shirt, still. He walked out into the fog, which had thinned a little but not enough to make the trees individual things anymore. They were just a dark suggestion of shapes.

He did not drive. He never did. He walked the old line, which took him past the Hargrove's used-parts store and along the half-mile stretch where the rails still ran in the grass, and then turned left off the abandoned right-of-way where a gravel road led up into the hills toward the old Kettle Pass tunnel.

That was where they'd found her.

It wasn't a person who'd found her, not exactly. It was a dog. A golden retriever, somebody's, that had gotten out of a yard near the tunnel mouth and had run the old service road up the grade. The dog had started yelping. Its owner, a young woman named Pat Kowalski, had followed it. She found the woman in the tunnel, or rather, she found the woman's shoes first, and then the rest.

Eddie walked fast, but not a run. His knees would have complained by now, and he had learned to respect that. The tunnel was a low cut through the rock, a half-century-old cut, with a concrete arch that had begun to crack in the last couple of years. The fog was thicker up here, almost solid, pressing against the back of his neck. The tunnel mouth was a dark rectangle in the gray.

The dog was sitting a hundred feet down the road, tail tucked, whining.

Sheriff Voss was there, standing in the tunnel entrance with her jacket unbuttoned. A young officer stood beside her, younger than Voss, a kid who had probably just come out of the academy in Boise. There was a body bag on the ground just inside the arch. There were three or four officers standing in the fog, spread out, trying to make the fog look like a perimeter.

"Eddie."

Voss didn't smile. She had a face that was better for not smiling. "Thanks for coming out."

"You called me."

"I needed someone who knew this stretch. The tunnel's got a service ladder on the south face. Old construction, pre-1960s. If she came in from the rock side, the ladder's the only access. I needed to know if the angle would show drag marks or if she'd just... walked in."

He nodded. He'd been a conductor, not a geologist, but he'd walked this line for three decades. He'd stood at the tunnel mouth on a hundred frozen mornings. He knew where the rock wept. He knew where the gravel shifted in the spring thaw.

"Let's look."

Voss stepped aside. He went in.

The tunnel was maybe two hundred feet long, not more. The ceiling was low, just enough to keep the cold out of the rock. The walls were dark, and the fog was coming in from the exit behind him, so the air in the tunnel was still and cold and smelled like wet stone and something else, something faint and sweet that he did not want to name.

The body bag was on the ground, on a flat patch of packed earth near the south wall, about thirty feet from the entrance. He crouched. His knee made its formal complaint, a low throb that he ignored.

"She's not in a tunnel, Eddie," the young officer said. "She's at the end of it. The rock's cut through to a service adit on the far side. The whole tunnel's a dead end on this side. Whoever she was, she could only have come in from the rock side, on that ladder."

Eddie looked at the adit. A narrow cut, just wide enough for a man to crawl through on his belly, going straight into the rock. The ladder was rusted, almost, the rungs green with age. It was the only thing in the tunnel that had not been maintained in decades.

He stood up. He looked at the body bag again. He looked at the adit. He looked at the fog coming in from behind him.

"Who was she?"

"We don't know. No ID. No phone on her. The dog's owner found the shoes, then the bag. We pulled the bag up from the adit."

"From the adit."

"Yes."

Eddie turned. He looked at the ladder. He looked at the adit. He looked at the body bag, which sat on the packed earth as if it had been placed there on purpose, the way a conductor places a handrail: in the spot where it's supposed to be, in the spot where the eye finds it first.

Something sat at the back of his mind, small and cold and patient, the way a signal light does before it turns.

"The adit," he said. "When was the rock side last cleared?"

Voss looked at him. "What?"

"The adit. The rock face. If it's been closed for four years, the rock would have fallen in. The adit would be blocked. You can't walk in and out of a blocked adit."

The young officer shifted.

"Have we had anyone go in?" Voss asked.

"No. Not yet. We haven't had time."

Eddie straightened. He looked at the ladder, at the rust, at the green corrosion eating the metal. He looked at the fog pressing against the tunnel exit.

"Who found the body?" he asked. "The dog. The owner."

"Pat Kowalski."

"Did she go into the tunnel?"

"No. She called it in from the road. Said the dog came back from the tunnel and wouldn't stay."

Eddie turned to Voss.

"Then the body wasn't in the tunnel when the dog got there."

Voss did not answer. The fog thickened.

"I'm not finished," Eddie said. It was not a question. He was still crouching by the body bag, still looking at the angle of the adit, at the way the packed earth had been disturbed, at the small flat stone that was out of place three inches from the bag's corner, as if someone had set it down and then changed their mind.

Voss put her hand on his arm. Not hard. Not soft. The way she had put her hand on him once, years ago, when the line had flooded and he'd been the last conductor to leave the depot and he'd told himself he was fine and she'd said *you're not fine* and he'd said *I'm fine* and she'd just stood there and said nothing, which was the only honest thing she'd ever said to him.

"Eddie."

He stood up. He looked at the adit. He looked at the ladder. He looked at the body bag.

"Nobody's going to the adit," he said. "Not yet. I need to go to the logging office. I need to know who had the right-of-way this year and whether they've done any clearing work. And I need to know if there's a road into the rock face on the far side of the pass."

Voss looked at him for a long time.

"You're not the police."

"No. I'm not."

"Then who are you?"

Eddie picked up the flashlight from the ground, where the young officer had set it. He turned it on. The beam cut through the fog and hit the adit, and the rock was there, black and dry, and the mouth of it was open, and open meant that something had been in there and had gone out again, and the place it had come from was not the tunnel.

He looked at Voss.

"I'm the man who walked this line for thirty-one years. And I know how a man gets into a rock face. And I know how a man doesn't."

He put the flashlight in his pocket. He turned and walked back out into the fog, where the mountains were waiting, solid and dark, and the tunnel mouth was a dark rectangle behind him, and the body bag was still on the packed earth where it had been placed, and the adit was open, and open meant something.

He did not look back.
