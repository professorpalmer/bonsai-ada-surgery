# Chapter One

The city of Kess woke to a groaning sound that came up through the soles of every shoe, through every floor, through the bones of everyone asleep in it.

Not the usual groan. Not the small creak of the morning, when the great turtle Vael shifted in his sleep and the whole city swayed like a boat on calm water. This groan was long, and low, and it ended — after a few seconds of silence — with a crack that ran a white thread of light through the black stone of the harbor district.

Kessa was already awake. She was always awake before the bell, on the morning her roof leaked.

She stood in the doorway of the small room above her master's workshop, her bare feet cold on the flagstones, and listened. The crack had opened in the harbor district, two districts from here, but she could feel the way the city tilted. It had tilted for a heartbeat, and then held, as if Vael himself had drawn the breath in and was deciding whether to let it go.

She pulled on her coat, took her lamp, and went downstairs.

The street outside smelled of brine and wet stone, because the sea was always just below the city, and the shell, though it was a living thing, was covered in the smell of it. Kess was built on the back of a turtle the size of an island — well, the size of the island of Kess, which was the only way anyone ever managed to measure it — and the streets ran across the shell like the ribs of a great animal, following the curves of the scutes that were older than the city.

She hurried down the hill toward the harbor. People were already on the street with lanterns, and the harbor master's men were roping off the cracked stone. A white line, thin as a vein, ran from the edge of the breakwater to the base of the old bell tower.

"It's not going to spread," the harbor master said without turning around, "not today."

"It was spreading yesterday too," Kessa said.

He did turn around. He was a broad man who looked like the harbor district had worn him down. "It's the shell settling. Vael's old. You know that."

"I'm twenty-two, Master Vane. I don't need to know things about Vael from the harbor."

"Twenty-two," he repeated, as if that explained everything. "And you think you know things from the shell."

Kessa didn't answer. She let him look at her until he looked away first, then climbed the breakwater and knelt by the crack. The stone there was warm, and it was warm in a way that was not the warmth of the morning sun. She pressed her palm flat against the scute, the great dark plate that arched above the street, and closed her eyes.

That was what she was good at. Her master, old Merren, had told her that everyone on Kess could feel the shell — every citizen of every district felt it, the way one felt a boat's deck rise under a wave — but only a shell-reader could listen to it. Kess had been Merren's apprentice for six years. She had been told that only once in a lifetime could she feel Vael thinking.

She pressed her palm deeper and listened.

Under the stone there was stone. Under the stone there was the scute, dark and warm as a living thing. Under the scute, something else. A pulse. Slow. Deliberate. And below the pulse, something that was not a pulse at all — a knocking. Three slow taps, far off, and then a silence so deep it seemed to swallow the sea.

Kessa opened her eyes.

"Master Vane."

The harbor master was staring at the crack. "What is it?"

"Something under Vael is knocking," Kessa said.

For a long time, neither of them answered. Above the harbor, the great bell tower — the tower that rang out the morning hour over the whole city — let out a single deep note, and the sound went on, and on, and on, as if the city itself had begun to ring in answer.

Kessa stood, and she thought: the Council will call it a trick of the wind, the way they called everything a trick of the wind. They will send men with chisels and lime. They will patch the crack with mortar, and they will say the city is safe, and they will go back to their ledgers, and they will not hear anything at all.

But Vael had never knocked from below.

She walked back up the hill toward her master's workshop, and behind her, from deep inside the city, another sound began — three slow taps, this time closer.
