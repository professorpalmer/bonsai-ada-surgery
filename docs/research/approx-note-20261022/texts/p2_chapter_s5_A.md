CHAPTER ONE

The first thing Elias Wren noticed about the dead woman was her shoes. They were black oxfords, polished to a soft sheen, with a thin line of red wax worked into the toe caps. The second thing he noticed was that they were not hers. He knew that because the feet beneath them were wrapped in damp grey wool, the toes swollen in a way that had nothing to do with cold, and because the left oxford was tied with a knot so tight it would have taken a strong hand to make and an even stronger one to leave.

He stood at the edge of the old switchyard, where the rails rose out of the black earth like the ribs of a buried animal. Fog had come down from the ridge an hour ago, and it moved slowly through the abandoned tracks, pooling in the crotches between the sleepers. Somewhere behind him, in the valley, the town of Blackwater was beginning its nightly routine: headlights on the bridge road, the low cough of the logging yard, the distant chime from the church steeple where the bells had not rung properly for years.

Elias was sixty-seven years old, and he had not worn a conductor's cap in eleven years.

For forty-three of his life he had walked the Blackwater Mountain Line. He had ridden the dawn train with the windows fogged from the inside, checked tickets at the station in Pine Hollow, and once, in a blizzard so thick it swallowed the world three feet ahead, guided a boxcar through the Kettle Curve by sound and memory. The railroad had closed in the early nineties, leaving the tracks to rust and the town to its own smaller griefs. Elias had kept a small office in the old depot, not because it made much money but because the roof still kept the rain out and the floorboards had been laid by the same men who built the line.

Over the years, people had learned to bring their problems to him the way they brought in broken fences or sick goats. He was not a sheriff, and he had never held a badge. But he knew the mountain, the people, and the places where secrets went to hide. That was enough, in Blackwater, to be called a detective.

The body lay on the middle rail, a little below the point where the main line split toward the old mine. Nora Bell. He would learn her name from the sheriff, though the face already seemed to settle into the shape of someone he had seen at the library a few days ago, a woman with ink on her fingers and a question in her voice.

"Mr. Wren?" The deputy's flashlight beam trembled on his coat. "You shouldn't be out here."

"I was walking," Elias said.

"That's what worries me, sir."

The deputy was young, maybe twenty-five, with the kind of face that had already been hardened by too many long winters. His name, Elias would soon learn, was Paul Marlow. He had been in Blackwater three months, which meant he was still new enough to be surprised by things, and old enough to have learned that surprise was usually a mistake.

"Who is she?" Elias asked.

The deputy did not answer at once. He stepped closer, his boots crunching on the frozen leaves that had collected in the ties. "Her name's Nora Bell. She came into town two weeks ago. Said she was doing research for a university project. The old railroad records. The mine. The bridge."

"The bridge," Elias said.

The deputy glanced at him. "You know something about the bridge?"

"I know the bridge," Elias said, "the way I know most things in Blackwater. It stands when it should, and it lies when it shouldn't."

Marlow shifted the flashlight, and for a moment the beam caught a small brass object half buried in the gravel beside the woman's hand. Elias bent down, careful not to touch the body, and picked it up.

It was a conductor's token, old, with the Blackwater Mountain Line stamped on one side. On the other, in faded ink, was a date: March 14, 1974.

Elias's hand closed around it without his permission.

The year he was nineteen and had been riding as a brakeman's assistant. The year of the Kettle derailment. The year three men had been carried out of the tunnel and never carried in again.

"Where'd that come from?" Marlow asked.

Elias did not answer. He looked down at the woman's face. The fog had begun to thin, just enough to reveal the pale line of a bruise at her temple, not deep enough to be dramatic, but enough to tell him that she had not fallen. She had been placed here. The shoes, the knot, the careful arrangement: all of it spoke of someone who wanted the scene to look like an accident and still be able to read the train's old language.

"Call the coroner," Elias said. "And the sheriff. And get me a cup of coffee that isn't old enough to start a fire."

Marlow pulled out his phone. "You think someone knew her?"

Elias looked at the rails. They disappeared into the fog, then returned, as if the mountain were breathing. "I think," he said, "someone wanted her found where I would find her."

The words surprised him even as they left his mouth. He had not expected to say them so plainly. But the line had been in his bones for so long that it was hard to separate the body from the tracks, the tracks from the memory, the memory from the guilt that had followed him since the derailment. He had been young then. He had been brave enough to do the things a young man does to prove he can survive. He had been wrong enough to survive anyway.

Nora Bell had come to his office five days earlier. He remembered because the day had been cold and the heating in the depot had been acting up, and she had wrapped her scarf twice and still looked as if she was trying to decide whether to trust the room. She had sat across from him at the old conductor's desk, where the brass bell still worked and the ledger from the 1920s was used as a paperweight, and she had asked for help finding a list of workers who had died in the 1974 accident.

"It's not just a list," she had said. "It's a name. My great-aunt was a nurse in the mountain hospital that year. She kept a journal, but a lot of it is missing. Someone took pages."

Elias had asked what she thought had happened to the pages.

"That's what I'm trying to find out."

He had handed her the file he kept in the drawer beneath the desk: a thin folder of newspaper clippings, photographs, and a hand-drawn map of the old switchyard. Most of it had come from his own memory and the stories his father had told him before the stroke took the talking part. The map had shown the Kettle Curve, the water tank, the abandoned tunnel, and a place marked only with an X that his father had called the sleeping spot.

Nora had studied it for a long time. Then she had asked if he would help her look at the tunnel.

He had said yes, because in Blackwater, a yes was a debt and a no was a wound.

Now, with the token still warm in his hand from the small effort of holding it, he thought about the tunnel and felt the first cold thread of something he had spent years trying not to pull.

Marlow was already on the phone, his voice low and urgent. "Yeah, it's Marlow. I need the sheriff at the old switchyard. No, don't tell them it's a train thing. Just... a body. Near the old line."

He hung up and looked at Elias. "You were there when she died?"

"I was there when she was already dead."

The deputy nodded slowly, as if that explained something it did not. "Sheriff's coming. You want to wait?"

"I want to sit," Elias said, "and drink coffee that hasn't been sitting."

Marlow laughed, but it was a thin sound. Then he crouched beside the body and opened a coat pocket with the care of a man who had been trained not to care. After a moment, he held up a folded piece of paper.

"It's in her pocket," he said. "A note."

Elias took it. The paper was soft with age, though it looked too new to be old. The handwriting was small, precise, and slanted to the left. He read it once, then again, and the fog beyond the tracks seemed to press closer.

The note said: You still walk the line. She never left.

Elias looked up. "Who's she?"

Marlow shrugged. "That's for you to find out."

In the distance, a bell began to ring from the church steeple, one clear note and then another, and the sound carried over the switchyard like a finger across the rim of a glass. Elias had not heard the church bells in years. He had stopped listening. But the line remembered. That was the thing about Blackwater. The mountains did not forget, and neither did the people who lived in their shadow.

His phone, which he kept in his breast pocket, began to vibrate.

He took it out. The screen showed an unknown number, no name, no location. The number blinked once, then twice, as if something on the other end were deciding. He should not have answered. He knew that with the same certainty he knew the rails were iron and the fog was water and the dead did not move. But the old habit of the line was stronger than sense.

He answered.

There was a long silence. Then, very faintly, he heard the click of a switch, the kind made in a railroad dispatcher's office, followed by the low hum of a generator and, beneath that, the faint creak of something moving in the dark.

"The train is still running," a voice said.

It was not a man's voice. It was too quiet, too careful, as if the speaker were afraid of waking something. Elias's throat tightened. He had heard a voice like that before, once, in the tunnel after the derailment, when he had thought it was only the wind and was glad he was wrong. He had been wrong then too.

"Who is this?" he said.

"You should have stopped asking," the voice said. "The line doesn't like to be asked."

The line went dead.

Elias stood there, the phone still in his hand, the token in his other fist. The fog was moving again, slower now, almost respectful. Somewhere beyond the switchyard, a branch of the old railroad disappeared into the mountain and did not come back. The body on the rail did not stir. The shoes did not stir. The town behind him did not stir.

But the line, Elias understood, was awake.

He looked down at the note in his hand. You still walk the line. She never left.

Then he did what he had done every night of his life since the railroad closed: he walked.

“You should have stopped asking,” the voice said. “The line doesn’t like to be asked.”

The line went dead.

Elias stood there, the phone still in his hand, the token in his other fist. The fog was moving again, slower now, almost respectful. Somewhere beyond the switchyard, a branch of the old railroad disappeared into the mountain and did not come back. The body on the rail did not stir. The shoes did not stir. The town behind him did not stir.

But the line, Elias understood, was awake.

He looked down at the note in his hand. *You still walk the line. She never left.*

Then he did what he had done every night of his life since the railroad closed: he walked.
