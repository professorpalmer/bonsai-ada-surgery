# Chapter One — The Still Ground

The first thing every child of Veranthos learns is that the ground beneath their feet is not the ground.

It is a living thing. It is warm in summer and cold in winter, and it moves. Every morning it leans a little toward the east, the harbor bells swing wide in the ripples, and salt water runs over the seawall with a long, lazy sound. The old women clutch their doorframes and mutter, "Slow down, big one," as if a mountain that has slept for an age could take offense at being asked to hurry.

Ghorvane is older than the oldest story. The priests have a name for him — Ghorvane, the Long-Strider — but the sailors don't. The sailors call him the Tide, because he carries Veranthos the way a wave carries a boat: you do not argue with it, you do not measure it, and you certainly do not expect it to stop.

Six thousand years of footsteps, the priests say. Six thousand years, and every spring, on the same morning, the turtle's great left foreleg crosses the Shallows and the city rises half a cubit, as if the current beneath him were lifting him on a wave only he could feel.

Lira had watched the crossing three hundred times. She was nineteen, and she was already tired of watching the same thing, because everyone in the city watched the same thing, and in Veranthos the only way to be different was to look up.

She did that now.

From the mossy rim at the city's highest point — the place where the shell's curve met the open sky and the wind carried salt spray from three different seas — Lira could see the whole world. The city rose around her in terracotta roofs and green-tiled towers, stacked up the turtle's back like barnacles on stone. Below the edge lay the shell itself: a vast pale mosaic of plates, each the size of a district, veined with dark lines that pulsed — or at least Lira believed pulsed — when the turtle drew its deep breath. The breath came once a day, at dawn, and the whole city shivered.

And there, beyond the edge, the water: black, endless, and empty of any land she had ever seen.

No one had ever seen the turtle's feet.

They said the land was miles below. They said the water was so deep that a stone dropped from Veranthos would fall for a month before touching the bottom. The priests called it legend. The sailors called it prayer, and they tossed coins into the gaps at the edge and let the current take them.

Lira had work to do. She was an edge-sailer, one of the few who worked the rim where shell met sky. It was dangerous work, the only work that paid, and the only work that let a girl like Lira — whose mother had drowned twelve years ago, swept over the seawall in the Tide's morning breath — stand where no one else could.

Her tools were a long iron pole, a coil of braided rope, and a dull satisfaction in the work. She trimmed the moss that grew along the shell's rim, the moss that kept rain off the old cracks and salt out of the seams. The edge-farmers picked what she cut and sold it in the lower markets for a coin a handful. She did not mind the work. She minded the questions. On the rim, no one asked a girl where her mother had gone.

It was the first morning of the long crossing when the turtle stopped.

It happened the way all the great things in Veranthos happened: slowly, and without warning. One minute the harbor bells were swinging and the seawall was sloshing; the next, both had gone still. The trickle of salt water over the wall had thinned to nothing. The wind had not changed. The sky was the same gray it had been since she was a child.

And the city was no longer tilting.

Lira felt it before she understood it, the way you feel a step before you see the foot that made it. The lean of the morning, the small rise of the foreleg in the Shallows, the familiar half-cubit lift — all of it simply absent. The ground under her boots was flat.

Flat.

She set the iron pole against the rim and stood. Below, the city had gone quiet in the way only large places can: not silent, but held, like a mouth that has forgotten its word. No one was running. No one was shouting. People stood in their doorways with their hands on their doorframes and looked at each other across the streets, the way they used to during the breaths, but slower, as if they had not been certain it was happening.

A boy from the farm quarter had dropped his moss basket. The baskets lay open in the alley, green fronds spilling into the cracks, and no one was going to pick them up.

Somewhere far below, in the deep and dark water where no one had ever looked, something had stopped.

The priests would call it a sign. The sailors would gather at the rim, press their palms flat against the shell, and throw their coins into the gaps, praying the Tide remembered which way was east. The city council would convene in the deep hall, where the acoustics made every whisper sound like a verdict.

And Lira, the edge-sailer, would stand where the moss grew and feel, for the first time in her nineteen years, the bottom of the world.

Because as she stood there, listening to the silence of a city that had forgotten how to be still, she felt it: a tremor beneath the tremor, slow as a heartbeat that had not yet decided to beat. The shell was warm under her feet. It had always been warm. But the warmth was moving, rolling upward through the stone and the moss and the plates, the way a tide rolls up a shore.

Ghorvane was still moving.

He was just moving in a different direction.

And far, far below the city, in water so deep it had no name, something enormous opened its eye.
