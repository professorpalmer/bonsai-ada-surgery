"The Unwritten Light"

The first envelope appeared on the third Tuesday after the autumn gales. Elias Wren found it under the landing board at the base of Crag Point Light, as if the sea had set it there with a thumb and withdrew. It was a square, cream-colored thing, unmarked except for his name. There was no postmark, no ship's seal, no stamp. The paper was dry. That was the thing that frightened him most.

He opened it in the lamp room, where the great lens turned its patient glass heart in the oil light. Inside, on a sheet of paper that smelled faintly of salt and milk, was a line of writing small and slanted.

Dear Grandfather,
My name is Mara. I am not born yet. I am sorry for the impossibility of that sentence. I am writing to you from the lighthouse after you have gone, and I am asking you to keep the light. The lamp is not a machine. It is a door. The sea is only the water in between.

Elias stood there, the letter in his hand, while the lens turned. He thought of his daughter Nora, three miles down the coast in the village of Carr's Cove, whose belly was round as a moon in its first quarter and whose eyes were always red from sleeplessness. He thought of her husband, Callum, out on the trawler, and of all the impossible things the sea had already asked of him. He folded the letter and put it in his coat.

For three days he said nothing. He climbed the stairs, trimmed the wick, polished the brass, logged the barometer, and fed the foghorn its breath. On the fourth day another letter lay on the landing board.

Dear Grandfather,
I am in the quiet before. I am only a shape in my mother's arms. My mother calls me Mara, though I have not yet been allowed to make the sound. I am afraid of the dark, but I have found that the dark is only a place where light is waiting to be remembered. Please do not let the light go out.

He read it again. He read it until the words lost their edges and became something like the rhythm of the waves. That night he did not go to bed. He sat in the lamp room and watched the beam sweep the black water, and he thought: A letter from a child who is not yet a child. A name from a mouth that has not yet opened. It was the kind of thing that happened to old men, he told himself, or to men who had spent too long alone with the sea.

But the sea, he knew, was not a person. It did not care about his loneliness. It only had its own long intentions.

The third letter came on a morning when the sky was the color of a bruise and the wind had a new voice in the pines.

Grandfather,
A storm is coming from the west. It will have a face like a hammer. Callum will be on the water, and the trawler will be low in the swells. If you light the red candle in the upper lantern and turn the lens toward the headland, they will see the point. If you do not, the sea will take the trawler, and Callum will not come home to Nora, and I will be only a name she has whispered to a room.

Light the candle. I am asking you. I am only a question in a body, but I am asking.

Elias looked at the barometer. The needle had been falling since dawn. He looked at the red candle, a small glass thing that had not been lit in years, kept in a drawer because it was the old keeper's emergency light before the lamp was electrified. He thought of Nora's face, white with worry. He thought of Callum, who had taught him to tie a bowline when he was a boy and who now had a daughter on the way who was, according to a letter in a cream-colored envelope, already old enough to ask for mercy.

He lit the red candle.

The storm came before noon. It rose from the west with a sound like a wall being torn down. The rain struck the windows in sheets, and the foghorn moaned through the tower until the metal seemed to be tired. Elias climbed the stairs with the candle in one hand and the logbook in the other, and he stood in the lamp room and turned the lens with his hand, though the machine was turning it for him. He kept the beam on the headland where the rocks rose like broken teeth. He watched for hours, and the sea threw itself against the rocks with a fury that made the tower lean in his imagination, though it did not.

At two in the morning, a horn sounded from the water. Then another. A light, small and yellow, passed near the rocks and moved away. Elias held his breath until the sound of the trawler's engine faded into the storm. His hands were shaking. He put out the candle and wrote in the logbook: Weather violent. Tractor? No, trawler? Let's not. "A light seen near the headland. All clear."

He went down to the landing and sat on a dry stone until the sea began to loosen its anger. In the morning, Callum came to the light, soaked and silent, and Nora met him at the door. She held her belly and said, "The baby turned." Elias looked at Nora's face and saw something in her eyes he could not name. It was not relief, exactly. It was recognition.

The letters stopped after that. For a while, Elias thought they had been a dream brought up by the storm, a trick of his solitary mind. He still kept the envelopes in a tin by the stove, but he did not speak of them. Nora came to the lighthouse once a week, bringing soup and news from the village. She spoke of the baby's name, Mara, as if it had been waiting for her. She said she had dreamed of a girl with Elias's eyes, standing in a tower of glass and salt, writing a letter to her grandfather before she was born.

"You have seen her," Elias said, though he was not sure it was a question.

Nora looked at him for a long time. "I don't know what that means."

The baby was born in the deep of winter, on a night when the sea was calm and the sky was so clear that the stars seemed to have been laid out on wet glass. Elias was not there when Mara first cried. He was in the lamp room, cleaning the lens, when he heard the wail from the village, thin and bright, like a note struck in a cold room. Nora's voice came up the hill, and then Callum, and then the baby, whose cry was not loud but certain.

Elias went down the hill with his coat over his arm. In the small house by the harbor, Nora sat in the chair by the fire, and Callum stood behind her with his hands on her shoulders. In Nora's arms was the baby, red and furious and small.

"Grandfather," Nora said, and her voice cracked. "She's here."

"Here," she said.

He took the baby into his arms. She was so light. She was so warm. She opened her eyes, and they were dark and new, like water over stones.

For a moment nothing happened. Then, in the pocket of his coat, he felt the thin edge of paper. He had not put anything there. He took it out: a cream-colored envelope, dry and warm, addressed in small slanted letters.

Dear Grandfather,
I am here now. I am still not real, but I am coming. The sea is not only the water in between. It is the road.

Hold me. The light is on.

## final text

**The Unwritten Light**

The first envelope appeared on the third Tuesday after the autumn gales. Elias Wren found it under the landing board at the base of Crag Point Light, as if the sea had set it there with a thumb and withdrew. It was a square, cream-colored thing, unmarked except for his name. There was no postmark, no ship’s seal, no stamp. The paper was dry. That was the thing that frightened him most.

He opened it in the lamp room, where the great lens turned its patient glass heart in the oil light. Inside, on a sheet of paper that smelled faintly of salt and milk, was a line of writing small and slanted.

*Dear Grandfather,*  
*My name is Mara. I am not born yet. I am sorry for the impossibility of that sentence. I am writing to you from the lighthouse after you have gone, and I am asking you to keep the light. The lamp is not a machine. It is a door. The sea is only the water in between.*

Elias stood there, the letter in his hand, while the lens turned. He thought of his daughter Nora, three miles down the coast in the village of Carr’s Cove, whose belly was round as a moon in its first quarter and whose eyes were always red from sleeplessness. He thought of her husband, Callum, out on the trawler, and of all the impossible things the sea had already asked of him. He folded the letter and put it in his coat.

For three days he said nothing. He climbed the stairs, trimmed the wick, polished the brass, logged the barometer, and fed the foghorn its breath. On the fourth day another letter lay on the landing board.

*Dear Grandfather,*  
*I am in the quiet before. I am only a shape in my mother’s arms. My mother calls me Mara, though I have not yet been allowed to make the sound. I am afraid of the dark, but I have found that the dark is only a place where light is waiting to be remembered. Please do not let the light go out.*

He read it again. He read it until the words lost their edges and became something like the rhythm of the waves. That night he did not go to bed. He sat in the lamp room and watched the beam sweep the black water, and he thought: a letter from a child who is not yet a child. A name from a mouth that has not yet opened. It was the kind of thing that happened to old men, he told himself, or to men who had spent too long alone with the sea.

But the sea, he knew, was not a person. It did not care about his loneliness. It only had its own long intentions.

The third letter came on a morning when the sky was the color of a bruise and the wind had a new voice in the pines.

*Grandfather,*  
*A storm is coming from the west. It will have a face like a hammer. Callum will be on the water, and the trawler will be low in the swells. If you light the red candle in the upper lantern and turn the lens toward the headland, they will see the point. If you do not, the sea will take the trawler, and Callum will not come home to Nora, and I will be only a name she has whispered to a room.*

*Light the candle. I am asking you. I am only a question in a body, but I am asking.*

Elias looked at the barometer. The needle had been falling since dawn. He looked at the red candle, a small glass thing that had not been lit in years, kept in a drawer because it was the old keeper’s emergency light before the lamp was electrified. He thought of Nora’s face, white with worry. He thought of Callum, who had taught him to tie a bowline when he was a boy and who now had a daughter on the way who was, according to a letter in a cream-colored envelope, already old enough to ask for mercy.

He lit the candle.

The storm came before noon. It rose from the west with a sound like a wall being torn down. The rain struck the windows in sheets, and the foghorn moaned through the tower until the metal seemed to be tired. Elias climbed the stairs with the candle in one hand and the logbook in the other, and he stood in the lamp room and turned the lens with his hand, though the machine was turning it for him. He kept the beam on the headland where the rocks rose like broken teeth. He watched for hours, and the sea threw itself against the rocks with a fury that made the tower lean in his imagination, though it did not.

At two in the morning, a horn sounded from the water. Then another. A light, small and yellow, passed near the rocks and moved away. Elias held his breath until the sound of the trawler’s engine faded into the storm. His hands were shaking. He put out the candle and wrote in the logbook: *Weather violent. Light seen near the headland. All clear.*

He went down to the landing and sat on a dry stone until the sea began to loosen its anger. In the morning, Callum came to the light, soaked and silent, and Nora met him at the door. She held her belly and said, “The baby turned.” Elias looked at Nora’s face and saw something in her eyes he could not name. It was not relief, exactly. It was recognition.

The letters stopped after that. For a while, Elias thought they had been a dream brought up by the storm, a trick of his solitary mind. He still kept the envelopes in a tin by the stove, but he did not speak of them. Nora came to the lighthouse once a week, bringing soup and news from the village. She spoke of the baby’s name, Mara, as if it had been waiting for her. She said she had dreamed of a girl with Elias’s eyes, standing in a tower of glass and salt, writing a letter to her grandfather before she was born.

“You have seen her,” Elias said, though he was not sure it was a question.

Nora looked at him for a long time. “I don’t know what that means.”

The baby was born in the deep of winter, on a night when the sea was calm and the sky was so clear that the stars seemed to have been laid out on wet glass. Elias was not there when Mara first cried. He was in the lamp room, cleaning the lens, when he heard the wail from the village, thin and bright, like a note struck in a cold room. Nora’s voice came up the hill, and then Callum, and then the baby, whose cry was not loud but certain.

Elias went down the hill with his coat over his arm. In the small house by the harbor, Nora sat in the chair by the fire, and Callum stood behind her with his hands on her shoulders. In Nora’s arms was the baby, red and furious and small.

“Grandfather,” Nora said, and her voice cracked. “She’s here.”

Elias looked at the child. The baby had no name in her face yet, but her eyes were closed, and her mouth was moving, as if she were already learning the sound of the world. He knelt. He reached out a hand, but before he could touch her, Nora lifted her higher.

“Here,” she said.

He took the baby into his arms. She was so light. She was so warm. She opened her eyes, and they were dark and new, like water over stones.

For a moment nothing happened. Then, in the pocket of his coat, he felt the thin edge of paper. He had not put anything there. He took it out: a cream-colored envelope, dry and warm, addressed in small slanted letters.

*Dear Grandfather,*  
*I am here now. I am still not real, but I am coming. The sea is not only the water in between. It is the road.*

*Hold me. The light is on.*

Elias looked up at Nora. Nora looked at Callum. The baby in his arms made a small sound, not a cry but a note, as if the lamp room had turned a corner and the beam had found a new shore.

He held her, and the light turned, and the sea went on being what it had always been: a door, a wall, a road, and a silence that sometimes, in the dark, learned to write.
