# The Postmark of Tomorrow

The last time Silas Finch saw the mainland, it was through a gap in the fog where a ferry had passed without slowing, a dark smudge of iron and glass that was already gone by the time he raised his binoculars again. He'd been at Garrick Point for eleven years, and in those eleven years the mainland had become a rumor he told himself on quiet mornings to justify the coffee he burned in the tin pot.

The letters began in March.

The first one came wrapped in oilskin, addressed to *The Keeper, Garrick Point Light, No. 7, the Island That Isn't on the Maps.* The envelope was the cream color of old teeth. There was no postmark, no stamp, no return address—only a small circle of wax on the corner, deep blue, stamped with a symbol he didn't recognize: a spiral that looked like it might, if he squinted, be a bird in flight.

Inside was a single page, typed on a machine that still had the faint impression of a human hand on the keys.

*Dear Mr. Finch,*

*My name is Lena. I haven't been born yet, and I know how that sounds, so I'll stop explaining. I wanted you to have the first letter before anyone else. I wanted you to have the time. I'll be here in two years, on the first Tuesday after the equinox, and I need you to not be the man you were last spring. I need you to be the man I'm about to meet. I don't know who you'll be yet. I just know that the version of you who drinks alone in the kitchen and talks to the radio when it's off is not the version I'll come home to.*

*Don't make me come home to a stranger.*

*Yours across the gap,*
*L.*

Silas read it four times. He sat in the kitchen of the lamp room, the kettle going on, and he did not pour the water. He held the paper to the gas lamp and watched the words swim in the amber light, then set it on the table where it would dry. He made a note in his log: *First of a series. Origin unknown. No reply address. Weather clear.* He did not write: *The woman is not born.* He could not have written it and then meant it.

The second letter came four days later. The third, eight days after that. Then weekly, then twice a week, then daily for a stretch in the spring when the gales made the sea a white noise against the rocks and he couldn't sleep either way.

Lena wrote about her life as if it were a country she was crossing on foot. She described her mother's kitchen, the specific blue of the tiles above the sink. She described the first day of school, the name of a teacher who called on her and she froze. She described a summer when she'd learned to ride a bike without training wheels and the way the wind in her hair made her feel she might detach from the rest of the world and float. She was nine, then ten, then eleven, and each letter was a little more complicated, a little more urgent, the typed words growing less careful as if she were learning to write by the hour.

She never said where she was going. She never said when. But she described *you*—Silas, the keeper, the man who kept the light turned and the log straight and who walked the headland at dawn to check the stairs—and she described him the way someone describes a house they've only seen in a photograph and are trying to guess the smell of. *You must be lonely in a way that isn't sad,* one letter read. *It's just the shape of the day. You fill it with the turning of the beam and the smell of the oil and the sound of the fog horn at dusk, and that's not loneliness, that's the whole of the day, and I think it must be enough for you but I'm sorry it's only enough.*

He read each letter and then put it in the drawer by the stove, behind the tin of fish hooks and the spare matches. By summer there were forty-three of them. He began to know the cadence of her hand in the type—where she skipped the letter *t*, where she double-spaced a paragraph when she was thinking, where she wrote *I'm sorry* before a question that wasn't an apology. He knew she would like the sea. He knew she would hate the smell of the lamp oil. He knew she was going to ask him, on the first day, whether the light was still there, whether he was still there, and that he was going to answer, *Yes and yes*, and that the pause before the *yes* would be longer than the word itself, and that she would not notice, and that would be all right.

In October a doctor's report arrived by the mainland courier boat. It was addressed to a Mrs. Lena Voss, care of Garrick Point. Silas didn't know what to do with it. He read it twice. A girl, six years of age, healthy, expected in two years. He set it on the table and sat for a long time. The lighthouse beam swept out over the black water, sweeping over and over, and he understood, with a certainty that settled into him like cold water, that the letters would stop when she was born. She would be born and the letters would stop because there would be no one to write them to. And he would have to keep the light.

The last letter arrived in April.

It was longer than the others, three pages, the typing uneven, some keys struck too hard, some barely at all.

*Dear Mr. Finch,*

*This is the last one. I'm going to be born in a week and I don't know what it'll be like but I think I'll be all right. I've been thinking about the day I come to the lighthouse. I've been thinking about what I'll say first. I've tried a lot of things. I think I'll just stand in the door and not say anything for a while, because I won't know what to say, and I'll be looking at your hands. I'll be looking at your hands and I'll be thinking that you must have worn out the wood on the stairs a hundred thousand times and that the oil in the lamp is the same oil you used when you started and that the light is the same light and that you're still here and I'll be here and the space between the two of us will be the whole of the gap between me and you and I don't know how to cross it and I'm sorry.*

*You don't have to be the man I wrote you to be. I think I was being unfair. I just need you to be there. I need you to be the one who's still here when I get off the boat and stand on the rocks with the wind in my hair and say, "So. Is it still there?" And I need you to say yes.*

*The rest I'll figure out. I promise. I've been practicing saying that for two years and it's the hardest thing I've ever practiced and I don't know if it'll be true. But I'll try.*

*Don't make me come home to a stranger. Or a man. Just make me come home to someone who's been here.*

*Yours, finally, across the whole of it,*
*L.*

Silas read it in the kitchen. The kettle was on. He poured the water into the pot and watched it fill and boil and fill again. He put the letter in the drawer with the other forty-four. He wiped the counter. He went up to the lamp room and lit the wick, and the light came on, slow and certain, sweeping out over the dark water where the mainland was only a rumor and the island was only a point and the gap between them was only the dark.

The first Tuesday after the equinox came on a Tuesday in November that the sea had made into a thing of white noise. The boat came at ten in the morning, a small ferry, dark iron and glass. He was at the headland stairs when he saw it, and a small girl stood at the rail with her hand on the cold metal and her coat open and her hair doing what hair does in wind.

She looked up. He saw her face. He saw the specific blue of the tiles she had written about, somehow, in the set of her jaw. He saw the shape of a woman who had been crossing a country of years on foot, arriving.

She stood in the door of the lamp room and did not say anything for a while. She looked at his hands. She looked at the stairs.

"Is it still there?" she said.

Silas Finch, keeper of Garrick Point Light, No. 7, on the island that wasn't on the maps, looked at the beam turning in the window behind him and he said, "Yes and yes."

The pause before the *yes* was longer than the word itself.

She did not notice. She stepped inside, and the wind closed the door behind her, and the light went on.
