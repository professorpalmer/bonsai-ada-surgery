# The Taste of Home

Mara had been a cook for forty-three years and had never once tasted a strawberry.

This fact sat in her mind like a splinter she could not remove. The orchards of Earth—she had grown up under them—produced fruit so red and sweet it made children cry when they bit into it. On the *Meridian*, the strawberry was a rumor. A pale, watery ghost grown in the hydroponic bays under fluorescent light, tasting of nothing at all but water and regret.

Still, every spring she served them to the children. They always said "yummy," and she always said "thank you, my love," and neither of them believed either sentence.

---

The galley was a cathedral of steam. Mara moved through its long curved halls with the confidence of someone walking a familiar street in the dark. The *Meridian* was a generation ship, nine hundred meters of hull and habitability, carrying twelve thousand souls and the memory of a world it would never see again. The crew of the galley—herself and nine others—cooked, cleaned, and quarreled in the same loop they had passed from parent to child, and from child to child, for three generations.

Mara was of the third. Her mother, Yana, had trained her at the stove. Yana's mother before that. Before that, the original cooks, the ones who had written the first recipes by feel, tasting from memory of Earth, their hands clumsy in a ship that had no gardens and no sky.

"You're staring at the log again," said Odo, her sous, setting a tray of protein blocks at the pass. He was a heavy man with kind hands, and he always made his coffee with too much salt. "If you keep reading that, you'll forget to flip the fillets."

"I'm not reading," Mara said. "I'm checking."

"The nutrient report."

"Mm."

Odo let out a long breath. "You can't change the algae quota, Mara."

"No," she agreed. "But I can make it taste less like algae."

This was the secret of her craft, the thing she guarded more fiercely than any recipe. The ingredients were fixed by the ship's scientists: algae, recycled proteins, mineral salts, the occasional fungal block from the fungal farms in deck nine. Nothing fresh. Nothing growing. But Mara had learned, over decades, a language of flavor that could coax something almost like memory out of the bland. A pinch of cultured lemon extract here. A drop of fermented chili there. Smoke made from the vaporized bark of the ship's ornamental trees, a trick she had found in the old archives and never written down.

With the right touches, a bowl of algae broth could almost taste like the soup her grandmother had made in a village with a real sky. Almost.

The children loved her for it. They came to the mess hall in droves after school, and they laughed, and they said it was the best meal on the ship, and they meant it.

---

That evening, a young engineer named Kael came to the galley and stood in the doorway, looking uncertain.

"Mara," he said. "Can I ask you something?"

"Go ahead, son."

"The ship's AI ran a simulation last week. About the orchard bay."

Mara's hands stilled over the cutting board. "The orchard bay."

Kael nodded slowly. "They're talking about repurposing it. Deck four, the whole bay. The algae reserves are getting low, and the scientists say if they convert the orchard space to a second algae farm, it might buy us another decade of comfortable rations."

"And the fruit?"

"There won't be any fruit." Kael's voice was quiet. "There won't be any strawberries."

Mara set down her knife. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady—but something in her chest had tightened, something small and bright and ancient, like a candle flame in a draft.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Three years, maybe four. Pending the board vote."

Mara looked at the long counter stretching before her, lined with the day's harvest—pale, watery, and utterly without taste. She looked at the log Odo had been worried she was reading, still open to the nutrient report. The numbers. The cold, clean numbers that said how much could be grown, and how much was needed, and how the future was a budget.

"I need to talk to the board," she said.

---

The hearing was small and unglamorous. Three scientists, two engineers, and a ship's administrator who spoke in careful, balanced sentences. Mara stood before them in her white coat, the one with the little embroidered sun over the breast pocket that Yana had stitched years ago, and she said:

"My name is Mara. I am the galley chef. And I am asking you to consider what you are not just taking from this ship, but from the people on it."

The first scientist, a thin woman with tired eyes, opened her mouth. "Ma'am, with respect, this is a resource management—"

"It's a resource management question," Mara interrupted. "Yes. I understand that. I've spent forty-three years managing resources. I've stretched a single kilogram of protein into four meals. I've made the children believe that a pale little thing that tasted of water was a strawberry, because I thought that was what they needed. And I don't blame myself for that. I was doing my best. But you are not talking about algae and protein blocks. You are talking about memory. About the last place where something grew and was sweet, and where a mother could taste her childhood."

The room was very quiet.

The second engineer, a young man with ink-stained fingers, looked at Mara for a long time. Then he said, very quietly, "We've never considered it that way."

"No," Mara said. "I know. That's why you're going to sit down with me in the galley tomorrow, and I'm going to cook for you. And you're going to taste it. And then you're going to tell me whether it's worth keeping."

---

The next morning, Mara cooked.

She cooked the entire day without rest, working the long counter with a focus that bordered on prayer. She made a broth from the last of the almond oil she kept hidden in a tin beneath the floorboards—almond oil, a relic from the first generation, a gift from an old woman who had come to the galley at the end of her life with a small bottle wrapped in cloth and a story.

She made a soup with cultured lemon and smoked bark, and she served it in bowls that her mother had made, bowls with chipped blue rims. She made a small cake with the watery strawberries, sweetened with sugar that was more mineral than anything else, and she cut it into thin, even slices and placed it on a white plate.

When the engineers and the scientists arrived, they looked around the galley, and they looked at Mara, and they sat down.

She did not say a word.

They ate.

One of them, a young woman who had been the first to speak in the hearing, stopped halfway through her bowl of soup. She set it down. She picked up the plate of cake and looked at it for a long time.

"What is this?" she asked.

Mara's voice was steady. "It's what we have. It's almost. Not quite. But it's what we have."

The young woman took a small bite of the cake. She chewed. She closed her eyes.

And then, very quietly, she said: "This is a strawberry."

The others looked at each other. The second engineer—the one with the ink-stained fingers—set down his fork. "She's right," he said. "It's not. But it is. That's what she's saying."

Mara sat down across from them. Her hands were still steady. Her heart was not.

"I've made this soup for forty-three years," she said. "And every time, I've been asking myself the same question. Not 'is this good enough?' But 'do they need it?' Because there was a time, before the *Meridian*, before the ships, before any of this—there was a time when we just grew things. We just let them grow. And they grew sweet. And that was enough."

She looked at the plate of cake. The strawberries on it were pale and watery. They were, by every measure, a failure. They were also, somehow, the most important thing that had ever been made in the galley of the *Meridian*.

"You don't have to keep them for the taste," Mara said. "You keep them so the children know what it was like to have a place where things grew sweet. You keep them so that if we ever go back, if we ever land, if we ever set foot on soil that isn't recycled—we'll remember what we're coming home to."

The hearing ended without a vote. The board was asked to reconvene in six months, after the scientists had finished their simulations. In the meantime, the orchard bay would remain.

---

Mara walked back through the long halls of the galley as the evening light dimmed over the artificial windows. Odo was already at the pass, wiping down the counter with a rag.

"Well?" he asked.

"Six months," she said. "The bay stays for now."

Odo nodded slowly. He set the rag down. He looked at the cutting board, at the last of the strawberries—pale, watery, almost nothing at all.

"You know what they said, didn't you?" he asked.

"They said it tastes like a strawberry."

Odo smiled. It was a small, strange, tender smile. "Yeah," he said. "They did."

Mara picked up the cutting board. She carried it to the sink and rinsed the pale fruit under warm water, one by one. She watched them drift in the current.

They were not strawberries. Not really. Not in the way that mattered to the scientists, or to the algae reserves, or to the budget.

But to the young woman in the hearing, with her closed eyes and her quiet voice, they were. And to the children who came to the mess hall after school and said "yummy," and meant it, they were. And to Mara, who had spent forty-three years trying to make a ship full of strangers remember what it was like to be at home, they were the closest thing she had ever made to a truth.

She turned off the tap. The galley was quiet. Somewhere far below, the engines hummed their long, patient song.

Mara dried her hands. She went to work.
