# Salt and Flour

The morning fog in Brinham did not behave like fog. It came in from the sound, low and gray and patient, slipping along the quay like a cat that had decided the whole town belonged to it. It licked at the moorings and the gulls and the glass of the little bakery on the north side of the lane, and it did not hurry. It had nowhere to be but Brinham, and Brinham, in the end, was nowhere at all except where the tide decided it would be.

Inside the bakery, Mara O'Shea was already up. She was up before the gulls had finished deciding whether they would be birds, before the first light had crossed the roof, before the day had the courtesy to introduce itself. Her grandmother, Nona Mae, had once said, in the flat, salt-weathered voice she carried into every argument and every lull, that a baker who waited for the sun to open was not a baker at all but a customer of luck. Mara had been eleven the first time she heard it. She was thirty-four now, and she still believed her, which was why she was already at work.

She wiped her hands on the front of her apron and reached for the first crook. The dough answered the way it always did: cool, alive, a little stubborn, the way her grandmother had been. She pressed the heel of her palm into it and felt the day settle, the way a room settles when the last guest leaves. The rye rose into the rafters, dark and sour and old, the smell of a house that had been baking the same bread for longer than anyone alive could prove.

O'Shea's had stood in the lane for three generations. The sign above the door, painted black on weathered oak — O'SHEA'S, with the O's a little too high, as if the letters were trying to get out — had seen the tide come over the quay in the winter of 1949, and it had seen Nona Mae's hands rise white with flour, and it had seen the first and worst winter Nona Mae survived alone after her husband had gone out on the schooner *Cormorant* and the radio had given them no word at all. The town remembered. Brinham was a small town and small towns remembered bread, because bread was the only honest thing you could be sure of in a place where the sea took men and returned nothing.

But it was not the bread that drew them, not in the end. It was the morning. It was the crust, and the smell of rye, and the way the window fogged and cleared like a long breath taken and let go. It was the sound of the first loaf coming out of the oven at half past five, a dry, crackling sound, and the way the whole lane seemed to hold still for a minute to listen.

It was the croissant that broke things.

Jonas Voss opened his door on the south side of the lane in late May, two months before the festival. His shop had a glass front and an espresso machine and a sign in clean, thin, italic letters: **VOSS**. He had trained in Vienna, he told the paper, in a line so short it looked like it had been cut with shears. He told the fishermen at the market that he had trained in Copenhagen, because fishermen preferred Copenhagen to Vienna, for some reason no one could explain. He made laminated doughs that rose in precise, golden layers, and on Saturday mornings he sold them to a line that ran past the fish crates and down to the water's edge.

Mara saw that line from her window. She saw the paper bags. She saw the women who had bought from her grandmother for forty years, the same hands, the same aprons, the same little coins folded into the cup at the counter. She saw them in his line, and she said nothing, which was the worst part. She stood at her counter and she folded a loaf of rye and she thought, in the quiet, sourdough way she thought everything, *not today.*

They did not meet, not at first. Jonas was not a man who met. He stood behind the counter of his new shop and let the bread do the talking. His sourdough was technically perfect. His croissants were perfect. And perfect is a word that wears down to nothing when it is the only thing a shop has to say. It gets thin. It gets papery. It tears.

The rivalry grew the way small-town rivalries grow: by whispers, by the little things no one would say to your face. The morning paper ran Jonas's profile with a photograph of him in his apron, looking like a man who had been made in a factory for the purpose of being photographed. The "Reader's Voice" column, which had always been a place for complaints about the tide table, carried a note about "the new style." Old Tobin at the fish market said Jonas's bread tasted like the city, which was a compliment meant to be a cut. Mara's mother-in-law said it tasted like nothing at all, and she said this at dinner, loud enough for the whole table to hear.

Jonas's line, which had been long enough to make a rumor, shrank by a loaf.

Mara answered the way she always answered. She made the rye her grandmother had made for the '49 storm — dense and dark, smelling of the sea and wood smoke, with a crust so thick and rough it could have stopped a knife. The town called it tide rye. It sold out by nine.

Jonas answered by making a croissant so delicate that people held it by the corners with their fingertips, as if it might break, as if it had been made by someone who understood that the most important thing in the world was not to ruin it. It sold out by ten.

By July, they had become two things in Brinham, and Brinham, which had once needed only one, was not sure which of the two it wanted.

The Bread of the Tide festival was held every September, on the quay, and it was the one event in the whole year that the entire town came to. The mayor cut a wheel of cheese that no one wanted. The choir sang, badly and with great feeling. The judges walked the long tables and tasted without saying much. There had always been three of them, the same three for as long as anyone could remember: Tobin, Mrs. Kowalski, and Paddy Dool, who had retired from the harbor forty years ago but still knew the weight of a good heel of bread in his palm, the way a man knows the weight of a good day.

This year would be the first festival in forty years to be held with two bakers in the town. The paper ran the story in bold type: *O'SHEA AND VOSS: A CLASH ON THE QUAY.* Mara read it twice, standing in the lane with her apron on, and then she folded it into a square and put it in her pocket. Jonas, she heard, had read it once and put it in the oven, not to read it again but to keep it warm, which was a more dangerous thing to do.

She entered her tide rye, her grandmother's recipe, with a crust she had been working on all month, turning it over and over in her head like a stone in a river. He entered his Vienna-style bread, a crust so thin it was almost translucent, and a crumb so fine it looked like fresh snow on a hillside.

They were not rivals on the quay, she told herself. They were rivals in the lanes. On the quay, the bread would just be bread.

But the sea, which had been waiting all summer, was not done with the town.

The storm came in at midnight on the twenty-seventh of September, out of the sound, with a wind so hard it threw the ferry off its mooring and put the gulls to bed. It came in the way a bad word comes in: all at once, and with more of it behind it than you expected. The lights on the quay went out. The gulls went silent. The whole town went dark except for the streetlamps on the north side, which kept their small, stubborn flames.

By half past two the damage had been done. The quay was a mess of driftwood and overturned fish crates. The ferry would not come back for two days. The festival was canceled. The choir did not sing. The mayor's cheese was found, the next morning, floating in the sound.

But the people still needed bread.

The harbor crew — twelve men and women who had been out on the water and were now on land, clearing the quay and checking the moorings — had not eaten since the previous day. The families on the quay, cut off from the road that went over the hill, needed bread. Old Tobin, who could not walk more than a yard at a time, needed bread, and he would not ask for it, which made it worse.

Mara opened her door at five. Her oven was cold. The electricity was gone. Her grandmother's starter, in its jar, the one labeled *Nona Mae's, 1949*, was still warm on the counter and still bubbling, slow and steady, like a small heartbeat. She could bake by hand. She could bake the way Nona Mae had baked before the electricity came, in the wood-fired oven built into the back wall of the shop, the one that had been there since before Nona Mae was born.

She could do it alone.

She almost did.

The bell over the other door rang at five-fifteen. Jonas Voss stood in the lane in his apron with a sack of flour over his shoulder. He had not come to her shop. He had come to her door. The wind was still up. His hair was wet in places.

"The harbor crew is at the pier," he said. "Twelve of them. And the ferry's two days out. I know you have the wood oven. I don't know how you run it. I don't know the recipe. But I know how to bake. And I know how to be there."

Mara looked at him. He had flour on his cheek. He looked like a man who had not slept.

"You want to help me," she said. It was not a question, but it was the shape of one.

"I want to bake," he said. "That's different."

She looked at the lane. The fog was still there, low and gray, the way it had been every morning for as long as she had been alive. She looked at the wood oven, dark and waiting. She looked at Jonas.

"Put the flour down," she said. "And wash your hands."

They worked until noon.

Mara's wood oven roared in the back of the shop, filling the room with a heat that was not electric, not measured, not anything that could be turned off at a switch. It was a living thing. You had to feed it. You had to listen to it. You had to know, by the color of the fire and the smell of the wood, when it was ready, which was the same as knowing, by the feel of the dough, when a loaf was ready.

Jonas's hands, trained and precise and city-made, learned the rhythm of hers by degrees. He watched the way she pressed the dough, not folding it but pushing it through, the way you push a door that sticks. He watched the way she knew, without looking, that the crust was done, by the sound it made, a low, dry crackle, like a match struck in a dark room. He watched the way she never measured anything, not once, not even the salt, and yet the rye came out the same every time, dark and dense and full of the taste of the sea.

It was not perfect, the loaf he helped her pull from the oven. The top was uneven. The crumb had one place that was too tight. Jonas stood behind her and looked at it and said, "It's not perfect."

"No," she said. "It's ours."

He looked at her for a second. Then he put his hands on the counter and started to knead.

By one o'clock the ovens were full. By two the quay had bread. Mara carried the loaves in her arms, out into the wet lane and across the quay, and she laid them on the tables where the harbor crew was standing, and Jonas stood behind her and did the same, and they did not talk, because there was nothing to say, because the bread said it.

They took no money. There was no one to give any. They just set the loaves down, and the harbor crew ate, and the families on the quay came down, and Old Tobin, who had not walked more than a yard in three years, walked to the end of his row of chairs and ate a piece of tide rye with his fingers, and he said nothing, which was the highest praise he knew how to give.

The storm passed by night. The wind went out of the sound. The ferry came back on the third morning, slow and gray and smelling of salt, and the town exhaled, the way a town exhales after it has been holding its breath.

The mayor announced, at the first meeting he could get anyone to sit down in, that the festival would be held again in October, and that this year it would be a festival for both bakers. He said this in the way people say things in Brinham, which is to say, carefully, and as if he were apologizing for something he had not done.

The judges came out on the morning of the festival. They walked the long tables. They tasted the tide rye. They tasted the Vienna bread. They said nothing, the way judges do. Paddy Dool ate a piece of each, and he chewed them slowly, the way he had done for forty years, and then he set both pieces down on the table and said, "Same thing. Different hands."

Mara looked at Jonas. Jonas looked at the quay. The gulls were back. The fog was out. The sun, which had been late all week, was in the sky, and it was the same sun it had been the day before the storm and the day after the storm and every day before that, and it did not know, or care, which of them it was shining on.

They did not become friends. Not that day. Not that season. The line on the south side of the lane still stretched past the fish crates, and the line on the north side still stretched to the quay, and the women and the men still came, and they still said nothing, and they still ate, and the bread was still there.

But something had changed, and it was the kind of change that does not show up in a newspaper and does not need to.

Jonas started coming to the lane on the north side in the mornings, not to the door, not to the shop, but to the lane, where he would stand for a minute or two and watch the fog come in, and Mara would be in the window, and he would not wave, and she would not wave, and it was enough. It was the smallest of things, and it was the only thing either of them had ever needed to say.

Mara started leaving a loaf at the glass front of Voss's shop on the mornings she could not get to the quay. She never signed it. Jonas never asked. The loaf was always tide rye, always the same, always dark and dense and full of the taste of the sea, and Jonas always took it to the back of the shop and put it in a paper bag and took it home, and his mother, who had come out of the city to be with him, would eat it for breakfast and say nothing, because she had learned, in Brinham, that not saying it was the whole point.

In the morning, when the fog came in from the sound, low and gray and patient, Mara O'Shea was already up. She wiped her hands on the front of her apron and reached for the first crook. Across the lane, behind glass and an espresso machine, Jonas Voss was already up, too, with his hands in the dough, folding it through, the way a man pushes a door that sticks.

The sign on the north side was black on oak, with the O's a little too high. The sign on the south side was clean and thin and italic: **VOSS**. Between them, on the quay, the tide came in, and went out, and came in again.

And the bread was there.

It always was.
