# The Brine and the Butter

In the town of Saltmere, the sea arrived first. It came in on the morning mist, grey and patient, filling the harbour until the fishing boats hung like hooks against the sky. Then, once the light found the rooftops, the second thing arrived: the smell of bread.

It came from two directions, and that had been the town's trouble, or its gift, for as long as anyone could remember.

On Harbour Road, the weathered sign above Brennan's Bakery read, in faded paint: **BREAD SINCE 1901. NO SHORTCUTS.** Inside, a wood-fired brick oven had been lit every morning since before Ruthie Brennan was born, and she had fed it, in one form or another, for forty-three years. At sixty-four, Ruthie was a small woman with knuckles like walnuts and hands that remembered every recipe in her family — her grandmother's soda bread, her mother's Sunday rolls, and the great brown loaf the town called simply the Brennan. It was dark as a stormy sea, dense as a ship's keel, and its crust crackled when you broke it in half, like the surface of water.

On the other side of the square, where the cobbles met the sea wall, Callie Wren had opened The Golden Ear three years ago. Her shop had large windows, a white marble counter, and a sign that read only *The Golden Ear* in elegant letters. Callie was thirty-nine, had come up from the city on a long road, and made pastry the way other people made weather — sudden and complete. Her croissants, people said, had more layers than the town had winters. Her apricot tart came with a glaze so bright it looked as though the sea had been captured in a jar of glass.

The rivalry had been going on since the day Callie first walked into Brennan's.

It began, as things of that kind do, with bread. Callie had come in for a loaf of the Brennan brown and a flat coffee, and Ruthie had handed the loaf over, still hot from the oven, and said, "You want butter? We don't do butter with that here." Callie, who had just left the pastry school in the capital, had said, with the calm of someone who had already decided who she was, "Everything deserves butter."

"Well," Ruthie had said, "then you've already made your case against me."

The town, which loved a good argument almost as much as a good loaf, decided on the spot that it had a show.

Every September, when the tide came in high enough to kiss the harbour wall, Saltmere held its Tide Bread Fair. For thirty years the fair had been a friendly war — a war between the town and the sea, between the old ways and the new, and, for the last three years, with a growing intensity the town found hard to resist, between the two bakers. Ruthie entered her traditional breads: the brown loaf, the soda, and a sea-salt rye with caraway and a strip of dried mackerel baked into the crust, which the fishermen swore kept the salt out of the cellar. Callie entered her pastry: the layered cakes filled with salted butter cream, a croissant the size of a dinner roll, and once — just once — a savoury pie made from the day's catch. The mackerel pie had been a triumph, and the following year Ruthie's rye had beaten it, and the two of them had stood at the end of the harbour with their hands in their pockets and had said nothing for a very long time.

But this September was different. This September, the fair carried a prize that had never before been offered: the Golden Whisk.

Chef Aldric Voss, who wrote the pastry books in the capital, whose tarts could make grown men cry, had agreed to travel down to Saltmere on one condition: the town would hold a proper contest. The winner would receive the whisk, a plaque, and — as the mayor put it in his speech — "the sort of fame that makes people drive forty minutes just to eat your pastry."

Ruthie had read the mayor's speech twice, and then had not said a word about it for a week.

Callie had read it once, and had spent the following week changing her recipes.

The weatherman on the radio, a man named Dale who spoke as though the sky had personally insulted him, called it a "significant system" on the Tuesday before the fair. By Thursday it was a gale. By Friday evening the harbour was a churn of grey and black, and the lighthouse horn sounded every few minutes, a sound the whole town carried in its bones.

Ruthie locked up her shop at six in the afternoon. "Don't leave the oven unattended for a storm," she told her daughter, who had been helping in the shop all week and had no particular interest in her opinions, and went home with the door bolted, listening to the wind press its face against the glass.

At The Golden Ear, Callie was still in. Her electric ovens hummed and clicked in the back, and the display case held the half-finished batch of tarts she had planned to enter in the contest. The storm was knocking on the windows like a burglar trying a lock. She stood in the middle of the shop, arms folded, watching the sea, and tried not to think about what her mother had told her the week before she died, that she should have stayed in Saltmere and been a good baker.

Callie had never been able to answer that, so she answered it with work.

At two in the morning, the market hall's roof came apart.

It happened with a long, low crack, like a ship's keel splitting, and then a rain of tiles and beams came down onto the square. Ruthie, woken in her sleep, pulled on her coat and ran out. The market hall had been the town's meeting place for over a hundred years, a tall building with a steep roof that had outlived three mayors and two floods. Now its roof had split open, and the rain was coming in sideways, and the stalls inside lay on the floor.

Ruthie stood under the eaves and looked. She could see the cracked and slanting display case from The Golden Ear. She could see the long table where the fair's samples were supposed to be set out the next day. She could see the plaque the mayor had brought down the day before, lying on its side in the water.

And then, in a gap in the dark, she saw Callie standing there in a raincoat the colour of mustard, face upturned to the rain, looking at the ruined hall with an expression Ruthie had no name for.

Not anger. Not disappointment. Something quieter. Something that looked like grief.

Callie came out of the rain and stood across the square from Ruthie's shop.

"It's all gone," she said.

"I can see that," Ruthie said.

"The tarts. The cakes. The display case. The samples for the judges' table." She paused. "The fair is tomorrow. The hall is where we were supposed to hold it."

Ruthie looked at her. "Are you saying it's my fault? I locked the doors at six."

"I'm saying the hall is gone and the fair is tomorrow and both our entries are on the floor." Callie's voice was level, but her hands were shaking, and the rain was running down her face. "Chef Voss is staying at the harbour inn. His boat's in the harbour. He's eating oysters right now."

Ruthie closed her eyes. She had worked on this year's loaves for three weeks. She had been baking the brown loaf every single day for three weeks, to get the crust exactly right. She had been working the soda bread over and over, trying to get the crumb soft inside and tough outside, the way her grandmother made it. The sea-salt rye had been her own recipe — the first thing she had ever made that was truly her own, after her mother's and her grandmother's.

"I'm not entering a loaf made with shop flour," she said. "And I'm not entering a loaf made with a shortcut."

"Neither am I," Callie said.

They stood there, two bakers in the rain, and the sea made its sound behind them, and neither of them said a word for a long time.

They argued about it for an hour, sitting on the bench outside Brennan's with their backs to the cold, drinking tea from two mugs Callie had carried over in a plastic bag.

"Two entries," Callie said. "I re-bake the tarts. We both enter."

"You can't re-bake tarts in one night with those hands."

"I have hands."

"You have hands," Ruthie said, in her own way, and Callie laughed — the first laugh either of them had done in a long time — and then, with the quiet of someone who has already lost but has not yet admitted it, "The judges will eat at the inn. We can bring the loaves to the inn. The hall is gone, but the fair can go on at the inn. If we have two entries, the contest is honest."

"And if you can't re-bake the tarts?"

Callie looked at the rain. "Then we don't have tarts. And we have the two of us."

"The two of us."

"Yes. Your oven. My ovens. Your bread. My pastry. One entry."

"My bread and your pastry don't go together."

"Everything does, with enough butter." Callie said it in the same voice she had used it three years ago, in her first visit to the counter. "It's a simple thing."

Ruthie opened her mouth, closed it, and said, "Fine. But no shortcuts."

The storm broke at four in the morning.

By five, the sky had gone a pale, exhausted grey, and the rain had slowed to a fine dust. The harbour lay calm, the fishing boats hung in the water, and the lighthouse was silent. The town stood in its windows and looked at the damage, and the damage was everywhere — a tree across the pub's roof, the market hall with its roof open like a wound, the streetlamps with their glass broken, the cobbles washed clean.

Ruthie unlocked her shop at six. Callie was there at seven, carrying two boxes of butter and a sack of flour from the city. "I had to take the truck in," she said. "It's the good butter. The one with the cream in it."

Ruthie looked at the butter. "That's a long way for butter to travel."

"It's the best butter in the capital."

"It's butter," Ruthie said. "Now put the kettle on."

What followed was the best and worst baking of Ruthie's life.

The work was divided between the two shops, and the small street between them became, for that one day, the busiest place in the town. Callie made her pastry at The Golden Ear; Ruthie made her bread at Brennan's. The heat came through both walls, and the two ovens filled the little street with a warmth that pushed the night cold out of it, and they passed each other's work over the cobbles on a cloth, and neither of them said much, because there was too much to do, and because the storm had only just gone, and because the fair was in twelve hours and there were two bakers in two shops trying to make something that mattered.

At nine, Callie made her first batch of croissants. They were good, and they were also wrong — the layers too thick, the butter too soft, the pastry coming out flat and heavy. Ruthie watched from across the cobbles.

"Your butter's too cold," she called over.

"It's cold enough."

"It's cold as a stone. You want it cold as the sea, not cold as a stone. There's a difference."

Callie looked at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've been doing this all my life, and I've finally figured it out." She set down a piece of dough and took a knife from the shelf. "Hold this. And this." She pointed at a piece of the brown loaf dough resting on the counter under a cloth. "This is my dough. It's been going two days. It's alive. If you touch it with your hands, it tastes like you. It will taste like you."

Callie touched it and drew her hand back as if it had burnt her.

"It's warm," she said.

"Everything that's warm is alive," Ruthie said.

At ten, Callie's tarts were done. They were beautiful, and they were also, in some small and unnameable way, wrong. The glaze was too bright. The filling was too sweet. And the pastry, though it was good, lacked the thing Ruthie was looking for — the thing that makes a bread taste of the place it comes from.

Ruthie took a bite.

"Too sweet," she said.

"Yes."

"Too bright."

"Yes."

"And the pastry lacks the sea."

Callie looked at her. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've been eating bread from this place my whole life, and I know what it's supposed to taste like when it's from here." She set the piece down. "The salt isn't enough. The butter isn't enough. And the pastry's too proud of itself."

Callie stood very still. "I've been making these tarts for six years. I've won things."

"Yes," Ruthie said. "You have. And this is a place. And the sea is here. And the judges are going to taste this, and they're going to know it's not from here."

Callie set down her knife. Then, quietly, she took the glaze bottle and poured it out.

They started again at eleven.

This time they worked together, and it was strange and also right. Callie made the tarts and the croissants; Ruthie made the breads; they passed each other's work back and forth, and they talked about almost nothing, and they talked about everything.

Ruthie told her about her mother's hands, and how her mother's hands had been so rough from the sea work that she could never make a clean fold on a pastry. Callie told her about the pastry school, and how the head teacher had said she had the hands of a sculptor and the mind of a poet, and how she had spent six years trying to make the two things fit.

"They fit," Ruthie said. "You just don't know where to put the seam."

"Where do I put the seam?"

"At the bottom," Ruthie said. "Everything good puts its seam at the bottom. Nobody sees it. And that's why it holds."

At two in the afternoon, the tarts were done, and the breads were done, and they were both the best things either of them had ever made. They were also, in some small and unnameable way, the same thing.

Callie's tarts had been made from Ruthie's starter, and the pastry had a tang that went with the apricot. The brown loaf had a thin fold of lamination worked into the crust, and it was dark, and it crackled, and it had a sheen it never had before. And the pie — the pie, which Callie had made with Ruthie's rye crust and her own filling and the day's catch — the pie was the thing the two of them had been trying all day to make.

Ruthie cut a slice of the brown loaf and handed it to Callie. Callie took a bite and didn't say anything for a long time.

"It's not the brown loaf," she said at last.

"No," Ruthie said. "It isn't."

"What is it, then?"

"It's a brown loaf that's been made with butter, and the sea, and you." Ruthie paused. "It's the best brown loaf I've ever made. And it isn't mine."

Callie looked at her. "It's ours," she said.

The morning of the fair came in a thin, clear light. The market hall was still a wound, and the town had put up a long table under a great canvas in the square, the canvas the colour of the sky, held up by ropes that ran to the sea wall, and the sea wall was covered with a thin crust of salt, and the salt made the light come back to the town in a soft, white glow.

Chef Voss came down the harbour road at ten, in a grey coat with a little notebook in his pocket. He walked to the table and stood there for a long time. He looked at the two bakers behind it, and said nothing, and then he picked up the first tart and bit into it.

He ate the whole tart without looking up. He ate the whole loaf the same way. He ate the rye. He ate the pie, which was so good that Ruthie had cried when she tasted it, and which she did not cry about again.

When he was finished, he opened his notebook, wrote something, closed it, and said, "The tarts are good. The bread is good. The pie is very good."

"Yes," Callie said.

"But you two," he said, looking at Ruthie, then at Callie, "you've made the same thing."

"Yes," Ruthie said.

"Then the question isn't which is better. The question is which one is the town."

He looked at the canvas, and at the harbour, and at the two ovens behind the two bakers, and said, "The prize goes to the bread that holds the town together. That's the only thing here that can win."

He set the Golden Whisk on the table, in the middle of the two of them, and walked away, and the town stood very still, and the sea made its sound, and the two bakers stood behind the table and said nothing.

That evening, the two of them were in the back room of The Golden Ear, washing up cups and plates from the fair.

"I'm going to retire," Ruthie said.

Callie set the cup down. "I know."

"I don't know when. But I know I can't do this forever."

"When?"

"Probably this winter. When the shop is quiet and the bread is done with."

"And the brown loaf?"

"It'll be yours. You'll have to make it your own."

Callie looked at her. "I don't know how to make a brown loaf."

"You don't need to know how. You need to know what it tastes like. And you know what it tastes like."

Callie set the cup down in the drying rack. "The starter," she said.

"The starter's in the back. It's been going as long as the bakery. It's alive. If you feed it every day, it'll keep going."

Callie looked across the room. "It'll take over my bakery."

"It'll take over your bakery," Ruthie said. "But it won't take over your tarts. The seam goes at the bottom, and nothing goes on top. That's the rule."

Callie smiled. "I'll keep the rule."

"Yes," Ruthie said. "You'll keep the rule."

The next morning, the town woke to the smell of bread, and the smell came from two directions, and the town stood in the square and looked at the two bakeries and said nothing, and the sea arrived first, and the bread arrived second, and the two bakers stood in their shops, and the two ovens were lit, and the town was quiet, and the tide went in, and the tide went out, and the bread went on.
