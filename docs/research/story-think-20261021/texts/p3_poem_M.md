## final text

The last basket tips into the truck bed with a soft thud,
and the apples—still warm from the sun,
still holding the morning's cold dew in their skins—
rattle like small stones against the tailgate.

My hands are stained the color of crushed plums
down to the second knuckle, the crease where the wrist
folds like old paper. I flex them. They ache
the way they have all August, a dull
percussion under the skin.

The supervisor, Dale, claps a hand on my shoulder
and says, "See you next year," which is
the same sentence he has said to every single one
since July, and I nod, because
the sentence is true and it is not true,
and both are acceptable.

I walk the last row of peaches,
slow this time, no hurry, no quota,
I press one to my cheek. The fuzz
is gone already, or I have stopped noticing.
There is a moth in the hollow where a tree
split in the August wind,
and I think about how a small creature
makes a home out of a wound.

The tractor hums its idle hymn somewhere
behind me. A crow stitches the sky
with black thread. The road home
smells like cut hay and diesel
and the particular sweetness
of overripe fruit left to brown
on the ground, unclaimed.

I take my keys, I put my boots back on,
I leave the orchard gate open
the way they told us to,
and the evening comes in like a hand
closing gently over the whole red, golden afternoon.
