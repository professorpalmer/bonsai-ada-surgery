# Chapter One: The Tide Below the City

No one in Veranthos looked down.

That was the first rule the children learned before they learned their names. Look at the horizon. Look at the masts. Look at your neighbor's face, the way the salt wind combed it. But never, ever, look at the edge. The edge of the city was where the shell ended and the world began, and the world was not kind to those who stared at it too long.

Mira had been watching the edge for three years, which made her a danger to herself, and she knew it.

She stood on the rim of the Old Quarter, her fingers resting on the mossy stone of the parapet, and counted the swells. The sea moved in slow, enormous breaths, each one lifting the whole city a little, settling it a little, like a cradle that had forgotten it was a cradle. The turtle — no one in Veranthos called him that, not aloud — carried the city the way a man carries a heavy coin: not with pride, not with love, but because it was heavy and it was his and he would not put it down.

Below, the water was the color of hammered pewter. The turtle's head rose and fell in a rhythm no tide table could name. When the great neck swayed, every window in the city shuddered. The bell in the Cathedral of the Long Neck tolled without a rope, without a hand, and the old people said the bell rang on its own because the city was still moving.

They never said who or what it was, or for how long it had been moving. The archives burned twice. The maps from before the second burning said the turtle was younger than the first stone laid at the harbor, and older than the names of the streets. The priests called him the Quiet. The merchants called him the Ledger, because he was never late. The children called him whatever they thought would make him blink.

Mira was sixteen, and the tide had been rising for six days.

She had seen a rising before. Not like this one. The last time the sea had crept up the city's flanks, the fishwives had dragged their nets onto higher ground and the priests had lit candles at every window for a week. The turtle had carried them through it the way he carried everything — without comment, without mercy, without hurry.

But this rising was different. The water was not climbing the shell. It was pulling at it.

She could see the lines on the tide. Where the foam broke against the lower streets, it hung for a moment, suspended, as if something down there were trying to lift her city by the hem of its clothes.

"Mira."

Her brother's voice from behind her. Kael, seventeen, with his forearms roped in rope burn and his eyes too bright for a man who worked the hulls. He had come up the spiral stair with a bucket of rope and a face full of things he wasn't saying.

"The low decks are wet again," he said. "Third night running. The water isn't coming from the sea. It's coming from the shell."

Mira did not turn around. "The shell doesn't sweat."

"No," Kael said, and set the bucket down. "It does. It just never had to before."

She turned then, and saw the thing he was pointing at. Along the inner curve of the Old Quarter's rim, where the shell met the stone of the city's lower ring, there were beads. Slow, dark, glistening beads that formed and slid down the stone in long threads, collecting in the gutters with a sound like rain falling in a very quiet house.

The city was wet from the inside.

Mira pressed her palm flat against the parapet stone. Beneath it, far below her feet, she felt it. A pulse. Not the tide. Not the swell. Something deeper. A slow, deliberate heartbeat that had been there all along, so regular that no one had ever thought to listen for it.

It was quickening.

She looked up the long sweep of the city — the terraced houses, the masts, the bell of the Cathedral of the Long Neck, the white towers of the Archivist's Hill. People were moving through the streets, carrying what they carried. No one was looking at the edge.

No one was looking at her.

The tide hung at the rim of the world, patient as a held breath, and Mira stood on the rim with her hand on the stone and her heart in her throat, and she understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones like cold water, that the city was not riding the turtle anymore.

It was being carried.

And something, far below the tide, had just opened its eyes.
