# The Last Meal

At 0600, Cora Venn, chief cook of the generation ship *Meridian*, smelled the ship.

This was the first thing she did every morning. Not the coffee — the coffee was a rationing tool, brewed on the same schedule for three hundred years — but the ship itself. The faint iodine of the recycler exhausts, the green smell of the hydroponics deck, the sweet rot of the waste composters, and beneath all of it, the clean ozone tang of the fusion core. For four hundred and twelve years the *Meridian* had been breathing through those vents, and Cora could tell you what a ship was feeling just the way her grandmother could.

Isla was already at the prep table, peeling potatoes with the flat of a knife. She was seventeen, and she had the look of someone who wanted to be somewhere else.

"You're here early," Cora said.

"The galley's cold," Isla said. "I figured."

Today the ship smelled of trouble. A thin, chalky note, like dust on an old book. Cora told herself it was the composters. She did not tell Isla.

She put on her coat — wool and polymer, the last of the real wool — and descended to Deck Nine.

The galley was a cathedral of copper. Copper was the first thing the launch crew had refused to let go. They had carried it up the tower at Cape Meridian and bolted it to the walls of the kitchen, and three centuries later it was still shiny, because copper does not oxidize the way the other metals do, and the people of this ship had stopped pretending that anything would be replaced. You did not replace what you had. You maintained it. You passed it on. That was the only rule of the *Meridian* older than the government.

On Deck Nine, the hydroponics bay was where the real work happened. Forty thousand people ate from fourteen hundred acres of suspended garden, and this morning Cora stood before the monitoring console and saw the problem before the alert had. Bay Six was dead. Not failing — dead. The soil sensors had flatlined at 0432, and the bay's lights, usually a soft green, were off.

The *Meridian* was three hundred and forty years from the star they had been told to reach, a star named Kestrel that was not in any of the charts no one had been allowed to update. They had stopped checking. Checking was a kind of hope, and the ship did not do hope. It did rations.

She reported it to Chief Engineer Haldane. He came down with his usual face.

"Recycler fault," he said. "We'll have it back by Friday."

"You have to feed forty thousand people until Friday. Bay Six is a third of the wheat crop."

Haldane's jaw tightened. "We'll stretch the protein paste. The council will announce a ration adjustment."

Cora looked at the dead lights. "You know what this feels like?"

"What?"

"The same as every other morning. Like the ship is forgetting something."

Haldane left. Cora stood alone before the dark bay, and she understood, the way you understand a bad dream, that the chalky smell she had noticed up top was coming from here. From the dead bay. From the thing that had stopped breathing.

That was when she remembered the cache.

It was on Deck One, in a corridor nobody walked anymore, behind the original captain's quarters. The first cook's lockbox. No one knew what was in it. The *Meridian*'s records showed it had never been opened since launch — not during the Long Dark, not during the Quiet War, not during the time the ship had been lost for eleven years and the children were raised on algae and salt and memory. The council had declared the lockbox "heritage, sealed." Heritage meant untouchable. Untouchable meant not counted.

Cora had found it in her grandmother's notes. The first cook, the woman who had carried the copper pots from Earth, had written a single line: "For the last meal. For when we stop being travelers."

Cora took the key from the captain's quarters. She was the chief cook. It was her cache. That was the only rule of the *Meridian* older than the government, and it was this.

The lock opened with a sound like a held breath being released.

Inside was not a recipe book. It was a jar of seeds — real seeds, not the cloned, stabilized, three-hundred-year-old stock in the hydroponics bay. Wheat. Barley. Peas. Sunflowers. And a note, in handwriting so worn it was almost a texture:

*If you are reading this, the wheat is dead or the wheat is gone, and we have stopped pretending that we know where we are going. I will not tell you what I know. I know only this: I spent my life feeding this ship, and I never once fed the people what they were. They wanted bread, so I gave them bread. They wanted to be home, so I gave them a kitchen. If you are reading this, you have done both already. Plant the sunflowers. They bloom in the dark. That is the proof I need that the light is coming.*

Cora stood there for a long time. She read the note again. She read it three more times. And then she understood what the first cook had been feeding them. Not wheat. Not barley. Not even the copper pots. The first cook had been feeding them the idea that the journey had a direction. That the last meal would be real. That someone, someday, would be the one who got to cook the last meal.

She looked at the seeds. They were brown and small and perfect. They were Earth, and they were the future, and they were the only thing on the *Meridian* that had not been stabilized by three centuries of careful, careful management.

She put them in her coat pocket.

On Deck Nine, the hydroponics bay was still dark. Haldane's repairs were three days out. The council would announce a ration adjustment, and the people would take it, because the people on the *Meridian* had always taken it. They were good at it. They were built for it. But something in Cora's chest was clenched, and she knew, the way you know a bad dream, that she was not going to let them eat the ration adjustment.

She went to the seed vault on Deck Nine, and she took out every plant that could be moved. She took out the wheat, the barley, the peas, and she planted them in the dark bay, in the dead soil that still smelled of chalk.

She worked for two days. She worked alone, and she sang, and the songs were old, and they were not songs this ship had heard in three centuries. She sang them to the seeds, the way the first cook had sung to the copper pots. The dead bay did not light up. The lights stayed off. But the soil did not stay chalky. It turned dark, and soft, and it smelled, faintly, of rain.

On the third day, Haldane came back with the recycler fixed, and the bay's lights came on, and the soil in the dead bay was dark and soft, and the seeds were pushing up through it.

"What the hell is this?" Haldane said.

"The last meal," Cora said.

Haldane stared at her. "The last meal? We're not at the end of anything."

"No," she said. "But we are at the end of the pretending. The first cook knew it. She knew we didn't know where we were going, and she fed us anyway. That is what she did. She fed us anyway. And the sunflowers are in there, in the seeds. They bloom in the dark. That's the proof I need that the light is coming."

Haldane did not argue. He went to the council. And the council, because the council is only as strong as its next decision, announced not a ration adjustment but a feast. A feast of the last meal, and it was not a meal. It was a beginning.

Cora cooked. She cooked for two days, and she used the real wheat, and the real barley, and the peas that were pushing up in the dark bay, and she cooked the sunflowers into a soup, and the soup was sweet, and it was not sweet in the way the ration adjustment was sweet, and the people of the *Meridian* ate it, and they closed their eyes, and they tasted something they had not tasted in three centuries.

They tasted a direction.

Isla was at the prep table when the last of the soup was served. She had not gone to bed. She had been there all day, and she had not said anything about the dead bay, and she had not said anything about the seeds, and that was all right.

Cora gave her the jar. The seeds were almost gone. Not all of them — she had saved a few. She gave Isla the note, the one with the handwriting so worn it was almost a texture.

"For when we stop being travelers," Cora said.

Isla took the jar. She held it like it was heavy, which itNow produce the complete answer.
