# Chapter One: The Tide Below the City

No one in Veranthos could remember the first time the turtle had moved.

The city was built on the shell of a creature so vast that its spine rose from the sea in three great arches of barnacled stone, and its belly was a drowned cathedral of current and kelp. From the rooftops of the Upper Quarter you could see the water lapping at the shell's edges, silver and endless, and if you stood very still and pressed your ear to a cobble, you could hear the slow, tectonic grinding beneath your feet. The city's elders called that sound the Drift. They said the Drift was the heartbeat of the world, and they were right, in the way that only old things are right: it was true, but it was not the whole truth.

The whole truth was that the Drift came and went.

It had been gone for nine days. Nine days of stillness so complete that the street vendors had started to grow uneasy. The fishmongers in the Lower Market swore the tide had stopped answering. The bell-towers of the Spire District had gone silent, because the great bronze bells were tuned to the Drift, and a bell that has never heard the sea's slow pulse does not ring. Children in the alleyways played a game called the Longest Hold, betting copper coins on how many minutes the sky could remain perfectly, terribly still.

Kess Vane had lost her last coin on the third day.

She stood now on the weathered lip of the Saltward Staircase, her cloak soaked through at the hem from mist that had not quite the courage to rain. Below her, the Lower Quarter sprawled across the curved underside of the shell like a map of someone else's problem: laundry ropes strung between chimneys, the smell of drying fish and roasting cress, the endless argument of two women about a fence line. Above her, the Upper Quarter climbed the shell's slope in tiers of white stone, crowned by the Spire, and the Spire was dead.

"The Drift is a lie," her neighbor had told her the night before, when the stillness had first settled over the city like a held breath. "An old story. The bells ring because the masons put springs in them."

Kess had wanted to argue. She was a cartographer's apprentice, and she knew that some lies were load-bearing. You could not simply take a story out of a city without checking whether the walls would fall. The Drift told the city when to plant the kelp, when to open the fish markets, when to stop and listen. For nine days the city had gone on without it, and it had gone on in a state she could only describe as anxious politeness. People smiled a half-beat too late. They said "the tide is due" the way sailors say "the sky's clear" when they know the sky is about to turn.

On the eighth day, something else had gone quiet.

The ink.

Every fountain in every writing shop in the Lower Quarter had stopped flowing. Not dried up — stopped. The ink sat in the wells of the pens like a held breath of its own, black and still, and no amount of stirring woke it. Kess's master, old Fen, had stared at his own pen for an hour, then said very quietly, "It knows before we do," and had put the pen back in its case and gone to bed.

Now, on the ninth morning, Kess did two things. She pulled a sheaf of charts from her satchel — blank, because no ink would write on them, but she had drawn the city by hand in the last three nights, from memory and from the window. And she walked down the Saltward Staircase toward the lowest part of the shell, where the city ended and the sea began.

The Drift, she had decided, was not a heartbeat. It was a question. And nine days without an answer was not stillness.

It was the turtle listening.

She reached the bottom of the stairs at the sound of a bell she could not have placed. One clear tone, rising from somewhere below the city, below the stone, below the shell. The fishmongers on the Lower Market stopped their haggling. The two women by the fence went very still. Even the gulls over the harbor dropped from the sky and hung in a slow, suspended circle, as if the air itself had forgotten the rest of the world.

From beneath the city came another sound.

A groan. Low and long, the kind of sound that makes the floor of a house feel like a boat. The cobble under Kess's feet trembled. The water at the shell's edge shivered in a single, perfect ring.

The bells in the Spire began to ring.

Not one at a time — all of them, every bronze and iron tongue the masons had ever tuned, striking in a chord so deep it was felt in the chest before it was heard. Kess gripped the railing of the stair as the whole city shuddered, and the sea rose, just a hand's breadth, in a slow green breath, and then the world began again.

Somewhere below, in the drowned cathedral of the current, something very old opened its eye.
