# The Last Recipe of the Meridian

The galley of the Meridian smelled of copper, yeast, and the green dust of the hydroponic bays. For ninety-two years the ship had sailed between stars, and Marisol Vane was the only one among the four thousand souls aboard who still measured things in pinches: a pinch of salt, a pinch of thyme, a pinch of the hope that the next course would bring more than the last. She was forty-seven, the last cook of the Third Generation, and tonight the tomatoes in Bay Seven had turned the color of dried blood.

The cooks had been a line on the Meridian since before the ship had a name. The First Cook, Elin Vasquez, had carried the ledger and the seed tin across the launch pad and written the first line in the black book: "Steady hands feed the whole ship." Marisol's grandmother had been a cook. Her grandmother's grandmother had been a cook. Marisol had learned the dough from a woman who never smiled and taught her that bread was a promise you made to the future, and that a promise broken at the table broke the ship with it.

She had watched them wilt for six weeks, the tomatoes in Bay Seven going limp at the stem and then pale, as if the plant were slowly forgetting how to be red. The crew called it "the fade." The engineers called it a nutrient imbalance in the regenerative soil. Marisol knew what it was. The ship was hungry. The Meridian ran on fusion and memory, and memory was a thin fuel. Somewhere in the humming dark beneath the deck plates, something was being fed before anything else — the reactor, the life support, the cold long sleep of the first colony's cryopods. And the tomatoes, and the wheat, and the little green herbs of the window box, were being squeezed to give back what the ship needed to survive another year.

The summons came at dusk, the way the Meridian still marked its evenings, an amber light that pulsed along the corridor like a second heartbeat. Captain Idris, young and sharp-eyed, stood in the doorway with his uniform still damp. "Council dinner," he said. "In an hour. Bring your ledger." Marisol felt something old tighten in her chest. Not the ledger itself — she had kept that one, the battered black book of the First Cook's Guild, for twenty-two years. What tightened in her chest was the last page. The last recipe. The one Elin Vasquez had written the night before she died, in a hand so small it could have been a spider's.

She set the table with white cloth, because that is what she had always done, and because the cloth came from the last cotton seed in the vault, a threadbare thing that smelled of the rain she had never seen. Four plates. Four cups. In the center, a bowl for the meal. Her hands were steady. They had always been steady in the galley, ever since her grandmother's grandmother had taught her to knead at the edge of a counter and press the dough flat with the heel of her palm. "Steady hands feed the whole ship," she read every morning. Now, in the amber light, she finally understood the weight of it.

The last entry was dated eighty-nine years before Marisol was born, the night of Elin Vasquez's death. The heading read: *Final Recipe — for the day the ship must decide what it is.* Beneath it, in letters no larger than grains of rice:

"Tomato, three, from Bay Seven. Wheat flour, one cup, from the red jar. Salt, a pinch, from the cold shelf. Thyme, from the window box. Butter, from the first morning after landing. Bake slow, and remember."

The kitchen was quiet. The galley hummed its one low note, and somewhere far below, the reactor sang its old song. Marisol turned the page slowly, and for a moment the only sound was the ticking of the wall clock — the last clock on the Meridian that still measured time in hours, not cycles.

She was going to make this meal, of course. That was what the captain had asked for, and a cook does not refuse the ship's table. She reached for the knife, and then she stopped. One cup of flour from the red jar in the flour room. The red jar had been broken for a hundred years. The cold shelf held nothing but distilled water and medicine. And there had never been butter on this ship. Not butter, not cream, not a drop of fat that had not been synthesized in a laboratory. Elin had known that too. She had written the recipe the night before she died, with the bays already beginning their long decline, and she had not been writing a recipe at all. She had been writing a map.

She went to Bay Seven at midnight, with a flashlight and the ledger under her arm. In the third row behind the support frame, she found them: three seed pods, no larger than a fist, wrapped in cloth the color of dried blood, each with a tag in the First Cook's handwriting. Tomato. Wheat. Thyme. Heirloom seeds, the true ones, not the clones the crew had been growing from a genetic line worn thin for three generations. In the cold shelf, beneath the medicine and the water, was a small tin of salt from the old world. And the window box by the captain's door was still green — thyme, alive and wild, the only thing on the Meridian that had not been measured out. Marisol stood in the corridor holding the tin of salt, and understood what Elin had done. She had not hidden a recipe. She had hidden the ship's memory. Every meal the crew had ever eaten was built on seeds that were dying a little each cycle, and the true ones had been sleeping in the dark, waiting for someone who knew how to ask.

The meal for the council was not a stew. Marisol made bread. She cracked open the wheat pod and ground it on the stone mill that had come up from Earth, and she kneaded the dough until it was a second skin under her palms, pressing with the heel of her palm the way her great-grandmother had taught her. The galley's oven was old, patient, and remembered a kind of heat. The smell of baking wheat spread down the corridor and into the sleeping bays, and for the first time in months the Meridian smelled like a place where people might stay. "Bake slow, and remember," she read again, and set the loaf in the oven.

The captain ate in silence. When he finished he looked at her the way people look at the sea. "The ship has been rationing," he said quietly. "We have been rationing for months. I did not know it was as bad as… as this." Marisol set down the knife. "It was never as bad as this," she said. "It was as bad as a promise." She showed them the seed pods. She showed them the ledger. She showed them the math the engineers had never looked at: what the ship was spending, what it was carrying, and what it would lose before the next port if they continued to feed the dead and starve the bays. Councilor Amari, who never argued with the numbers, said nothing for a long time. Then she said, "Then we stop feeding the dead. We feed the living." The council met in the small hours, and by dawn they had voted, and the Meridian changed direction, slowly, like a sleeper turning.

The bays were replanted the following week. Marisol did not teach the engineers. She taught everyone — the cooks, the medics, the children from Deck Nine who came with their faces pressed to the observation glass. How to open the pod. How to read the tag. How to water with a hand, not a meter. "The First Cook wrote it," she told them. "Steady hands feed the whole ship." She planted the tomato seeds in the light that remembered the sun, and the thyme in the window box by the captain's door, and the wheat in a pot no bigger than a cup. Three seeds. A pinch of salt. A small beginning.

Months later, Marisol stood at the counter with a boy of twelve — Kael, her apprentice — and showed him how to fold the dough. "The ship is the same," she said. "You keep the dough alive, and the ship stays alive." He asked if he could be a cook when he grew up, and she said yes without hesitation. Outside the porthole, the stars did not blink. They never did. But in Bay Seven, in the third row behind the support frame, something small and green was reaching toward the light, and for the first time in ninety-two years the Meridian carried a future it could eat.
