## final text

# The Last Departure

## Chapter One

The fog came in off the Silver River every October, crept along the valley floor in that thick, gray sheet that made the pines at the ridge look like they were sinking into water. Arthur Calloway had watched it for thirty-one years, from the conductor's window on the Cedar Hollow Line, and he could still tell, by the way it thinned or thickened, whether the morning would bring rain or just the slow, damp cold that seeped into a man's bones.

It was a thick one today.

He stood at the kitchen window of the little house on Birch Street, coffee cooling in his mug, and watched it swallow the switchyard. The old rail yard sat a quarter mile up the hill from the town, behind the shuttered depot and the row of derelict freight sheds that the county had tried, twice, to demolish and never managed. The tracks ran in three parallel lines through the yard before merging into the single northbound line that vanished over the mountain pass. The line had closed in 1996. The last passenger train had pulled out of the Cedar Hollow depot at 4:12 in the morning, carrying fourteen people and a violinist whose case had been left behind in the baggage compartment, where it probably still was.

Art had been the conductor on that last run.

He set the mug down on the counter and turned back to the stove. Eggs, black coffee, a slice of yesterday's toast. The radio on the shelf was murmuring something about the state budget and a baseball score. He hadn't listened to the radio in years, but his wife, before she'd moved out and back to her sister in Boise, had liked it on. He left it on. It filled the silence with something that wasn't silence.

The phone rang at 9:47.

Art let it ring. He wiped the counter with a dish towel, drying the ring the mug had made. He let it ring again. Then again. By the fifth ring, he knew it wasn't a number he would want to see on the display, and he picked it up.

"Arthur?"

Sheriff Dale Whitcomb's voice. Flat. The kind of flat a man gets when he's trying not to sound like what he is.

"Dale."

"You want to come to the switchyard?"

A pause. The fog. Art could almost feel the switchyard's particular damp through the phone. "Found a body."

"Who?"

"Found by a guy walking his dog on the service road. Young woman. Maybe twenty-five, twenty-six. No papers on her. Looks like she's been dead a day or two."

Art set the dish towel down carefully. He put it back in the drawer. He did not, this once, wipe the counter.

"I'll be there in twenty."

He hung up and stood for a long moment, listening to the fog press against the window. Then he went to the closet.

The closet held three suitcases he never packed, a pair of winter boots, and the conductor's cap. He took the cap. Black wool, a brass insignia shaped like a mountain peak. He had worn it on the northbound run every day for nineteen years, and he had taken it off only when he went to sleep. He had kept it in this closet for twenty-nine years, though he wore it occasionally for the little heritage parade in September, though no one had asked him to do that in the last three.

He pulled on the cap, turned it back, and went out the door.

His truck was in the drive, an old F-250 with rust blooming along the wheel wells. He drove up the service road, the one that wound behind the switchyard and out toward the ridge. The road had never been paved. The gravel crunched under the tires the same way it had when he used to walk it with the morning rounds. He passed the last of the freight sheds, the depot with its peeling blue paint and its window boarded shut in 1997, and turned into the gravel lot where the switchyard began.

The dog walker was there. A thin man in a windbreaker, pale as the fog, holding a small leash in one hand. The dog—a greyhound, a stray, a dog with too much name for the body—sat at his feet, unbothered.

Sheriff Whitcomb was standing over the body. He'd pulled on a jacket. Two county troopers were crouched at the far side, the side facing the ridge, taking measurements. The body lay on the old ballast, the crushed stone that the trains had once vibrated through. She was on the second line, not the main, not the siding. Between the depot and the water tower.

She was young. Dark hair, damp with whatever the fog had done to her. A leather jacket over a flannel shirt, dark jeans, boots that had been laced and unlaced many times. Her hands were at her sides. Her eyes were open. She had been dead, Art thought, at least a day. The fog would have taken her color by now, but there was a faintness around the mouth, a bruise at the corner of the left eye, not the kind the fog would give.

The dog had wandered over and sniffed her shoe, then lost interest.

"She's on the wrong track," Art said.

Whitcomb looked up. "What?"

"She's on the second line. The one that used to be the freight sidings. If you were walking that service road, you'd walk the edge of the yard. The natural line between the gravel and the ballast is the first track. This line is further in. You'd have to walk past the water tower to get here." Art pointed. "And the angle. She's laid parallel to the track. If she'd been hit, she'd be at an angle. If she'd fallen, she'd be where she fell. This is a straight line. A person was laid out on the ballast and left."

The two troopers looked at each other. One of them wrote something down.

"Did you get her name?" Art asked.

"No. No papers. No cell phone. No wallet. No shoes inside. Just what she's wearing."

Art knelt, slowly, his knees protesting. He looked at her face. Not at the bruise, which was new. He looked at the hands. The fingernails were clean. No dirt under them. No calluses. He looked at the boots—good boots, but worn in a way that suggested the owner kept them inside most of the time. He looked at the jacket. A zipper with a pull tag still attached to it.

He did not touch her.

He stood, turned, and walked the length of the yard. The pines at the ridge were gone in the fog. He walked until he reached the water tower, the rusted metal thing that had once supplied the engines at the mountain station. He looked back toward the body.

He came back and stood over the second line, the way he used to stand before a departure. He looked at the ballast on either side of the rails. He looked for the small white paint marks that had once indicated the crossing points—the little dashes at the edges where the service road met the track.

They were still there. Faded, but there. And there, between the two rails of the second line, just above the body's head, was something small and bright. Brass. A token, the kind the Cedar Hollow Line used for transfers at the mountain station. A small disc, worn smooth on one side and stamped with the old mountain logo on the other.

Art reached for it. Whitcomb said something that was not quite an order.

Art held up his hand.

"I've been on that line," he said, in the flat voice he'd used for twenty-nine years, "for twenty-nine years. That token was only issued at the mountain station. Only for passengers transferring to the freight cars. The line closed in '96. The tokens were melted down and put in the county office drawer for the heritage fund. There were maybe a hundred and twenty of them. They were never put back in circulation."

He held the token up.

"This isn't a random body, Dale. She was placed here. And whoever placed her knew exactly which track, exactly which crossing, exactly where the paint marks were. And he had a transfer token. Or he stole one out of the county drawer."

The fog thickened. The dog barked once at nothing.

Art put the token in his shirt pocket, over his heart, the way he had carried his conductor's watch for thirty years.

"Who was she?" he asked.

Whitcomb shifted his weight. "That's what I want to find out. I'm calling the state, because there's no one here with this kind of—"

"You know who she was," Art said.

It wasn't a question. Whitcomb's face did something. A small thing. The fog rolled over the ridge behind them.

"I don't know who she was," Whitcomb said, and his voice had the flatness back in it, the one where a man is holding his mouth shut and his eyes are doing the talking. "I'm calling the state. You can call your lawyers, if you want. You can call your friends. But you're not my man, Art. You retired."

Art looked at the body. He looked at the token in his hand. He looked at the pines at the ridge, which had disappeared and reappeared like something breathing.

"I was a conductor," he said. "I read people the way I read the tracks. And I'll tell you what I'm reading."

He pointed to the body's left hand, the one that had been lying flat against the ballast.

"Her right hand was gripping the strap of her bag. The left hand was open. Fingers slightly curled, like she was reaching for something. Look at the space. About six inches. That's where a phone would be. Or a key. Or a small object. She was reaching for something, and the person who placed her here put her hand there for you to find the gap and think she was holding something. He was directing your eye."

Whitcomb stared at the body. The troopers stared.

Art turned back to Whitcomb.

"The token goes in the drawer, Dale. Not your pocket. Not my. The drawer. Because if it disappears, I will know someone is cleaning this up faster than I can work it. And I will know they were here before me, which means they're in this town, and they know the line, and they're not going to stop at one body."

He walked back to his truck. The fog was so thick now that he could only see the rearview mirror and the road behind him, which was gone, which was also gone.

He started the engine. The F-250 coughed, caught.

He sat there a long moment, hand on the wheel, not driving, thinking.

The last passenger train. Fourteen people. A violinist. A case left behind.

The mountain pass. The single line. The fog that came in every October.

He put the truck in gear and drove down the service road, into the town, into the grey.

Behind him, in the switchyard, the dog sat on the ballast and watched the fog, and the young woman's eyes were open, and the token was no longer where it had been, and the left hand was still reaching for something, and the fog kept coming, and kept coming, and the pines at the ridge did not reappear.
