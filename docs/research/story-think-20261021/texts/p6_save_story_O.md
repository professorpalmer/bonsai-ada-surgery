# The Long Ladle

The galley smelled of nothing. That was the first thing a person noticed when they started the cook rotation on the *Aldous*, the second thing they noticed was that they could never get the smell of something.

Mara had been born in the belly of the ship, in a pod no bigger than a closet, to a mother who had died three days before the ship's engines finished their first adjustment. She had grown up in corridors that curved gently like the inside of a shell. Her favorite place was the hydroponics bay, where the light was green and the air had the faintest sweetness of things that were alive and growing. Now she was twenty-six, and she was the cook on the *Aldous*, which meant she was the person who was supposed to make the ship feel like it had once been a place people could die on.

The *Aldous* was a generation ship. That was the kind of phrase people used back on the old Earth, before the sky went brown, before the oceans got too warm, before the cities started arguing about which ones were worth saving. The *Aldous* was one of the ships they'd built to carry people to a star that might, might, might be habitable. Four hundred years of travel. Four hundred years of the same people, the same children, the same grandchildren, the same great-grandchildren, all living their whole lives on a metal tube that was slowly turning a slow circle around the void.

Mara's job was to make food. That was it. That was all. There were three hundred and twelve people aboard the *Aldous* at the time, and Mara had to make sure every single one of them ate something every single day that they could call a meal.

She stood in the galley, which was a small room near the middle of the ship's length, with the big circular window behind it looking out into the dark. The stars didn't move. They were pinned to the dark like insects under glass, and if Mara stared at them too long, she would start to feel like they were watching her back. She kept her eyes on the stove instead.

"Morning, cook," said a voice from the doorway.

Mara looked up. It was Jonas, the engineer who handled the water recycling system. He was a big man, broad through the shoulders, with a face that had gone red in the neck from the work of the shift. He had a smear of grease on his forehead and a look on his face that said he had just been told something he didn't want to hear.

"Morning, Jonas," Mara said.

"The reclamation unit's acting up again. Third time this week."

"I know."

"You know?"

"I've been here three days. I know the reclamation unit acts up again every Wednesday."

Jonas blinked. He looked at her like she was being a little too smart about it, like she was a trick, and then he laughed, a short sharp laugh that surprised both of them.

"Right. Well. I'll send someone to check the filters."

"Thanks, Jonas."

He nodded, and then he was gone. The door hissed shut behind him, and the galley was quiet again except for the low hum of the lights and the distant tick of the cooling panels.

Mara turned back to the stove. She had a list in front of her, written in her own hand, and she looked at it for a long time.

Oatmeal. Toast. Eggs, if we had eggs. Coffee, if we had coffee.

She had eggs. She had coffee. She had flour, and sugar, and salt, and a small tin of something called vanilla extract that she kept in a drawer like it was a secret. She had rice, and potatoes, and a crate of canned vegetables that had been on the ship since the day she was born, and a crate of frozen fruits that had been there since before she was born.

She had also, which was the thing she told herself was the best part of the job, a small garden on the upper deck. Not a garden like the old world, with flowers and everything. Just a few rows of greens, and some tomatoes that were too small and too sour to eat on their own, and a few herbs, rosemary and thyme and a little bit of mint, that she grew in little pots on the railing by the window.

It was a small thing. It was almost nothing. But when the tomatoes were ripe, and the mint was growing, and the rosemary had that green, sharp smell that filled the air when she touched the leaves, she could almost pretend that the *Aldous* was still moving through a place that had a name.

She started to make the breakfast.

She heated up a pot of water and poured the oats in. She set the toaster to the low setting, because the toaster had a bad habit of burning things, and she cracked two eggs into a small pan and watched the whites turn opaque at the edges. She made the coffee in the machine that had been on the *Aldous* since the beginning, which was to say since the end of the old world, and it made the best coffee the ship had ever made, which was to say it made coffee that was good, and it was warm, and it had a smell that was just right, and she drank a cup of it before she handed any out.

It was a small thing. It was almost nothing.

She brought the food out to the dining hall, which was a big room in the middle of the ship, with tables that curved along the walls and a window at one end that looked out at the dark. The people came in slowly, the way they always did, in small clusters, talking in low voices, and they sat down, and they ate, and they were quiet for a little while, and then they started to talk again, and the talk filled the room like water filling a glass.

Mara stood in the corner and watched. She watched the people eating. She watched the way the light from the window fell on their faces. She watched the way the old people sat down slowly and the young people sat down fast. She watched the way the children ran between the tables and the way the adults pretended not to be annoyed by it.

She was the cook. She made the food. She stood in the corner and watched.

And sometimes, when the light hit the window just right, and the steam from the coffee cups rose up in thin, lazy threads, she thought that this was it. That this was what it was all about. Not the stars. Not the four hundred years of travel. Not the engine or the water recycling or the hydroponics bay. Just this. The small, warm, quiet thing of people sitting down to eat together, and being alive, and being here.

She thought about her mother, who had died in a pod no bigger than a closet, who had never seen the dining hall, who had never stood in a galley and watched the light fall on a room full of people. She thought about how her mother had been a botanist, and how she had spent her whole life in the hydroponics bay, tending the plants, and how she had died with her hands in the soil, and how the soil had been the last thing she had ever touched.

Mara had become a cook because she had wanted to feed the people who were still here. She had become a cook because the hydroponics bay was too small for her, and because the dining hall was the only place on the ship where she felt like she belonged. She had become a cook because it was the only job that didn't have to be explained, and the only job that made sense.

She made the breakfast. She made the dinner. She made the meals in between. She made them every day, and every day, the people ate, and every day, the ship turned, and every day, the stars stayed where they were, pinned to the dark like insects under glass, and she stood in the galley and watched them, and she thought that this was enough. That this was the thing. That the whole long, slow journey, the whole four hundred years of turning in the dark, was just a way of getting to a room where people could sit down and eat, and be alive, and be here, and be, for one small moment, exactly where they were supposed to be.

She would be here for a long time. She knew that. She was the cook on the *Aldous*, and the *Aldous* was going to be here for a long time, and the stars were going to stay where they were, and the people were going to keep coming, and keeping going, and sitting down at the tables, and eating, and talking, and being alive.

And she would make the breakfast. And she would make the dinner. And she would make the meals in between.

She would do it every day, and every day, it would be enough.
