## The Shape of the Room

The first letter arrived on the day the gale stopped.

Silas Marr was fifty-four, and keeper of Morvaine Light had been his whole life: light, trim the wick, log the weather, sleep in the small room at the foot of the stair, wake before the sun, do it again. The last thing he expected from the sea was paper. The tin came up on the shingle at the foot of the stair, waxed shut, sealed in blue with the impression of a bird. Inside was a single sheet, folded into a square.

"Dear stranger, I am writing from the marsh at Dunmoss. The house is new and the room is yellow, with one window that looks out onto the water. I am not yet born, but I know the shape of the room. When the keeper at the end of the black headland sees this letter, tell him I am coming. My name is Clara. C."

In the corner, in a clerk's hand: Dunmoss, 14 June 1899. The paper was new. The ink looked quite old.

Silas stood in the gallery for a long time while the light turned above him. Dunmoss was a name only the surveyors used for a stretch of drowned marsh that would not become a village for another decade, if it became one at all. He checked the parish registers the next morning. No Clara Wren. No Wren, indeed, in three counties. He asked the postmaster at the village if he knew it.

The second letter came in the spring of 1904, in the same hand. Dunmoss, 14 June 1908. "I am learning my letters. The master's name is Mr. Hale and he smells of chalk. My father hums a song about a boat in the kitchen. I have seen your light once, from the boat, and it made the whole sea look like something that could be held."

Silas wrote back. He did not know why. He had not received a letter since his wife Maura died, and he was old enough to have stopped being frightened by strange things. "Dear Clara, I am the keeper at the end of the black headland. I do not know how to answer a girl who is not yet born, but I will try. The eastern window in the gallery is still unpainted. I have been meaning to paint it."

He posted it to an address that did not exist, at a postbox that took everything and returned nothing. Another letter came from the marsh in the summer of 1905, postmarked 1909. In the winter of 1907 a third, postmarked 1912: "I am married. My husband's name is Fionn. He is a sailor, and he sings badly, and I am beginning to love it."

Year after year he wrote to her. He wrote of the weather and the ships, of the gulls that had taken to nesting in the gallery, of small things. The letters came through the years — one in 1908, one in 1910, one in 1914 — each arriving in the present with a date in the future, the girl growing on the page, the room still yellow, the window always one. Once, in the autumn of 1910, the letter described a storm: the sea over the dunes, the house shaking, her father carrying the dog up to the upper room. Silas read it at the eastern window. A week later the sea rose to the gallery steps and the lamp shuddered in its frame, and he knew, without knowing, that she had felt the same wind in another house.

In the autumn of 1916 he wrote, "Do you believe the letters come from the future?"

The answer came a year later, in a smaller hand: "I believe the letters come from the person. The date is only a formality. C."

Something in him loosened. He began to live, for the first time, for the future, which was a strange habit for a man whose life had been narrowed to the keeping of a light. In the autumn of 1908 he painted the eastern window of the gallery lead white, though his letter had only said he had been meaning to paint it. He set potatoes in the little plot below the house. He left a candle in the eastern window on clear nights, the way he had come to leave things for someone who had not yet come.

For the first time since Maura's death, Silas had a future worth tending.

In the summer of 1918 the letters stopped. The world had turned to war. The last letter from the marsh was thin and plain: "There is a war in the world. I will write when there is something safe to write."

Then, in the winter of 1919: "Fionn is gone. A shell, at the coast. I am alone, and there is a girl in my belly, and she is coming. I will come to you in the autumn. My mother says I should not, but my mother is not the one who kept your letters."

The autumn came. A woman came up the stair with a child on her hip. She was tired, and her eyes were the grey-green of a March sea. She carried the marsh smell. Elowen, the child, slept with her face against Clara's shoulder.

"Clara Wren," Silas said, at the door.

"You painted the eastern window," she said. "Your letter said you would. I have read every letter. My mother kept them in a box. I read the first when I was nine, and I have read the years before I had them."

He led her inside. He had no explanation for a place that was not there, or for letters that went out to sea and came back to no one. He did not need one. She set the small tin on the table — blue wax, a wren — and opened it.

"Your letters came," she said. "All of them."

She sat at the desk. Elowen woke, saw the beam swinging through the glass, and went quiet. Silas looked at the girl who had written from before she existed, and thought: you crossed a country no one had mapped, and I am the only one who ever got your letters. He poured tea for the two of them. It went cold, and neither of them drank it.

That night the light turned above them and the child slept, and Silas sat at the desk with his wife's old shawl about his shoulders, and he understood, at last, that he had not been keeping the light for the ships. He had been keeping it for a girl.

In the spring of 1920 the doctor from the village came up the stair and shook his head. The winter would be hard, he said, and the spring harder, and then there would be no more of it. Silas went back to work as though nothing had changed. The log wanted its entries. The wick wanted its trimming.

In January he wrote a last letter.

"Dear Clara. The winter will be hard for me, and I am writing it from the desk in the gallery. The eastern window is white and the sea is flat and the light is turning. I have kept it for you. All of it. The years I did not know I was waiting for. The keeper."

He had written to a place that was not there for twenty-two years. It had begun to feel less like an address and more like a door. He dropped the letter in the box with the others. He did not know whether it would arrive. He did not need it to.

On the last clear night of January, with snow on the gallery and the beam sweeping the black water, Silas Marr went down to the small room at the foot of the stair and sat in the yellow light. Clara held Elowen's hand at the door and watched the beam sweep out over the water. She did not call for him.

In the morning Elowen drew in the logbook: a yellow square, and inside it a smaller square. One window.

The light was manned until the spring of 1951. The last keeper, a young man who had come up from the village below, found a tin on the shingle after a gale. It was his first winter at the light. Inside was a single sheet, folded into a square.

"Dear stranger, I am writing from the marsh at Dunmoss. The house is new and the room is yellow, with one window that looks out onto the water. I am not yet born, but I know the shape of the room. When the keeper at the end of the black headland sees this letter, tell him I am coming. My name is Clara. C."

Somewhere ahead, on the marsh, in a yellow room with one window, a girl who was not yet born was learning the shape of things.
