## final text

The lighthouse stood on a headland called the Toe, because the headland pointed out at the sea the way a foot points at a door. Its keeper, Eadric Vale, was seventy-two years old and had been standing in that lamp room for forty-one of them. He told no one this, because no one ever came, and the few who did come were usually soaked, shivering, and looking at him with the particular blankness of people who have just lost the last reason for their journey.

For twenty years, the only sound Eadric heard at night was the tick of the clock on the wall and the low groan of the wind against the stone. He had married a woman named Maren in the village at the foot of the cliff, a village called Bream, which had forty-three houses and a church with a leaking roof, and they had been happy in the way that only people who share a small, salt-eaten world can be happy. Maren had a laugh like a kettle, and she made bread that rose so high the neighbors thought it was a new kind of bird nesting in the ovens. And then the winter the fever came, and the fever took her, and Eadric came up to the lighthouse because a lighthouse keeper does not ask to be given a tower. A lighthouse keeper is given a tower, and the tower is given a keeper, and neither of them has a say in the arrangement.

He did not come up because he was brave. He came up because Maren, on her last good evening, had set her hand against his cheek and said, "You have always been good with light, Ead. Don't let it go out." And so he did not let it go out. He oiled the lens, he trimmed the wicks, he polished the glass until it was as clear as a held breath, and every night he stood there, a small gray figure behind a great circle of fire, keeping the ships from the rocks.

It was in his thirtieth winter at the tower that the storm came that changed things.

The storm had no name, which was worse than a name, because named things can be spoken to, and speaking to a thing is the first step to keeping it from you. The unnamed storm came up out of the north with a sound like a million stones being dragged across a floor, and the sea rose until the waves were not waves but mountains, and the mountains broke on the cliffs with a roar that shook the paint from the tower's walls. Eadric climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room with his lamp and his coat, and he stood at the window, and he saw, out in the black and the white, a ship.

It was not a big ship. It was a brigantine, the kind with a square sail and a long narrow hull, and it was on its side, caught against the rocks below the headland, with water pouring through its broken hull and its lights swinging in the dark like the eyes of a wounded animal. The crew were dead, or they would be dead within the hour, for the sea had been rising all night and the ship was sinking, and the rocks were waiting for it, patient as teeth.

Eadric was not a strong man, and he was not a swimmer, and he was afraid in a way he had not been afraid since the day Maren had died. But he had oil on him, and he had rope, and he had a small boat that he kept tied to the side of the tower for emergencies, and he did the only thing he could think of, which was to go.

He launched the boat in the black water, and the sea took him, and he rowed, and the rope he had tied behind him paid out and paid out until he was out past the reach of the headland, where the water was deep and the waves were tall and the storm was at its loudest. He reached the ship just as the last of the lights went out, and he found them in the hold: a woman, a child, and an old man with a map rolled in oilskin.

The woman was young, or she had been, and she was cold and she was awake, and when Eadric reached her she did not scream. She only looked at him, and said, "You shouldn't be here."

"I am a keeper," he said. "It is what I do."

She laughed, and it was a small sound, and it cost her. "There is no one here to keep," she said.

He pulled her into the boat, and the old man, and the child, and the map, and the sea closed over the ship like a hand closing over a coin, and he rowed home through the storm, and by the time he reached the tower the sun was coming up, and the storm was over, and the sea was still, and the world looked the same as it had the day before, except that he had four more people in it, and one of them was a child, and the child had a map, and the map had a name on it that Eadric had never heard and could not forget.

The name was Verath.

The old man told him, as the storm passed and the sun came out and Eadric's hands stopped shaking, that Verath was a city that had been under the sea for a thousand years, a city that had built the lighthouse, or the first lighthouse, the one that stood on the headland before the one that Eadric stood in, and that had sunk when its people made a deal with the sea to survive the winter, and that the city had been waiting, ever since, for someone to bring the map back to the light. The old man said this in a voice like dry leaves, and he said it without looking at Eadric, because he was already gone, or nearly gone, and the child was crying, and the woman was quiet, and Eadric was holding them all and trying not to fall apart.

They did not go down to the city that night, or the next day, or the next week. The sea was too rough, and the map was a map of the sea, and the sea was a thing that had to be asked, not commanded, and Eadric did not know how to ask. He knew how to keep a light burning. He knew how to trim a wick, and how to oil a lens, and how to stand in a room with a clock on the wall and a wind against the glass, and he knew that there are other ways to be afraid of the dark, and that the dark does not always ask to be kept out. He fed the child. He mended the woman's coat. He built a fire in the kitchen and he cooked a meal and he sat at the table with them and he said nothing, because there was nothing to say that was true enough.

But at night, when the lamp was lit and the clock was ticking and the sea was breathing against the rocks, Eadric took the map down to the floor and he unrolled it, and he looked at it, and he saw that it was not a map of water. It was a map of light. The lines on it were not currents or depths or reefs. They were beams, and they were all aimed at the headland, and they were all aimed at the tower, and they were all aimed at the lamp that Eadric had been burning for forty-one years. He understood, then, what the old man had been trying to say, and he understood that he had been the light all along, and that the city was not under the sea, but in the light, and that the light had been waiting for him to see it.

He did not go down to the city. He did not have to. He climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, and he stood at the window, and he looked out at the sea, and he saw, in the light, a city of towers and spires and bridges, and he saw a street, and he saw a door, and he saw a woman standing in the doorway, and she had a laugh like a kettle, and she was making bread, and the bread was rising so high the neighbors were looking at it and thinking it was a new kind of bird, and Eadric stood at the window, and he wept, and the light went on.

And if you ask, what happened to the map, and the woman, and the child, and the old man, I will tell you this: the old man woke up, and he went home, and he lived a quiet life, and he died in his sleep, and he never told anyone about Verath. The child grew up to be a keeper of his own, of a small light in a small village, and he never went to the sea. The woman, whose name was Lira, came back to Bream, and she found a church with a leaking roof, and she found a man named Eadric who was not there, because he was in the lamp room, and she waited for him, and he came down, and he took her hand, and they walked home, and they made bread, and the bread rose, and the neighbors thought it was a bird, and they did not know that it was a city, but they were happy anyway, because a bird is a kind of city, and a city is a kind of bird, and both of them are made of light, and both of them need to be kept burning.

And the lighthouse kept burning, and the sea kept breathing, and the wind kept groaning against the stone, and the clock kept ticking, and the map stayed on the floor of the kitchen, rolled up and tied with string, and no one ever unrolled it again, because it was not needed anymore, because the light was enough, and the light had always been enough, and Eadric, the old keeper, the man who had been good with light, stood in the lamp room, and he looked out at the sea, and he saw the city, and he saw the door, and he saw the woman, and he saw the bread, and he saw the bird, and he saw the light, and he kept it going.

That is the story. It is the story of a man who kept a light, and a light that kept a city, and a city that kept a woman, and a woman who kept a man, and a man who kept a light, and a light that kept a story, and a story that kept a world, and a world that kept a sea, and a sea that kept a storm, and a storm that kept a ship, and a ship that kept a map, and a map that kept a city, and a city that kept a light, and a light that kept a man, and a man who kept a story, and a story that keeps this, and this keeps you, and you keep this, and we keep each other, and the light goes on.
