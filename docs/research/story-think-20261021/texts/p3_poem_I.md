## final text

The last morning in the orchard, I wake before the sun,
and the air still holds the sweetness of the day before.
I take the bucket out; the rows are cold and quiet.
I climb the ladder to the bough where the fruit is red,
and pull them down, one by one, into the bucket's rim,
gathering the summer's day into a small, red weight.
The work has grown easy by now; my hands remember the shape.
The foreman's voice comes through the dust: "Last basket, then."
I look back down the long rows where the shadows lie,
and feel the season slipping through the branches like water.
By noon the sky is a clean blue, and the orchard is bare of others—
only the birds, and me, and the sound of the ladder.
I sit on an overturned crate and eat a warm peach,
its juice running down my wrist like a small red river.
The foreman counts the buckets by the door; the numbers are good.
"You'll do better next year," he says, as if it were a promise,
as if the sun would stand in the same place, as if the apples
would fill the same trees in the same heavy way.
I know that is not true, but the words are warm anyway.
I sweep the last of the stems and leaves from the walk.
My hands are rough from the stems and rough from the sun,
and the dark lines of the dirt are under the nails.
I lean the work boots against the gate; the laces are frayed from August.
The truck's engine coughs to life, and the whole orchard shakes.
I climb into the cab and look back at the rows:
long green lines that disappear into the heat.
I think of my hands, how they fit the fruit,
how I have learned the way to hold an apple
so it will not bruise, how I have learned
the way to work without making a sound.
The road unspools like a ribbon behind the field.
The orchard shrinks to a square of green, then to a line,
then to a green blur, then to nothing at all.
I drive until the trees are a memory
of something I once held up and felt in the sun.
That night I wash my hands at the sink,
and the water turns the dirt to brown, and the dirt runs away.
But something of the season stays on the skin:
the sweet, green scent, the weight, the red,
the last basket of the year, still waiting by the door.
