# Chapter 1: The Long Inhale

The city of Vael had no foundation, no bedrock, no mountain. It was built on a shell the size of an island, and the island was alive.

Every morning, the harbor bells rang not for the tide but for the breath. Deep below the cobblestone streets of the Lower Quarters, something vast inhaled, and the whole city shuddered upward, and the bell towers swayed on their iron anchors, and the fish in the canal-ditches went still, and the women with laundry on their lines whispered the old prayer: *May the Shell carry us, and the Water bear the Shell.*

Mira knew the prayer the way she knew her own heartbeat. She was a Shell-Watcher, one of the children on the watch-platform at the city's eastern edge, where the shell's edge dropped away into the Sea of Mists and the giant's back became the world's.

This morning the platform was crowded. Old Fen was already there, leaning on his staff, his one glass eye fixed on the horizon.

"Something's off," he said.

Mira looked. The sea was wrong. Not the color—the mist sea was always the color of a bruise in morning light. She meant the water line. Where the shell met the water, the waves used to break in a white curtain that stretched three hundred steps to either side of the city. Today the waves curled and died short. The water sat back, as if something out beyond the mist was holding its breath.

"That's not how it does," she said.

"No," Old Fen said. "It is not."

Mira had grown up knowing the stories. The elders said the turtle was the last of a kind, older than the kingdoms below the mist, older than the names people still used for the stars. Its name, if it had one, had been swallowed by the sea. Its shell had become the island where the city's founders had landed three hundred years ago, fleeing a war no living person remembered. They had nailed the first chapel to the shell, and the second, and the third, and slowly, the whole city had grown out of the living hide, until the houses were like barnacles and the streets were like the ridges of the shell's ancient patterns.

People were born here, grew up, and died. The turtle walked, but it walked slowly—a mile in a day, they said, and only when the mist thinned. Children played in the shell's crevices without ever knowing they were playing on something that could, if it chose, sink an entire continent.

The city's children had grown up believing the shell was as still as the sea was still. The ridges the elders called the Old Patterns were worn into stairways, and the great central depression—the Hollow, where the oldest houses clustered like seeds in a palm—was the place where the first chapel stood, and where, on the first morning of every month, the whole city's bells were tuned until the water in the canal-ditches shivered in time, as if the thing below were listening.

But this morning, the water line had changed.

Mira went down the spiral steps to the street, and the city was already talking. The market stalls had opened, and the smell of roasting kelp and honeybread hung in the cold air, but no one was buying. Merchants stood by their carts, watching the horizon. A fisherman had tied his boat to the dock and was staring at the water the way people stare at a thing they suspect is staring back.

"Mist-thin," a woman said. "The old ones said that means the turtle has seen something it remembers."

"Who is the old one?" someone asked.

"Does it matter? What matters is what the sea is doing."

Mira found her mother in the bakery on Bell Street. Her mother was kneading dough, and her hands were steady, which was the worst kind of quiet.

"Mother?"

"Your father would be angry," her mother said, not looking up. "He always said the sea changes first."

"Mother."

Her mother set the dough down and washed her hands at the basin. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and her voice was flat, as if she had already rehearsed what she was going to say.

"The Council met an hour ago. They've ordered the watch-towers to fire the signal lanterns when they see movement. They've ordered the fish to be landed and the boats to be tied to the pylons. They've ordered everything."

"What did they order me to do?"

Her mother looked at her for a long moment, and something in Mira's chest tightened.

"Nothing," her mother said. "That's the problem."

Mira ran to him. "Fen. What?"

He turned, and his glass eye caught the light. "The mist is not thinning, child. It is being lifted."

And deep beneath the cobblestones, beneath the houses and the churches and the roots of the street, the thing beneath the city—the thing they called the Shell, the thing they had built their lives on, the thing that had carried them through three hundred years of weather and war and quiet, ordinary mornings—pulled its breath in, and held it.
