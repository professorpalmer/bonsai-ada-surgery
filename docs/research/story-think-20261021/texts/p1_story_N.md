## final text

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday in November, slipped under the lighthouse door with the mail carrier's stamp still damp. Elias Finch, sixty-one years old and keeper of the Point Vane Light for the forty-third consecutive year, set it on the stone table beside his tea and looked at the envelope the way a man looks at a footprint on a windowpane: with the certainty that he does not believe what he is seeing.

No return address. No postmark. Just his name in a hand he had never seen before—Clara's name written on the outside in pencil, as though someone had addressed the letter to himself and then, mid-sentence, decided to write *from* someone instead.

He opened it in the lamp room, where the great Fresnel lens turned its slow, mechanical waltz overhead. The letter was three pages of ruled notebook paper, the ink blue and slightly bled at the edges.

*Dear Mr. Finch,*

*My name is Clara Wynn. I am writing to you from a town I have not yet learned the name of. I am twenty-six years old. I am a cartographer, though I have only been one for four months and I am still terrible at it. I have a scar on my left thumb from a paper cut that went on longer than it should have. I am writing because I have been thinking about your light all day, the one at Point Vane, and I wanted to say that it looks from here like a promise someone made to the sea. I do not know if that is a fair thing to say. I am only saying it because it is true and because I have no one else to say it to yet.*

*I hope the weather treats you well.*
*Clara*

Elias read it twice. Then he read it a third time, standing at the window while the November wind pushed the sea against the rocks in short, angry bursts. He looked at the letter. He looked at the hand. He looked at his own hands, thick-knuckled and mapped with the same kind of small scars from paper cuts and rope burns. He put the letter in his breast pocket and went to the tea.

The second letter came on Thursday. The third on the following Monday. By the end of the first week, five had arrived, and Elias had begun to leave a small brass bell by the door so he would hear when the mail carrier passed. The letters grew in frequency and in specificity. Clara wrote about her work: the way the coastline changed after a storm, the way a river narrowed in September, the way the old women in the market square argued about the price of salt as though it were a moral question. She wrote about her cat, a grey one called Ink, who slept on the drafting table. She wrote about a winter that would, she said, be *the* winter, and about a man she had met at a train station whose name she had not yet decided to remember.

She wrote, in the seventh letter, *I know you think this is strange. I am not a stranger to the strange. I am simply arriving a little early, which I find rather inconvenient. Time has a habit of folding for me, like a napkin on a good table. I will not explain it. I do not think explanation is the point. The point is that I am here, and I am here, and I want you to know that the light at Point Vane is the first thing I ever saw in this folded-over moment, and I have been trying to tell it to you for a long time.*

Elias stopped answering. He started writing.

He kept a small ledger of his replies, though he never sent them. He wrote to Clara about the weather, the fog, the way the sea turned copper-colored in October and went to iron in December. He wrote about the old keeper, his father, who had died on the steps of the lighthouse in 1961 and had been looking at the water the way other men look at a book. He wrote, *Do you know what it is like to be the only person on an island that is not an island? I mean, you can drive up in ten minutes if the road is not out. But the people in the town below, they do not come up. They look at the light and they thank it, but they do not come and sit in the room where the light is made.*

He wrote, *I think you are lonely. I think you are lonely in the same way I am lonely. The difference is that I have lived long enough to be used to the shape of it.*

He never sent the letters. They went into a tin box under his mattress, stacked in the order he wrote them, and they grew a small, private library.

In February the storms came. The sea rose and the rocks turned into teeth. Elias climbed the one hundred and fourteen steps to the lamp room every night, trimmed the wick, cleaned the lens, and watched the light turn. On the night of the thirtieth, a ship's horn sounded from somewhere beyond the breakwater. A fishing trawler, struggling through a squall. He watched it through the glass, the little red eye of it bobbing and listing, and he kept the light turning and he kept the light turning. The next morning the trawler's name was in the local paper: *Safe to harbor. No injuries.* No one mentioned the lighthouse. The lighthouse is not the story. The lighthouse is the reason the story has no other ending.

Clara wrote through the storms. Her letters came thinner in that season, the handwriting shakier. *It is very cold. Ink has taken to the radiator. I am thinking of you and your light and the way you keep it turning when no one is watching. I think that is the whole of it, isn't it? Not the sea, not the ships. The keeping. The turning. The refusal to let the dark be the last word of the evening.*

Elias read that letter on a Saturday, sitting in his chair by the stove, and he wept a little. He did not know why. He set the letter in the tin box and went to make bread.

The last letter arrived in May. It was only one page. The handwriting was smaller, tighter, as though the hand holding the pen had grown tired.

*My dear Mr. Finch,*

*I have been thinking about you every morning since I first wrote. I want you to know that the town I was never going to name is called Harrow's Reach, and the cartography job is gone, and I have a new one in a city I have not yet seen. I am leaving on a Tuesday. I do not know which Tuesday. I do not know if you will ever meet me in the straight, un-folded line of time, the line where every letter arrives in the order it was sent and where a woman twenty-six and a man sixty-one meet over a glass of coffee and nothing miraculous happens. But I want you to know: the light. You keep the light. I will keep my thumb and my cat and my small, ordinary days. And when the wind comes from the west, look at Point Vane and think that someone, somewhere in a room that is not quite a room, is looking back.*

*Yours, in a way I will not explain,*
*Clara*

He read it on the porch in the late afternoon. The light was still turning in the window, a slow white circle on the ceiling of the lamp room. The sea was the color of the sky, which is to say it was neither one thing nor the other. He put the letter in his hand and held it for a long time, and he did not put it in the tin box. He put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, and he walked back inside, and he climbed the one hundred and fourteen steps.

The letters stopped. He knew they would, because he had known for some time before they stopped, the way you know a winter is ending before the first crocus appears. He waited. He left the bell by the door. He climbed the stairs every night.

In August a woman came to the lighthouse. She was twenty-six and had a scar on her left thumb from a paper cut that went on longer than it should have. She sat in his kitchen and drank his tea and talked about cartography and grey cats and the price of salt at the market square. She did not say *Clara* and he did not say *Clara* and she said, at the end, *I think you've been expecting someone,* and he said, *I think I have,* and they both looked at the light through the window, turning and turning, and neither of them said what the light was for.

That night Elias wrote a letter. He did not send it. He wrote it in blue ink on ruled notebook paper. He set it in the tin box under his mattress, where it lay with the others, a small and ordinary collection of words that had traveled a distance no postmark could explain. He climbed the stairs. He trimmed the wick. He cleaned the lens. He kept the light turning.

Outside, the sea made its long, low sound against the rocks. The dark came down like a hand over the mouth of the world. And the light, and the light, and the light.
