# The Taste of Salt

The Meridian had no sea. Elara knew this the way the crew knew the direction of gravity—as something absent but constantly felt. The salt in her pantry was synthesized in the chemistry bay from seawater recovered from condensation, crystallized until it was pure and white and tasted of nothing at all. She had been the cook for eleven years now, and in eleven years no one on the Meridian had ever told her what the ocean actually tasted like.

She stood at her station at dawn, the long galley humming underfoot. Above her, the hydroponic lights cycled from blue to gold, waking the wheat fields and potato rows that climbed the curved walls of sector four. Below her, in the dark, the ship's heart beat: reactors, pumps, the slow turn of the generation garden that had carried three thousand descendants from Earth to a new star. Outside, there was nothing but black and the cold patient dark.

"Morning, Cook," said a voice from the service lift. Ilsa emerged in her botanist's coveralls, a smear of green on her cheek. Her daughter. Twenty-nine, third in the line of cooks on the Meridian, still asking the questions Elara had once asked.

"The whisper is back in sector nine," Ilsa said. It was not a greeting. It was a report.

The sector-nine failure was the third of the season. A spore in the irrigation water—the crew called it the whisper because it did not kill the plants quickly. It made them sullen, stunted, slow to leaf. The ship's nutritionist, a tight-lipped woman named Dr. Okafor who treated calories as mathematical certainties, had already announced the ration.

Elara wrote the numbers down on the grease-stained clipboard. Protein ration. Broth thinner. Bread smaller. She had learned to do the arithmetic of hunger before she had learned to read.

That night, alone in the galley, she took the tin from the magnet on the prep counter. Inside were three grains of salt. Earth salt. Found in the archive, in a sealed canister that had survived three centuries of drift, in a box that had once held a bottle of French olive oil. The canister's label had long since gone white. The salt inside was salt, though. Not white and nothing. One grain on the tongue meant rain and iron and a long way from anywhere.

She never used the grains. They were not ingredients. They were proof.

At the end of the week the whisper reached the wheat in sector two, and the whole ship slowed. Meals were smaller. Conversations at the tables got quieter, the way they did whenever the lights dimmed to save power. Children asked why the potatoes tasted like chalk. Elara told them potatoes had always tasted like chalk. The children were not convinced, but children never are, and it is a mercy.

Ilsa came to her the night before the anniversary with the small crate.

"They're not real," Ilsa said, setting the crate on the counter. "They're the idea of real. The closest we'll ever get."

Inside, wrapped in damp cloth, were six tomatoes. Small. Red-orange. Perfect. Ilsa had grown them in the personal garden behind the hydroponics lab, a place where botanists kept experimental strains and allowed failure to look like science. The seeds came from a capsule in the archive. She had grown them for two years, in secret, out of what Elara could only call stubbornness.

The secret was in the patience. Elara cooked the tomatoes over three days. First a paste: the tomatoes roasted in a hot tray until their skins blackened at the edges and their flesh gave up its sweetness. Then a reduction: the paste simmered with a little oil, a pinch of the ship's salt, and a strip of dried herb the archive called oregano. Finally a broth: the reduction thinned with the ship's own water and a small measure of flour.

It took everything she had.

On the night of the ship's anniversary she served the broth to the whole crew. A small bowl. A handful of noodles. A thin crust of bread. Nothing more. But when the first cup was passed, the galley went quiet in the way it had not gone quiet in eleven years.

An old woman on deck eleven closed her eyes and lifted her bowl to her lips and held it there a long time. A boy on the reactor deck set his bowl down and said, "It tastes like a story." Dr. Okafor, who did not weep, wept a little, and no one saw her.

It was not a tomato, not in any chemical sense. It was six small seeds, a botanist's patience, and three days of a cook's time, and it was the closest thing to Earth the Meridian had ever carried.

In the morning Ilsa brought her a new tin. Inside was a single seed, wrapped in paper, with a note that said: For the next one.

Elara put it in her drawer. She put the three grains of salt beside it. And when the first cup of the day was poured, she tasted the broth and, for the first time, did not wonder what the sea tasted like.

She knew.

It tasted like this.
