**Chapter One: The Last Stop**

The fog in Kettle Creek did not roll in so much as it exhaled, rising from the riverbed in thick, gray curtains that swallowed the pine trees whole. It was the kind of weather that made the mountain feel less like a landscape and more like a living thing, breathing slowly and keeping secrets.

Detective Elias Thorne sat on the porch of his small, cedar-sided house, watching the mist cling to the power lines. At sixty-four, Elias was a man who had spent forty years measuring the world in feet and inches, in the precise spacing of rail ties and the exact angle of a switch. He had been a train conductor for the Northern Highlands Line until his knees gave out and his heart decided it had done enough work for one lifetime.

Now, his job was mostly to watch the weather and worry about his arthritis. But the fog had a different smell today. It smelled of wet stone and something metallic, sharp and sour, like blood on a penny.

His front door creaked. Elias looked up, his eyes adjusting to the dim light. Standing there was Sheriff Miller, though he did not look like a man in uniform. He looked like a man in trouble. His hat was off, and his hands were tucked into his pockets, trembling slightly.

"Elias," Miller said. His voice was raspy. "You need to come to the station."

"The line is closed for the season," Elias said, standing up slowly. He leaned on his cane, the wood worn smooth by years of use. "There’s no one to conduct for."

"The conductor is dead," Miller said.

The words hung in the air, heavier than the fog. Elias blinked. "Who?"

"Arthur Pennington."

Arthur Pennington was not just any conductor. He was the last of the old guard, a man who knew every curve of the mountain track, who could hear a wheel bearing failing from a mile away. He was also Elias’s best friend of forty years.

"Where?" Elias asked, though he knew.

"The maintenance shed on the south side of the station. Miller said the police tape was up, but they were short-handed. The county detective is three hours away. Miller wants your eyes on it."

Elias looked at the fog again. It was swirling around the house, isolating them from the rest of the world. He knew he should go. It was the right thing to do. But as he looked at Miller, he saw something in the Sheriff’s eyes that made his stomach drop. It wasn’t fear, exactly. It was recognition. Miller knew who had done this, or at least, he knew who was about to be found.

"Come on, Elias," Miller said softly. "Please."

***

The station building was a relic of a time when trains were the lifeblood of the town. The wood was dark and peeling, and the windows were fogged over from the inside. The smell of old coal smoke and wet wool clung to the air.

Inside, the main office was dark. Miller led him through the back door and out onto the platform. The fog was so thick here that the tracks vanished into white after a few steps.

They walked to the maintenance shed, a squat, brick building at the end of the platform. The door was propped open with a plank of wood.

Arthur Pennington lay on the concrete floor, near a workbench cluttered with oil cans and wrenches. He was dressed in his uniform, the brass buttons catching the faint light from the window. A single red pool spread beneath him, mixing with the oil and dirt.

Elias stopped at the doorway. He did not go in.

"Elias," Miller said, his voice tight. "You need to see this."

Elias stepped inside. His eyes adjusted, and he took in the scene. Arthur’s face was pale, his eyes wide open, staring at the ceiling. There was no sign of struggle. No defensive wounds. Just the small, neat hole in his temple.

But it was the object lying next to Arthur’s hand that caught Elias’s eye.

It was a train ticket.

It was old, yellowed at the edges, the ink faded. It was from the Northern Highlands Line, Route 4. The departure time was listed, but the arrival time was blank. The ticket was dated three days ago.

Elias picked it up. His hands, which had never trembled in forty years, began to shake.

"Who is this?" Elias asked.

Miller shrugged. "That’s the question, isn’t it?"

Elias looked at the ticket again. He knew Route 4. It was the scenic line, the one that went through the narrowest parts of the canyon. It was the route the tourists took, the one that was known for its sharp turns and narrow bridges.

And it was the route that had been closed for repairs two weeks ago.

"Why is he dead with a ticket from a line that’s closed?" Elias asked.

Miller didn’t answer. He was staring at something else.

Elias looked. On the workbench, there was a ledger. It was open to a page with a list of names. And next to Arthur’s name, there was a checkmark.

Elias looked back at the ticket. The blank space for the arrival time wasn’t empty. There was a symbol there, a small, black circle.

He had seen that symbol before.

***

They went back to the station office. Miller called in a few more officers, but they were all nervous, unsettled by the fog and the death. Elias sat in Arthur’s chair, the same one where he had spent hundreds of hours planning the schedules for the last few years.

He looked at the ledger. The list of names was long. Passengers, cargo manifests, maintenance records. But next to Arthur’s name, the checkmark was red.

"There’s something else," Miller said, sitting down across from him. "The police found a phone in Arthur’s pocket. It was off, but the battery was still in."

"A phone?" Elias frowned. "Arthur didn’t use a phone. He used the radio."

"I know." Miller’s face was grim. "But there were messages. Encrypted."

Elias looked up. "Encrypted? On a phone?"

"I don’t know what it says," Miller said. "But the police tech guys are working on it. They said it’s not standard code. It’s... weird."

Elias felt a chill that had nothing to do with the cold. "What’s weird about it?"

Miller hesitated. "It’s in Morse code."

Elias laughed, a dry, humorless sound. "Morse code? On a smartphone?"

"Listen to me, Elias." Miller leaned forward. "Arthur was a conductor. He knew every line, every train, every schedule. If he was sending messages in Morse code, it means it was something he couldn’t say out loud. Something dangerous."

Elias looked at the ticket in his hand. The black circle. The blank space.

"It’s a threat," Elias said.

"It’s a warning," Miller corrected. "And Arthur died for it."

Elias stood up. He walked to the window, looking out at the fog. The train tracks were still visible, a dark line cutting through the white mist.

"Who was Arthur talking to?" Elias asked.

Miller didn’t answer. He was thinking.

Elias turned back to him. "Arthur was my friend. He trusted me more than anyone. If he was in trouble, he would have told me. Or he would have sent a signal."

"Like the ticket?" Miller asked.

Elias nodded. "Route 4. The scenic line. Closed for repairs."

"But the ticket was three days ago," Miller said. "The line has been closed for two weeks."

Elias frowned. "That means someone went on that line. After it was closed."

Miller’s eyes widened. "You think someone broke the lock on the maintenance shed?"

"No," Elias said, a slow smile spreading across his face. "I think someone knew where Arthur was. And they knew the route. And they knew the time."

Elias looked at the ticket again. The black circle. He knew what it meant.

"It’s a symbol," Elias said. "The old conductors used it. A 'Kiss of Death'. It meant a passenger was a threat. Someone who would be better off gone."

Miller’s face went pale. "So Arthur marked himself?"

"No," Elias said. "He marked someone else. But he died with the ticket. Which means... whoever sent the ticket, they wanted Arthur to know. And they wanted him to die."

Miller stood up. "We need to find out who sent that ticket."

Elias picked up the ticket. "It’s not the sender who killed Arthur, Miller. The sender is just a pawn. Someone is using Arthur’s reputation. Using the old ways. To cover their own tracks."

He looked at the ledger again. The list of names.

"Who was the last passenger on Route 4 before it was closed?" Elias asked.

Miller checked his notebook. "A group of tourists. A family. Four people."

"Four people," Elias repeated. "And a dead conductor."

He looked out the window. The fog was still thick, swallowing the mountain.

"We’re going to have to find out who was in that car," Elias said. "Because I have a feeling one of them didn’t get off at the last stop."
