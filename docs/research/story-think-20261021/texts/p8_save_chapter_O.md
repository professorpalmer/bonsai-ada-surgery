# Chapter One: The City That Breathed

The city of Tessarine breathed.

Every few hundred seconds, the cobblestone streets beneath Mira's boots rose and fell with a slow, tidal shudder. She had been born on the back of the World-Turtle and had never once thought to question the rhythm, but this morning, as she climbed the last of the seven hills toward the Temple of the Old Shell, the breathing felt wrong. Too shallow. Too quick.

She pressed a hand against the warm stone of the temple wall and felt the heartbeat beneath it—a deep, ancient thrum that had pulsed since before her grandmother's grandmother had set foot on the shell. The heartbeat was skipping.

*What is wrong with you?* she thought, though she wasn't sure whether she was speaking to the turtle, to the city, or to her own racing pulse.

Mira was sixteen, with dirt beneath her fingernails and the particular stubbornness that her mother had always called "a flaw in the architecture." She was a shell-tender, one of the hundred-and-twelve workers who maintained the great stone city built upon the back of Tessarine the Elder, the turtle so vast that its shell stretched across an entire ocean and its head, which had never been seen alive, rested in the mist beyond the horizon.

The city had no name for the land beyond the shell. The elders said the turtle was all there was. The elders said the sea was the world's edge and the turtle was the world's center. Mira had believed both things the way you believe that the sky is not a ceiling, only until one day you find a crack in it.

And today was that day.

She reached the temple steps as the third breath came, and this one was a gasp. The whole city lurched, and a hundred chimneys on the lower quarter began to wail with a sound Mira had only ever heard in the stories—the sound of a creature in pain. Below, the harbor district was emptying. Fishwives pulled their boats to the piers. The great bells of the temple began to toll, not with the measured peals of morning worship, but in the frantic, overlapping chaos of the Alarm of the Deep.

Mira ran.

The streets of Tessarine were a maze of tiered stone, each level carved higher into the shell's curve to follow its natural arch. The Temple of the Old Shell sat at the highest point, a spiraling tower of pale stone that reached toward the clouds where the sky always curled and shimmered, as if the air itself were afraid of the edge. Below the temple, the city fell away in seven great terraces: the Market, the Homes, the Forges, the Schools, the Docks, the Gardens, and the Lowest Level, where the shell's bone-white undercarriage gleamed and the shadow of the creature's true body lay beneath like a sleeping god.

As she descended the steps, Mira's mind turned over the signs. The breathing had quickened. The heartbeat had stuttered. The city had shuddered with a pain no one had felt in living memory.

Her mother had told her once, in the hush of a fever night, that the turtle had a sickness.

"The elders hide it," her mother had said, her eyes red and bright. "They say Tessarine is eternal. They say the city is the world and the world is perfect. But nothing is eternal, Mira. And nothing is perfect. Not even a god."

Mira had been seven. She had thought her mother was ill.

Now, at sixteen, she understood that her mother had not been ill at all. She had been a warning.

The lower streets were thick with people. Fishwives argued with dockworkers. A priest in white robes ran through the crowd, slapping down a rumor with the back of his hand. *It is a dream,* he called out. *The turtle dreams sometimes. It is nothing.*

But the turtle had never dreamed with pain in its voice.

Mira pushed through the crowd and reached the Docks, where the harbor lay a churning gray sea lapping against the city's stone walls. The water was wrong. It was too still, and the stillness was wrong. The sea of the Old World was supposed to be a restless, heaving thing, full of wind and salt and the endless motion of a living creature's breath. This stillness was the stillness of something holding its breath.

And then she saw it.

A crack.

A thin, black line, no wider than a finger, split the pale stone of the shell at the waterline, where the city's foundations met the creature's skin. It ran the length of the dock, a dark vein of something that was not stone and not water and not quite the earth she knew.

And from the crack, something was coming up.

It rose slowly at first, like a limb from a dream. A hand. A hand made of pale, bone-white stone, with fingers too long and joints that bent in the wrong places. It gripped the edge of the crack, and Mira felt the whole city tilt as it pulled itself upward, inch by inch, like a diver breaking the surface of water.

A face emerged.

It was a face, but it was a face the way a painting is a person—wrong in every small and essential way. The eyes were too large, set too wide apart, and they were empty, with no color at all. The mouth was a long, thin line, and when it opened, the sound that came out was not a voice but a wind, a cold wind that smelled of deep water and old stone and something else, something Mira could not name.

The face looked up at the city.

The face looked at Tessarine.

And the face smiled.

The temple bells stopped tolling. The people stopped shouting. For one long, terrible moment, the entire city held still, and the only sound was the wind from the crack and the slow, shallow breathing of the turtle, as if the great creature beneath them had just realized, for the first time in its endless, patient life, that something else was alive.

Mira stood at the edge of the dock, her hand on the cold stone, her breath caught in her throat, and understood with a clarity that was almost like pain:

The turtle was not the world.

The turtle was only the door.

And something, something old and patient and very, very hungry, had just opened it.

She did not run.

She could not run.

The face on the other side of the crack had turned, and its empty eyes were looking at her.

Not through her.

At her.

And in that look, in that long, patient, hungry look, Mira saw the beginning of everything that was to come.

She saw the end of the world she had been told was eternal.

And, beneath the fear, beneath the cold, beneath the trembling in her hands, she saw the very first spark of something she did not yet have a name for:

Courage.

The wind from the crack blew warmer.

The turtle's heartbeat, for the first time in the history of the city, stopped.

And in the silence that followed, in the silence of a world holding its breath, Mira took her first step toward the door.
