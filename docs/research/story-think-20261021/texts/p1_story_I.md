**The Shape of the Room**
*(1,496 words)*

The first letter arrived on the day the gale stopped.

Silas Marr was fifty-four, keeper of Morvaine Light for twenty-two years, and the last thing he expected from the sea was paper. The lamp had been turning above him since he took the post, since before his wife Maura died in the winter of his first year, and the house had learned the sound of one breathing. The tin came up on the shingle at the base of the stair, waxed shut, sealed in blue with the impression of a bird. Inside was a single sheet, folded into a square.

"Dear stranger, I am writing from the marsh at Dunmoss. The house is new and the room is yellow, with one window that looks out onto the water. I am not yet born, but I know the shape of the room. When the keeper at the end of the black headland sees this letter, tell him I am coming. My name is Clara. — C."

At the corner, in a clerk's hand: Dunmoss, 14 June 1899.

Silas stood in the gallery for a long time with the light turning above him. Dunmoss was a name only the surveyors used for a stretch of drowned marsh that would not become a village in another decade, if it would at all. He checked the parish registers the next morning. No Clara Wren. No Wren, indeed, in three counties. He put the letter in his shirt pocket and went down the coast to ask the postmaster, who only shook his head.

He pinned the first letter to the wall of the gallery and read it when the weather turned. It was not a joke, he told himself. The paper smelled faintly of the marsh.

The second letter came in the spring of 1904, from the same hand. Dunmoss, 14 June 1908. "I am learning my letters. The master's name is Mr. Hale and he smells of chalk. My father hums in the kitchen a song about a boat. I have seen your light once, from the boat, and it made the whole sea look like a thing that could be held."

Silas wrote back. He did not know why. He had not received a letter since his wife Maura died, and he was old enough to have stopped being frightened by strange things. "Dear Clara, I am the keeper at the end of the black headland. I do not know how to answer a girl who is not yet born, but I will try. The east window of the gallery is still unpainted. I have been meaning to paint it."

He posted it to an address that did not exist, through a post box that took anything and returned nothing. In the summer of 1905 another letter came from the marsh, marked 1909. In the winter of 1907 a third, marked 1912: "I am married. My husband's name is Fionn. He is a sailor, and he sings badly, and I have begun to love it."

Year after year he wrote to her. He wrote about the weather and the ships, about the gulls that had taken to nesting in the gallery, about small things. The letters came through the years — one in 1908, one in 1910, one in 1914 — each arriving in the present with a date in the future, the girl growing on the page, the room still yellow, the window still one. In the winter of 1913 a spark caught the gallery and went out before it started; the next letter said she had dreamed of the fire, and the dream had been the same fire. He kept thinking about it. He kept writing.

In the autumn of 1916 he wrote, "Do you believe the letters come from the future?"

The answer came a year later, in a smaller hand: "I believe the letters come from the person. The date is only a formality. — C."

Something in him loosened. He began to live not only toward the present but toward the future, a strange habit for a man whose life had been reduced to keeping a light. In the autumn of 1908 he painted the east window of the gallery lead white, though his letter had only said that he had been meaning to paint it. He planted potatoes in the little plot below. He left a candle in the east window on clear nights, the way one leaves things for someone who has not yet come.

For the first time since Maura's death, Silas had a future worth tending.

In the summer of 1918 the letters stopped. The world had turned to war, and the last letter from the marsh was thin and plain: "There is a war in the world. I will write again when there is something safe to write."

Then, in the winter of 1919: "Fionn is gone. A shell, at the coast. I am alone, and there is a child on the way, and I am going to call her Elowen, and I am coming to you in the autumn. My mother says I should not, but my mother is the one who kept your letters."

The autumn came. A woman came up the stair with a child on her hip, about a year old, asleep against her shoulder. She was tired, and her eyes were the grey-green of the sea in March.

"Clara Wren," Silas said at the door.

"You painted the east window," she said. "Your letter said you would. I was nine when my mother first read you to me. I have read them all."

He led her in. The gallery was cold, and he set the candle in the east window, and the child woke and reached for it, and Clara took her hand. Silas took the tin from the bottom shelf — the first tin, from the gale, with the first letter inside — and set it between them. Clara turned it once over. "There are two of them," she said. "The one you kept, and the one I kept." He did not ask what that meant. He had stopped asking what the letters meant.

He did not know how to explain that he had written to a place that did not exist. He did not need to. On the table lay a small tin, blue wax, the impression of a wren. "Your letters came," she said. "All of them. I kept them. I read the years before I had them."

He sat with her, and with the child, who asked for the candle and was given it. He told her about the potatoes, and the gulls, and the years he had kept the light burning for a person who did not exist. She listened. Outside the lamp turned its slow circle, and the beam went out over the sea, and beyond the sea was a marsh, and on the marsh a house, and in the house a room, yellow, with one window looking out on the water.

In the spring of 1921 the doctor came and stayed three days, and went, and said the winter would be hard. A heart that has been turning for twenty-two years wears thin on the turning. Silas wrote a letter that was not to Clara, and was. He set it on the desk in the gallery.

"Dear Clara. The winter will be hard for me, and I have stopped pretending. I shall not see Elowen's wedding, or the autumn after that. I am writing this from the desk in the gallery, and the east window is white, and the sea is flat, and the light is turning. I kept it for you — all of it, all the years I did not know I was waiting for.
— The Keeper"

He died on a clear night in January, with the light turning above him. Clara held Elowen's hand at the foot of the stair. In the morning the child drew a yellow square with a window in the log book, and the beam went on.

The light was staffed until 1951. The young keeper was new, and he kept the lamp by the book, and the house had not yet learned the sound of one breathing. In the spring of that year a tin came up on the shingle at the foot of the stair, waxed shut, sealed in blue with the impression of a bird.

Inside was a single sheet, folded into a square.

"Dear stranger, I am writing from the marsh at Dunmoss. The house is new and the room is yellow, with one window that looks out onto the water. I am not yet born, but I know the shape of the room. My name is Clara."

The young keeper folded the letter and put it in the drawer with the others. Then he went up the stair and lit the lamp, and the beam swept the dark water, and the light went on.
