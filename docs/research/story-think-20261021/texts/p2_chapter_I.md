# Chapter One

The mountain never forgave a train.

That was what old Pete Halloway, the engineer who had taken Frank under his wing at the depot in 1962, used to say. "Every cut has a price, Frank. Every grade has a claim. And the mountain keeps the books." Frank had listened to the man for thirty years. He memorized every switch, every mile marker, every place where the black mountain folded itself around the valley like a fist. Then he retired. The line was sold. The tracks rusted. And the mountain kept the books for both of them.

Now Frank sat on the porch of the house he had bought the year the depot closed, watching the fog peel away from the ridge in long, wet ribbons. He was sixty-eight. His knees ached when the weather turned, which was most of the year. He drank coffee too black, smoked too little, and did nothing that would make anyone notice him. It was the only job he had ever done well: being useful and invisible.

He kept the porch lamp on, not for the light but for the habit. Pete used to say a conductor's real job was to keep the light burning so the next person on the mountain knew the track was still there. Frank had kept the light burning for thirty years, ever since the last passenger train rolled down and the station went quiet. Some nights it flickered. Some nights it burned so steady he could count the seconds.

Copper Creek was the kind of town the maps had given up on. Twelve houses at the valley floor, a general store, a church with a steeple that had leaned since 1987. The only road in came over the pass, and the only road out went down the cut. Most people lived here to get out of the mountain, or to hide in it. Frank had chosen neither. He had simply stopped.

The neighbors had stopped asking his name. They knew his habits. He knew their habits. That was the arrangement; each kept the other's business, and each kept the other's silence.

By day, Frank mended the fence and split wood and sat in his chair by the stove, listening. He had learned to read the silence of the valley the way he had read the rails. A hawk that called too often meant a storm. A creek that ran high meant the snow was moving. And the silence itself, the deep, settled silence of the cut, was the loudest thing in the world. He had counted every mile of it, and every mile of it had been empty.

The phone rang at 6:47.

He let it ring nine times. Ten. The tenth ring was a habit Pete had drilled into him. Nine was for the mailman. Ten was for something that mattered.

"Mercer," he said.

"Frank, it's Ruiz. I don't want you to come to the station. It's the cut."

The cut was three miles past the old Kettle Junction, where the line had been severed in 2009 and the ties sawn into fence posts. A dead-end rail that still bit through the mountain like a needle.

"Where," Frank said.

"I'm on my way. You'll need a light. The sun's not up yet."

The pickup was waiting at the end of the gravel road, its headlights still on. Ruiz had the window down. The air was sharp enough to taste, and the smell of the mountain after rain, wet stone, pine resin, cold iron. Frank walked ahead of the pickup up the track, his hand resting on the steel. He did not touch the rail. That was a rule. But his hand followed it, the way it had followed ten thousand miles of steel before.

Ten thousand miles. That was the number Pete had once written on the back of a timetable. Frank had never forgotten it. Every mile was a decision, and every decision was a price. The mountain had collected. He had paid.

Pete had taught him to read the rail the way other men read weather. A tight joint meant a heavy load coming. A loose one meant the mountain was shifting. A rail that sang in the wind at the wrong pitch meant there was water in the sleep, and water in the sleep meant a washout on the far side of the cut. Frank had listened to the steel for thirty years, and the steel had never once lied to him.

When he reached the tape, Ruiz was there, already crouched, his flashlight shaking in his hand.

"Frank, don't touch anything," Ruiz said, without looking up.

"I haven't," Frank said. "I'm just looking."

That was a lie, and they both knew it.

Ruiz had set up two tape lines. Frank noticed that before he noticed the body. Tape across the rail. Not tape around anything. The rookie move, or a man covering a mistake.

The moon had come up behind the ridge while they were walking, and the cut lay pale under it. The ballast glinted like crushed bone. Below them, the valley lay wrapped in fog, and the only sound in the cut was the tick of the cooling rails.

Frank stopped.

The woman was on the track.

She lay face-down in the ballast, on the north rail. Her coat was dark, the kind of thing you bought at the department store in the valley town. Her hair was auburn, long. One of her boots had come off.

The way she lay, it was wrong.

He knelt, and Ruiz backed off. "You good?"

"Look at her boots," Frank said.

"One's gone."

"No. Look at the rail she's on. She's on the north rail. The south rail is four inches clear of her shoulder. If someone rolled her down from the embankment, the body would have landed on the south rail first. The ballast here is packed tight. Nothing slides on the south side. This woman was set here."

"Set?"

"Placed. Deliberately. And the boot." Frank picked up the missing boot with two fingers. "See the tread? Worn even on the insole, on the arch. Left boot, worn on the inside. She walks left-footed. So she took it off herself, not someone."

Ruiz wrote it down. Frank watched him and thought: write it all down. They'll throw it out.

The body was on its side, but Frank noticed the head. The face was turned toward the south rail. A dead body in this cold shifts with the wind and the settling, but this face had not shifted. It had been turned. Placed to be seen.

He reached up. Tucked into the lapel of the woman's coat, pressed flat against the collar, was a pin. A small brass pin in the shape of a whistle. A conductor's pin. Old style, the kind the company had issued in the seventies.

Frank's hand went still.

He took off his glove and pulled the pin free. He turned it over in his thumb. The engraving was worn. The maker's mark was still there. The pin he had worn on his chest for twenty years, the one he had taken off the night he walked away, was in this dead woman's coat.

For thirty years it had sat at the bottom of a drawer, wrapped in cloth, a small brass weight he could not throw away.

Ruiz saw his face. "Frank?"

"I knew that whistle," Frank said. "I heard it on the Kettle line. 1974. The man who wore it died in the accident."

"What accident?"

"The one that closed my career."

The wind came through the cut, and the rails rang with it.

Frank crouched over the body. He was not a doctor, but he had seen enough to know the difference between a body that had been killed and a body that had been killed in a place. He pressed his thumb to the woman's neck. No pulse. No warmth. The cold had taken the color from her skin and set it in stone. She had been dead a long time. Days, maybe.

1974. The Kettle line. The storm that came off the ridge like a living thing. The train that had never been stopped. Frank had been on the next one, or the last one; he had never told anyone which, and he could not remember why that distinction mattered. He had seen the whistle light in the dark. He had seen the man with the pin, standing on the footplate, looking up the mountain as if the mountain owed him an answer. And Frank had kept walking. In his memory, the whistle had sounded only once, but it had gone on ringing for thirty years. He had never stopped counting it.

The mountain kept the books. And the page for 1974 was the one he could not read.

The deputy's radio crackled. "Dispatch, unit two."

"Copy. What's the status?"

"Body's still at the cut. We need a medical examiner. We need—"

"Copy."

Frank stood. He looked at the pin in his hand. He looked at the body. He looked at the mountain, which was still there, keeping the books.

He put the pin back in his coat.

"You know," Ruiz said, "the thing that bothers me. She was wearing a watch. And the watch is still running."

Frank looked. The watch was an old thing, a gold thing, the kind of thing that stops when it dies. It was still ticking.

"That's not possible," Frank said.

"It's a mechanical watch. It runs as long as it's wound."

"Wound. When?"

"I don't know."

Frank picked up the watch. It was warm. Not cold. Not dead. Warm, like something that had been held. Like someone had wound it in the last few minutes.

He looked up. "Call the medical examiner. And call the lab. And bag that pin. Don't touch it."

"I did."

"I saw you put it back in my hand."

Ruiz frowned. "You took it out."

"I know."

The mountain held the books. And Frank Mercer, retired conductor, had just been handed a page that did not match.

The pin was his. His own pin, taken from his coat in the night of a choice he had never spoken of. And the woman on the track was wearing it like a badge.

When they found her, the name had already been spoken. Not here. In the valley. At the store, over coffee and the kind of quiet that only comes to small towns. The name had been spoken, and Frank had heard it. He had heard it the way you hear a train coming: in the bones before the ears.

The name was Lila Voss. The name of the woman who had been on the last train. The name of the woman who had never come back.

He looked down at the body one more time.

It was not Lila.

Ruiz stood behind him, breathing hard, his face a pale mask in the first light. "What do we do, Frank?"

"We do what we always did. We count the miles, and we listen for the next one."

The sun came up over the ridge. The fog broke. And in the cut, where the old line ended, the mountain kept its silence, and the tracks rang like a bell that no one had heard in thirty years.

In the valley, at the mouth of the road, a car sat parked at the edge of the pass with its headlights on. Frank had seen it. Or he thought he had.

Somewhere behind the ridge, a whistle sounded. Low, slow, and patient, like a man taking his time.

The last train from Copper Creek was already on its way.
