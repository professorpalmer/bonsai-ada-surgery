## final text

**The Keeper’s Last Light**

The storm took the road to Greysand before it took the rest of the world.

Iris saw the first sign of it from the bus window, a wall of gray rolling over the headland as though the sea had grown tired of being only water. The driver slowed to a crawl. On the cliff above Greysand, the lighthouse beam swept east, west, east, west, and for one breathless second it caught the bus window and turned Iris’s face white.

Then the rain began in earnest.

She had not been back to Greysand in ten years. She had not expected to come at all. Her mother’s solicitor had sent a box with the contents of the house: a broken pocket watch, a jar of dried lavender, three water-stained novels, a brass key with no label, and a letter written in a hand so faded it seemed to be trying to forget its own letters.

*Iris,* it had said. *If you can read this, you know better than I do what this town is to you. I have left the key to the cottage. The lighthouse belongs to no one now, not really, but the house does. Come, if you can.*

Iris had opened the envelope in a city office where the air smelled of paper and cold coffee. Outside her window, traffic moved in thin silver lines. She had folded the letter into the pocket of her coat and walked home, because some things must be read slowly.

By the time the bus reached the village, the sea had swollen to a height that made the harbor wall look like a child’s table. The street was empty except for a few dogs under shop awnings and a single lamp that burned with a stubborn orange light. The bus dropped her at the foot of the cliff road, but the road itself had vanished behind a curtain of spray.

“I’m sorry,” the driver said. “There’s no way across until the rain lets up.”

Iris climbed down. She was thirty-two, lean, with the careful posture of someone accustomed to archiving other people’s lives. She carried a cloth bag and an umbrella that opened inside itself and refused to be useful. The wind pulled at her coat.

“Where are you going?” someone asked.

She turned. A man stood in the doorway of the chandler’s shop, a red raincoat dripping from his shoulders. He was younger than she expected, with salt-bright eyes and a face still soft at the jaw.

“To my mother’s house,” Iris said. “Is it still there?”

“House and all,” he said. “Road may be another story. Name?”

“Iris Wren.”

The man went very still.

“Jonah’s sister,” he said.

She nodded.

He did not step back from the door. He simply looked at her with a kind of startled respect, as if she had been a name he had been waiting to hear.

“I’m Soren,” he said. “I live in the old station. If the road stays out, I can walk you by the cliff path. Not the best way in the rain.”

“Thank you.”

They walked.

The cliff path was narrow and slick with seaweed. Greysand lay below them like a town that had been washed over and not quite returned. The houses were small and pale, set close together against the dark slope. Smoke rose from a few chimneys, thin and uncertain. The harbor lights twinkled in the rain.

Soren talked at first only to keep the silence from growing teeth. He was a lighthouse technician, he said, one of the new ones who came up every few weeks to check the automated lights. The old lighthouse had no keeper for years now. The village had learned to live with the machine.

“But you know it’s not the same,” he said.

“I know,” Iris said.

“It’s a strange thing,” he went on, “to have a light that knows nothing. It turns because it must. It doesn’t care whether the ship is lost or found. It just goes on.”

Iris did not answer.

The cottage appeared at the end of a lane of black stones. It was smaller than she remembered, the roof bowed slightly as if it had been leaning toward the sea for a long time. The door was unlocked. Soren stopped at the threshold.

“You should be careful inside,” he said. “Old place. Pipes burst in storms. And the lighthouse steps—”

“I know the steps.”

He looked at her for a moment, then nodded.

“I’ll be at the station if you need anything. If the road comes back, I can bring you supplies.”

Iris said, “Thank you.”

When she was inside, she stood in the doorway for a long time. The house smelled of dust, dried flowers, and salt. A curtain moved in the hall, though no window was open. In the kitchen, a kettle sat on the stove as if someone had only just gone out.

She set her bag on the table and took the brass key from her pocket. It was warm from her hand. The teeth were worn smooth. For a moment she thought she had seen a door somewhere, but the cottage had only the usual doors: kitchen, bedroom, the small back room where her mother kept her sewing.

She went upstairs.

The room smelled of lavender, though the jar in the box had not been opened yet. Her mother’s bed was made with military precision. On the desk, a letter lay open beneath a glass paperweight. Iris crossed to it.

The paper was thin and blue, and the ink had faded to the color of weak tea.

*Iris,* it began again. *By the time you read this, I will be gone, and you will be angry, because you are always angry when I leave things unfinished. That is why I left them unfinished. So you would keep coming back.*

She sat down. Her fingers trembled only slightly.

The letter went on to describe the house, the garden, the sea, the lighthouse. It spoke of Jonah in the past tense, then corrected itself, as if the past tense were too final.

*He is not gone,* it said. *Not yet. The sea is a slow thief. It takes a body and then the name and then the face and then the way a person laughed in a particular light. I have tried to keep him in the house, in the books, in the meals I set for one. But a house cannot hold a person. A lighthouse can, if it is built right.*

Iris turned the page.

*There is a key in the box. It is not for the door. It is for the lamp room. Or rather, for the door behind the lamp room. I was a keeper once, long ago. I knew a man named Callum who kept the light before the machines came. He told me the light was not for the ships. It was for the dead. Not to bring them back. Only to let them be seen one more time.*

The rest of the letter was a warning, written quickly.

*Do not open the lamp room if you are not ready to say goodbye. Do not ask it to give him to you. Ask it to show him. There is a difference. One is a cage. The other is a window.*

Iris read it twice, then three times.

Below her, the sea crashed against the cliffs with such force that the floorboards shuddered. The lamp in the hall flickered. She closed the letter and went to the window.

Greysand lay in the rain, all dark stone and pale walls, and the lighthouse light swept the water in long white arcs. Every time it passed, the sea seemed to brighten, then darken again, as if the light were breathing.

She stood there until the rain thinned and the wind dropped to a moan.

Then she took the brass key from her pocket and went to the door.

The lamp room was at the top of the lighthouse, reached by a spiral stair that creaked like an old ship in a port. Iris had not been there in twenty years, not since she was a girl and her mother had carried her up on her shoulders to see the horizon. She remembered the smell of oil and hot metal, the vibration in her feet, the way the glass room had made the world seem both closer and farther away at once.

The automatic light was off. Or rather, it was on, but from inside the lamp room it looked only a faint blue point, as if the true light were somewhere else.

At the center of the room stood the great lens, a cage of glass and brass, cold and still. Around it, shelves held jars of oil, wicks, tools, and books. On the floor, near the base of the stair, there was a hatch, half hidden by a rug with frayed edges.

Iris knelt.

The brass key fit a small lock in the iron ring set into the hatch. It turned with a sound like a held breath released.

The lid swung open.

Stairs descended into a darkness so complete it seemed to have weight. Iris went down carefully, her hand on the wall. The air grew cooler and wetter, not with water but with something older, as if the house were holding its breath. After twelve steps, the stairs ended in a narrow room no larger than a closet.

In the center, on a stone pedestal, stood a lamp unlike the one above.

It was smaller, made of black glass and silver, with a bowl of oil so clear it looked like liquid air. Above the oil, a ring of fine glass tubes spiraled upward like a flower opening in reverse. There was no lens, no great machine to cast light across miles of sea. Only the lamp.

On the pedestal beside it lay a book bound in sea-green leather.

Iris opened it.

The first page was covered in a tight, slanting hand.

*If you have found this, you are the one Callum meant, or one like her. Do not be frightened. The lamp does not lie, but it does not comfort. It shows. It gathers. It keeps.*

The pages that followed were a record of years. Callum had been a keeper before the lighthouse was automated, before the village had begun to think of it as a relic. He wrote of ships, of storms, of people lost. But he also wrote of the room beneath the lamp, and of the thing the sea left behind.

*The sea does not only take bodies,* he wrote. *It takes the hours around them. The last conversation. The last laugh. The last time a mother called a child by name. Those things do not end. They sink. They gather in the dark places of the world, where light is thin and memory is thick.*

Iris turned the pages faster.

Callum had found the room by accident, he said, during a storm when the main light had failed. The lamp below the floor had begun to glow on its own, and the glass tubes had filled with a pale blue light. In the light, he had seen faces. Not ghosts, he insisted. Faces of the lost, preserved in the hours before they were gone.

*The lamp can be lit by a willing keeper,* he wrote. *It does not bring the dead back. It gives them one hour in which to be seen. The keeper must choose what to ask. The question is the oil. The answer is the flame.*

There were warnings, too.

*Do not ask for them to stay. They cannot stay. They are made of time, and time will pass through them. Do not ask the lamp to keep a name forever. Names are not stones. They are rivers.*

Iris reached the last pages. The handwriting changed. It was smaller, more careful, as if the pen had been held with great effort.

*The last one I saved was Jonah Wren. I am sorry. I should have told you sooner, but I was afraid. Afraid you would come here and not leave. Afraid the lamp would make you love him more than I did. It does that to keepers. They fall in love with the light, and the light falls in love with them.*

A photograph was tucked into the final page.

Jonah.

He was twenty-four in the picture, standing on a deck with the sea behind him, grinning at something off-camera. His hair was wet, his cheeks flushed, and he held up a bottle of wine as if he were about to drink it and not yet had. Iris looked at the photo until her vision blurred.

Below the photograph was a single line.

*His last hour is in the lamp. Light it if you can bear to.*

The room was silent except for the distant sound of the sea. Iris stood very still. Her hand was on the edge of the pedestal. The black glass of the lamp reflected her face, pale and uncertain.

She thought of Jonah on the Meridian, the fishing trawler that had vanished ten years ago in a storm almost like this one. She thought of the harbor meeting where the fishermen had stood in the rain and not looked at each other. She thought of her mother’s face when the body was never found. She thought of herself, growing older in a city where no one knew her name, where she worked in quiet rooms and handled other people’s papers, as if she were trying to become a document.

She thought of the lamp.

To light it, she had to ask a question.

The question had to be an oil.

Iris placed her hand over the bowl of oil. The glass was cold, but not dead cold. It seemed to pulse, faintly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

“Where are you?” she whispered.

The oil did not move.

“Jonah,” she said. “Where are you?”

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the glass tubes above the oil began to fill with light. It started at the bottom, a thin blue line, and climbed, climbing, until the whole lamp was bright. The room filled with a soft blue glow. The air changed. It became harder, clearer, as if the rain outside had been replaced by the sound of a ship’s hull in water.

Iris looked into the lamp.

At first she saw only mist.

Then the mist gathered.

It formed a shape.

It became a man.

He stood in the blue light, not quite solid, more like a painting seen through water. He was younger than she remembered, but not much. His clothes were soaked and dark, and there was a cut above his brow that had not yet stopped bleeding. His eyes were wide, and when he looked at her, she felt the whole room tilt.

“Iris,” he said.

His voice was the voice she had dreamed about for ten years, but it was not quite right. It was thinner, as if it had been worn smooth by the sea.

She did not speak. She could not.

“You came,” he said.

“How?” she asked.

“I’ve been waiting,” he said. “Not waiting like you think. Waiting like light waits. I don’t know what time is here. I don’t know where I am. I only know the lamp. I only know that when it lights, you might come.”

Iris stepped closer. “Are you alive?”

Jonah shook his head.

“No. I’m not. I’m the hour before.”

The words struck her like cold water.

“The hour before what?” she asked.

He looked at his hands. They were translucent at the edges. “The hour before I went under. That’s all I am. The last time. The last light.”

Iris wanted to reach out, but she was afraid that if she touched him, he would dissolve. Instead she held her hand in front of her, trembling.

“Tell me,” she said. “Tell me what happened.”

Jonah looked at the lamp, then at her. “The Meridian went down at two in the morning. I was in the hold. We thought the engine had failed. Then the sea came in through the porthole. I don’t remember much after that. I remember water. I remember the sound. I remember calling your name.”

“Calling my name?”

He nodded. “I was scared. I was always scared when I was far from you. You didn’t know that. You thought I was brave because I never told you.”

Iris felt a pain so sharp it was almost pleasant. “I thought you were brave because you were brave.”

He smiled, and the smile was so like the one in the photograph that she had to look away. “I was terrified. But I wasn’t sorry.”

“What?”

“I’m not sorry I went out. I’m not sorry I loved the sea. I’m just sorry you didn’t know I was afraid of losing you.”

The lamp flickered.

For a second, the room was dark. Iris gasped. When the light returned, Jonah was still there, but he seemed thinner.

“The lamp is weakening,” he said.

“Why?”

“Because I’m leaving.”

“No,” she said. “Not yet. Stay.”

He shook his head. “I can’t. I’m only an hour. If I stay, you’ll stop living. I’ve seen it in the others. The keepers who couldn’t let go. They spent their whole lives here, in the light, waiting for one more face.”

“Callum?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“Because I was afraid. If I had told you I was here, you would have come for me. Not to see me. To keep me. And I couldn’t let you do that.”

Iris pressed her hand to the edge of the pedestal. “Callum said the lamp can be lit with a question. I asked where you are. You’re here.”

“In a way.”

“That’s not the same as alive.”

“No.”

The blue light was fading now. Jonah’s outline was less certain. The edges of his face were softening into mist.

Iris looked at him, and for the first time in ten years she did not see the boy who had gone out to sea. She saw the man he had been, the man she had only half known. She saw his fear, his love, his small and human courage.

“Jonah,” she said, “I’m sorry I never let you say goodbye.”

He looked at her with such tenderness that she almost broke. “I did say goodbye. I said it to the sea. I said it to you. I just couldn’t make you hear it.”

“I’m hearing it now.”

“Then let me go.”

“No.”

“Please, Iris.”

Her eyes were wet. “If I let you go, I’ll never see you again.”

“Yes.”

“Then I’ll forget you.”

“No,” he said. “You’ll remember me differently. You’ll remember me whole, not only as the one who was lost. That’s what the lamp is for. Not to keep us. To finish us.”

The light was almost gone now. The glass tubes were dimming, one by one, like stars going out. Jonah was fading, but he was still smiling.

“I love you, sister,” he said. “I’m sorry I was afraid.”

“I love you, brother,” she said. “Be brave.”

He laughed, softly. “I was brave in the end. I’m brave now.”

The blue light vanished.

The room was dark again.

Iris stood alone in the silence. The lamp on the pedestal was cold, the oil still and clear. The glass tubes were empty. The photograph on the book had not disappeared, but it seemed less heavy, as if some weight had been lifted from it.

She sat on the floor and cried for a long time.

Not the sharp, helpless crying of ten years ago, but the slow, deep crying of someone who has finally been allowed to set something down. The sound of it filled the small room, and the sea outside answered it, over and over, as if the whole coast were breathing with her.

When at last she could stand, she went back to the book. She opened it to Callum’s final page and read the last entry again.

*The last keeper will not be a keeper at all. The last keeper will be the one who finally understands that light is not a cage. It is a door. Open it, close it, and walk.*

Iris closed the book. She took the photograph from between the pages and put it in her coat. She closed the hatch, locked it, and climbed the stairs.

The lamp room above was still cold and blue. The great automatic light had begun to turn again, casting long white arcs across the water. For a moment, Iris thought she saw a ship out on the horizon, small and dark, passing safely beneath the beam.

She looked at the lens. “Thank you,” she whispered to no one.

Then she went down.

The road was still gone when Soren came to look for her. He found her sitting on the stone steps at the bottom of the cliff, her umbrella broken across her lap, the brass key in her hand. The rain had stopped. The sky was the pale gray of early morning.

“You look like you’ve been to war,” he said.

“I’ve been to a lighthouse,” she said.

He sat down beside her. For a while, neither of them spoke.

Finally she said, “I found something.”

“What?”

“A lamp. A room. A name.” She held up the brass key. “It opens a door.”

Soren looked at the key. “Which door?”

Iris smiled, and it was the first real smile he had seen on her face.

“The one I didn’t know was locked,” she said.

In the morning, the road came back. The storm had torn up the lower lane, but the cliff path was passable. Soren drove her into the village in his small service car. The harbor was calm, the boats bobbing gently, the water so clear it seemed almost blue beneath the gray sky.

Iris went to her mother’s house and began to empty it.

It took her three days. She gave the books away. She put the dried lavender in the garden. She carried the broken pocket watch to the shop and paid a man to repair it, though he told her it would not keep time for long. She did not mind. Some things are not meant to be fixed; only carried a little longer.

On the fourth day, she went to the lighthouse one last time.

The lamp room was empty. The black lamp was gone from the small room below. In its place, Callum’s journal lay on the pedestal, closed. Iris opened it and took out the last few pages. They were not a record. They were a list of names.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she tore them out and fed them into the small stove in the cottage. They curled and blackened and turned to ash, and she watched the ash drift up the chimney like tiny gray birds.

When she was done, she walked to the cliff’s edge.

Greysand lay below her, white and quiet. The sea stretched out to the horizon, endless and bright. Somewhere out there, a ship was passing. She did not know where it was going, and for the first time in ten years she did not want to know.

Iris put her hand in her pocket and touched the photograph.

Jonah’s face was still there, but it was no longer a wound. It was a window.

She turned from the cliff and walked back to town.

The lighthouse light came on that night, and the beam swept the water. It did not stop. It did not call. It simply went on, doing its work.

And in the quiet village below, Iris Wren went home, opened her door, and let the dark in.
