# The Light at the End of the Letters

The first envelope arrived on a Tuesday in November, which was nothing like an event. The second came three weeks later. The third, on the day the fog rolled in thick enough to swallow the point whole.

Each was postmarked from a town called Kettering, in a state the keeper did not know, and addressed in careful blue ink to: *Elias Voss, Keeper, Maren's Head Light — the one that is still on.*

He knew every vessel that reached the island. The supply barge came on the first Thursday of each month, and before that, nothing but gulls and the occasional fishing trawler that lost its way. No letter-carrier. No one. And yet, each morning, a sealed envelope sat in the mail slot carved into the base of the tower, the slot the mainland clerk had cut in 1893 for correspondence that had, until now, never arrived.

The first letter was short.

*Dear Mr. Voss,*

*I'm six years old today. I don't know why I'm writing to you. I just woke up and there was a note under my door that said: Write to the keeper at the end of the world. I don't know who you are. I don't know why I'm supposed to. But I'm going to try.*

*My name is— well. I think it's Mara. Or Mara is coming. I don't know yet. I just know that when I close my eyes I see a light. Not a lighthouse exactly. Just a light. The color of a held breath.*

*I hope you're okay. The fog is very thick here too.*

*— A person who doesn't exist yet*

Elias read it three times. He set it on the table beside the lamp's maintenance log, which was mostly a ledger of wicks, paraffin, and the date of each rotation. Then he stood at the window and looked out at the grey nothing of the North Channel. The fog was not out there. He had checked. There was no fog. He turned the lamp anyway, out of habit, and watched the beam carve its long pale arc across the water.

He put the letter in his shirt pocket. It was there the next morning, and the one after. It was there for eleven days.

The second letter arrived without a preceding one. It described a schoolyard in October, rain on the pavement, a girl named Sable who sat in the front row. The writer signed with two initials, *M. K.*, and closed with a line that made Elias stop at the top of the stairs: *I am nine now, I think. I think time is the wrong word. I think it's more like the tide.*

The third described a summer at the sea, not this sea, a different one, warm and shallow. The writer was eleven, learning to ride a bicycle, terrified of the brakes. The fourth described a winter in Kettering, snow on the porch, a dog named Biscuit that stole socks.

Each letter was dated. The dates were all in the future. They stretched forward through the keeper's remaining years and well beyond, as though someone had already written the entire book and was handing him the pages one at a time, out of order, in a wind.

Elias began to check the town records. Kettering did not exist on any map he owned. The state name was a string of consonants he could not pronounce. When he tried to trace a route to the postmark, his pencil stopped at the edge of the page and came away clean, as though the paper had no further country in it.

He stopped checking.

He kept the letters in a tin box beside his bed. He read them at night, after the lamp rotation, when the beam had done its long circle and the sea was flat and black and the tower groaned in the wind. They did not leave. But sometimes, in the morning, one of them had a faint warmth to it, as though something had touched it and moved on.
