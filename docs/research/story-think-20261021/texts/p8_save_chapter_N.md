# Chapter One: The Hush Line

The old turtle coughed at dawn, and Vellum shivered.

Sela felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it — a shudder that began deep in the stone floor and climbed up through her boots, up her shins, and into her teeth. She gripped the window ledge and watched a rain gutter spatter the cobbles below. Not rain. Gravy, or something like it: a thin dark water that smelled of salt and of deep, deep earth.

That was the first sign.

Below the rim of the city — that sheer edge where the great carapace curves down and away into the mist — the gutter ran, and the street was still. Vellum was built on the back of the world, a city of white stone and copper wire set into the grooves between the shell plates, its towers rising from the ridges of the carapace like barnacles on an old ship. Below it, forever, there was only mist: an ocean of cloud, the sea of the world, with no floor that anyone had ever found.

Sela was a shell singer. Most people called it a title. The elders called it a debt. Every morning at the edge of the seam she knelt, laid a palm flat on the shell, and listened. The old turtle — the elders called him Oren, which meant *the one who is*, and everyone in Vellum knew the name was a prayer, not a description — was the largest living thing under the sky. He had been old when the first tower rose. He would be old when the last one fell.

Sela pressed her ear to the plate and breathed.

The shell hummed. That was the thing everyone in Vellum took for granted: the hum. Low and steady, the sound of a vast body turning in a vast dark water. Beneath the hum came the breath, every few minutes — the long draw that lifted the whole city, and the slow let-down that settled it back. The mist below rose and fell with the breathing, and the fog in the lower streets swirled and cleared like vapor over a lake.

This morning the hum was right. Almost.

That was the problem with *almost*. Sela had listened for eleven years, and she had learned the difference between the way a plate rang and the way a bone rang. She took a tuning fork from her belt — a sliver of shed plate, cut from the edge last winter, worn until it held a note for a week — and struck it against her boot. The fork sang once and began to fade. She pressed both tines to the carapace, between the ridges.

A hush line.

Every singer knew what a hush line was. The plates grew in rings, like the rings of an old tree, and where the rings met there ran thin pale seams — the hush lines, the places where sound crossed. If a line rang clean, the turtle was well. If the sound dropped, like a voice in a long hall, something had changed under the stone.

The sound dropped.

Sela drew her hand back as if the plate had burned. She stayed on her knees a long moment, breathing in the salt-sweet smell of deep water. Somewhere in the city a door opened. Somewhere a kettle was set on the fire. The city was waking, and none of its thirty thousand people knew that the thing beneath their feet had just said something no one had heard in a hundred years.

The thing the plate said was this: it was tired.

Not the deep, ageless weariness she was used to. Not the slow tiredness of a body that had turned the same circles in the same dark for centuries. This was a new tiredness. A tiredness with an edge to it. It climbed up from the far side of the city, from where the Council's spire stood — the oldest tower on the old turtle — and it climbed toward the seam where the carapace began to curl down into the mist.

She checked the hush lines she had marked in her book since she came to the city — a hundred and twelve, in small careful strokes. She had counted them every morning for eleven years. The number had not changed.

Not anymore.

The new one ran from the Council spire to the rim of the city, pale as a bone and thin as a whisper, and it was growing. She watched it — she would have sworn the shell was warm under her touch — and she watched the pale line creep another finger's width toward the edge.

"Sela."

She turned. Master Iven stood in the door of the song house, his robe the color of the mist and his face the color of it, too. He never crossed into the street. To a singer the street was a promise that the shell was solid, and Iven had no faith in promises.

"I heard it," he said.

"You heard it," Sela said, and rose.

"Then you know the Council meets at the bell."

"I know."

He paused in the doorway. "There are old ones in this city. Older than me. And there is one thing the old ones know that I have never told anyone."

Sela waited.

"They are not afraid of the shell breaking," Iven said. "They are afraid of what is under the mist."

The morning sun climbed. Vellum brightened, copper and white, a city of birds and bells and the slow turn of the world. And far below, in the mist where no light had ever reached, something vast shifted — and for a moment, Sela would swear it was the shell under her palm that turned with it.

The old turtle was listening, too.
