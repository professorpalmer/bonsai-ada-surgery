# Chapter One: The Ground Is a Lie

The first thing every child learns in Verath is that the ground is a lie.

It moves. Not like a ship at sea, but slower than that, more patient than that — like the world breathing while you sleep on top of it. When the giant yawns, the whole city tilts three degrees and every cup of wine in the tavern spills at once. When it stretches — which happens only once a year, in the Month of Long Shadows — the city rolls forward for three days straight, and the horizon is redrawn.

After a stretch, the new horizon arrives. The new horizon is the new truth: ships are re-anchored, post roads are relaid, and the High Spire writes the new map and nails it to the wall of the market square. Every citizen of Verath sees three horizons in a lifetime. It is considered a sort of wealth.

The city's name is Verath. It is built on the back of a turtle so vast that no living soul has ever seen its head.

Below the city, in the depths no ship has ever mapped, the old ones call the creature the Unnamed. The scholars of the High Spire write *Leviathan of the Deep* in their ledgers. The fishmongers of the Salt Walk call it *her*, because once a year, on market morning, its great flat neck turns toward the sunrise, and no one who has ever watched the motion can describe it without reaching for a word that means *she*.

Verath, the old maps say, was built three hundred years ago, on a day when the Unnamed lay at rest. The founders were sailors — the last of a fleet that had sailed beyond the last charted coast and found, instead of water, a back. Warm. Steady. Breathing.

Maren Vael was not supposed to be at the edge at dawn. She was supposed to be in her aunt's shop counting shells, or in the lecture hall with chalk on her sleeves, listening to Master Ildren argue that the Unnamed was a machine and not a beast. But Maren was born with the edge-itch — that feeling, like a hook behind the sternum, that the rim of the world was trying to call her name.

So here she was, perched on a rusted rung of the Rim Bell's tower, the wind ripping the grey of her cloak from her back. Below her, Verath spread out like a patchwork quilt stitched onto a hillside: ten thousand roofs in seven colors, the pale spire of the High Spire, the black water of the Tidepool at the city's center where the shell's oldest scar had healed. And then the edge.

The edge of the shell.

The rim rose twenty feet above the streets and curved over like the lip of a bowl. Beyond it: nothing. Not sea, not land. Only a pale blue distance going down, and down, and down, and the horizon arriving at an angle, as if the whole world were tilted on its side. The people who lived closest to the edge — the Tidefolk — had a saying: *The Unnamed does not carry us anywhere. We are where it walks.*

Maren swung her legs over the rim and dangled them above the blue nothing. Beneath the rim, the air moved in long, slow waves — the Unnamed's breath, the Tidefolk called it — though it smelled of rain on stone, of iron, of something very far below. The hook behind her sternum tugged.

Something was wrong today.

The Tidepool at the city's center had gone still an hour before dawn — still as glass, as if the thing beneath it had held its breath.

The Unnamed had not stretched this year. It had not yawned. And yet, beneath the rumble of her boots on the bell tower, there was a pulse — a slow, deep, almost musical thump coming from below the shell, from a depth no sound should reach.

Thump. Pause. Thump.

It was not a heartbeat. The scholars had nailed that to every lecture board in the High Spire: a creature that size would need a heart the size of a house. What pulsed beneath Verath was not a heart. No one had ever named it. The old Tidefolk said it was the creature dreaming. The scholars said it was machinery.

Maren said it was calling.

She climbed back over the rim into the tower and began to ring the Rim Bell. The bell was older than the spire, older than the city. Its bronze tongue was cracked, so it sang in two notes at once. The Tidefolk said the bell was tuned to the Unnamed's voice. They said that if you rang it at the right hour, the creature would turn its face toward the sound and let you see.

No one had ever seen.

Maren struck the bell. The note hung in the air like a second sunrise, and the city held its breath. For a moment, all of Verath — fishmongers, scholars, bakers, chalk-dusted children — felt the same thing: the ground beneath them shifting, settling, leaning.

And then, far below, beneath the stone and the foundations and the oldest roots of the city, something answered.

The Unnamed exhaled.

The sky shuddered. And on the edge of Verath, Maren Vael felt the hook behind her sternum tighten like a fist.

She knew, the way she knew the taste of her own name, that something had come up out of the deep. Something that had been sleeping longer than the city. And it had found her voice.

She was certain of it. Because the pulse had changed — the slow, patient thump had turned quick, eager, climbing the depths like something learning to run. And somewhere below the shell, in a place no light had ever reached, something was coming up.
