Chapter One: The Silent Track

The mountain had a voice, but it had been talking to Elias for forty years, and lately, it had become a lot louder. It spoke in the groan of the granite underfoot, the snap of a frozen pine branch, and the low, thrumming vibration that lived in the base of his skull. Elias Thorne, once the most reliable conductor on the Silver Ridge Line, had spent his life listening for those sounds. He knew which section of the mountain was shifting in the spring, and he knew when the wind was trying to tear a roof off a cabin in the valley. But today, the mountain wasn’t shifting. Today, it was holding its breath.

Elias stood on the porch of the old station house, his coat buttoned to the throat against the biting wind. The station had been abandoned for six years, since the day the last train had groaned to a halt on the tracks and the company had shuttered the books. Now, it was just a museum of rust and rot, the only place in the town of Blackwood where Elias still felt the phantom weight of a passenger’s hand on his shoulder. He took a sip of coffee from a thermos, the steam rising into the gray morning.

It was a small town, tucked away in a valley that felt like the bottom of a funnel. Blackwood was made of stone and secrets, and Elias was the only man who remembered the old map, the one that showed where the river used to run before the dam was built. He was seventy-two years old, and his knees ached with the damp. He had retired on his own terms, or so he thought. He had simply walked away from the job after the accident in 1994. He never spoke of it. He just walked away.

The sound of a siren cut through the stillness. It was a distant wail, but Elias knew exactly which siren it was. It came from the ridge, where the old mining town of Hallow’s Gate sat in the snow.

Elias finished his coffee and set the thermos on the windowsill. He looked at the clock on the wall, a brass thing that had stopped ticking twenty years ago. He didn't check the time; he knew what time it was. He walked down the creaking porch steps and started toward the ridge.

The path up to Hallow’s Gate was steep and narrow, choked with snow that had turned to ice overnight. Elias’s boots crunched against the frozen earth. He wasn't a fast man anymore, and his lungs were like bellows, but the mountain called to him. He had spent decades walking these paths, checking the rails, clearing the snow, and watching over the passengers. Now, he was just an old man walking a path he knew by heart.

When he reached the ridge, he saw the black SUV parked on the side of the road, its lights flashing a frantic red and blue. Three figures stood near the old stone cabin at the edge of the cliff. A police officer was checking his watch. A woman in a long red coat was looking into the tree line. And a young man, barely twenty, was leaning against the SUV with an air of bored impatience.

Elias recognized the young man. It was Silas, the son of the man who lived in that cabin.

He stopped at the edge of the clearing. The cabin was the old Blackwood Ranger’s House, a place of stone and timber that had stood for a century. It was a place of silence, where the only sound was the wind. And yet, today, the silence was broken by the flashing lights and the murmur of voices.

Elias pulled his collar up and pushed through the snow. He walked toward the group, his movements slow and deliberate.

"Mr. Thorne!" The officer, a tall, thin man in a uniform that looked too new for the mountains, turned to look at Elias. "Step back, please. We’re in the middle of an investigation."

"I’m not here for the investigation," Elias said, his voice quiet but clear. "I’m here because I know who’s in that cabin."

The officer frowned. "Who?"

"Arthur Penhaligon," Elias said. "He’s dead."

The officer’s eyes widened slightly. "We just found the body. It was twenty minutes ago. How did you know?"

Elias looked past the officer, toward the cabin. He didn't see Arthur Penhaligon’s body. He saw the arrangement of the world around it. He saw the way the snow had been disturbed, not by a footfall, but by the drag of a heavy object. He saw the way the branches of the pines near the window were bent inward, as if someone had been pushed out from inside. He saw the way the young man, Silas, was standing too far away, his hands in his pockets, his eyes on the cabin door rather than the police work.

"Because I’ve been walking these paths for forty years," Elias said. "I know the mountain. And I know what happens when someone dies in this valley."

The officer stepped forward, his hand going to his radio. "I don’t know what you’re implying, Mr. Thorne. Please step back."

Elias ignored him and walked toward the cabin. The red light from the police car washed over the stone walls, casting long, shifting shadows across the ground. He could smell the pine, the snow, and beneath that, something else. Something metallic and sharp, like copper coins.

He stopped at the cabin door. It was ajar, a crack of darkness visible inside. He peered through the gap.

He didn’t see a body. He saw a table. A simple wooden table with a glass of water on it. The glass was empty. Next to it, a book was open, its pages bent down as if a hand had just been resting on it.

And then he saw the rug.

It was a small rug, a pattern of dark red and black. It was pulled slightly to the left, the edge of the rug tucked under the leg of the table. But the floor beneath the table was covered in snow, a fresh, undisturbed layer that had been blown in from outside.

Elias’s breath caught in his chest.

It was a mistake. A small, almost invisible mistake.

He turned back to the officer, who was now standing right behind him.

"What do you see, Mr. Thorne?" the officer asked, his voice tight.

Elias didn't turn around. He just kept looking at the cabin.

"A mistake," Elias said. "Someone pushed the table against the door. But they forgot to push the rug with it."

The officer didn't understand. "The rug?"

"The rug is on the inside of the table. The floor outside the table is snow. If someone pushed the table from outside, the rug would have gone with it. It would be on the floor outside. But the rug is inside, and the snow is outside. Which means someone pushed the table from inside, after the snow had already come in."

The officer blinked. "That doesn't make sense."

"It makes perfect sense," Elias said. "It means the person who died was killed inside the cabin. The snow came in after the death. Which means the window was open. Or the door was open."

The officer looked at the door, then at the window. "The window is locked. The door is... the door is open, but there’s no sign of forced entry."

"Exactly," Elias said. "But the snow on the floor is only on one side of the table. It’s as if the table was a barrier. Someone stood on the other side of the table, in the clean area, and killed Arthur. Then they left. And then the wind blew in the snow, but it didn’t get under the table. Because the table was already in place."

The officer was quiet for a moment. "You’re saying Arthur was killed while sitting at that table?"

"I’m saying he was killed by someone who wanted him to stay at that table. Who wanted him to look at something." Elias’s eyes narrowed. "Who wanted him to see something before he died."

He turned to look at Silas, the young man by the car. Silas was watching Elias, his face pale and tight. He didn't say a word. He just looked at Elias with a mix of fear and recognition.

Elias looked at the cabin one last time. He could see the book on the table. He knew what kind of book Arthur Penhaligon read. He knew Arthur’s habits, his fears, his secrets. Elias had known Arthur for thirty years. They had worked on the same train. They had shared the same small cabin in the valley.

Elias turned to the officer.

"Who was with him last night?" Elias asked.

The officer shook his head. "We don't know yet. We’re still going through his things. His son is here. He says his father was alone."

Elias looked at Silas. The young man was still leaning against the car, his eyes fixed on Elias.

"He’s lying," Elias said.

The officer turned to Silas. "Young man, is that true? You were with your father last night?"

Silas didn't answer. He just stood there, his hands in his pockets, his face a mask of blankness.

Elias walked past the officer and up to Silas. He looked at the young man, really looked at him, the way he had looked at passengers for forty years. He looked at the way Silas held his shoulders, the tension in his jaw, the slight tremor in his hands. He looked at the snow on the car, which was undisturbed, but the snow on the ground around Silas’s boots, which was pressed down, as if someone had been standing there for a long time.

"You were there," Elias said quietly. "You were at the table."

Silas’s head snapped up. "I was nowhere near that cabin last night. I was in town. I was at the pub."

Elias smiled, a sad, thin smile. "You were in the pub, maybe. But you were there after. You came back after the snow had started to fall. You came back to see if he was still alive. And when you saw he was, you pushed the table."

Silas’s face changed. The blankness broke, and a flicker of fear, and something else, something like relief, crossed his eyes. He opened his mouth to speak, but the officer was there, his hand on Silas’s shoulder.

"Mr. Thorne," the officer said, his voice low. "I think you’ve done enough. I’d like to ask you a few more questions in the station house."

Elias looked at the cabin one last time. He saw the book on the table, the glass of water, the open window. He saw the mountain, the wind, the snow. He saw the mountain’s voice, and for the first time in forty years, he understood what it was saying.

It wasn't the wind. It was the whistle.

He turned and walked down the ridge, the sound of the snow crunching under his boots echoing in the silent valley. He didn't know who had done it, not really. But he knew who was next. And he knew that the mountain was watching.
