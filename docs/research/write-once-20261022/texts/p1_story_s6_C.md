## final text

# The Unwritten

The first letter arrived with the mail boat on a Thursday in November, 1985, the same week the wind turned to something the sea kept muttering about but never quite finished saying.

Aldous Wren was seventy-three years old and had kept the light at Greymouth Head since the lighthouse was automated in 1961, when the mainland decided no one needed a man on a rock. But the contract had been extended, then extended again, and he had stayed, not because he loved the island or the work, but because he had nowhere else to go and because his wife had died eleven years ago and he had not yet figured out what a life without a door that opened onto a kitchen looked like. He was still figuring it out. He had been figuring it out for eleven years.

The letter came in a plain brown envelope, postmarked nowhere. The return address was blank. The handwriting inside was not neat, but it was careful, the kind of care a person uses when they know the words will be read more than once.

*Dear Mr. Wren,*

*My name is Clara Haskett. I will be born on the fourth of March, 1989, in a small house in Bath, in the room where the wallpaper is the color of weak tea and my mother sings off-key when she thinks no one is listening. I am writing to you now, from the place I have not yet come from, because I know that in four years I will be a child and in twelve I will be a young woman and in twenty I will be older than I know how to be, and I will need you to be here. Not as a lighthouse keeper. Not as a man on a rock. But as the person who kept the light on for me before I knew what light meant.*

*I know this is strange. I know you will think it is a joke or a hallucination or a message from someone who has lost their mind. I am asking you not to throw this in the fire. I am asking you to keep it in the drawer beside the logbook. I am asking you to be ready.*

*Yours, in some order that is not yet fixed,*
*C.*

Aldous read it three times. The third time he set the paper down and looked at the sea through the salt-bitten window, and the sea was doing its ordinary business, and the light was turning its slow beam through the fog, and nothing was wrong, and everything had.

He put the letter in the drawer beside the logbook, as she had asked.

---

The second letter came in February. The third in April. They came roughly every six weeks, always with the mail boat, always on the same day of the week, always in that careful, un-neat hand. They grew longer and more specific. She wrote about the house in Bath, about a father who was kind in the way of men who do not know how to be kind, about a cat with one ear missing that she would meet in a garden in 1996. She wrote about the smell of rain on hot stone, about the way a particular frequency of gull cry made her chest ache even before she knew what aching was.

She never wrote about the future as a sequence. She wrote about it as a texture, a room she was still learning the shape of, and she described it to Aldous the way you describe a dream you are having while still in it.

*You are still keeping the log,* the fifth letter said, *which means you are still here, which means the light is still turning, and I am grateful for all three, though I am not sure I can explain why.*

Aldous began to look forward to the mail boat. He was a man who had stopped looking forward to things after Margaret died. He had stopped looking forward to breakfast. He had stopped looking forward to the next season. But the mail boat came on Thursdays, and now he set the kettle for Thursday with a particular care he could not name, and he left the letter in the drawer where it could warm itself against the metal of the pen and the old compass he kept from his father.

He told no one. There was no one. The mainland was a bus ride to the village, and the village had a pub and a post office and a woman who sold flowers in a tin pot out front, and Aldous had not been to the pub in three years. He told the postmistress, when she came to collect his pension, that he had received a letter from an old friend. She nodded, the way she nodded at everything, and put the penny in the little tin on the counter.

---

By 1989 Clara was not yet born, and the letters were still coming.

By 1991 she was a child in Bath, and the letters were still coming.

By 1995 the letters changed. They became shorter. They became more urgent. They stopped describing and started asking.

*Are you still here,* she wrote in the spring of 1997. *Are you still on the island. Is the light still turning. I need you to say yes in this letter. I need you to write the word yes. If the word yes is not in this drawer in 1997, I will not be able to find the way.*

Aldous wrote *Yes* in the corner of the letter he sent back. It was the only word. He sat at the table for an hour afterward, looking at the sea, and he felt, for the first time in a long time, that he was being waited for by something that had not yet arrived, and that the waiting was the thing, and that the arrival was the thing, and that he could not separate them.

---

The last letter arrived in the winter of 2003.

Clara Haskett was sixteen. She had left Bath. She had gone to Edinburgh, then to Lisbon, then to a place in the north of France that she did not name. The letter was three pages, written in a hand that had changed, that had lost some of its carefulness and gained something harder, something that looked like it had been written by a person who had already been through a great deal and did not have the luxury of softness.

*Dear Mr. Wren,*

*You are eighty-nine years old. You are still here. The light is still turning. I am writing this from a room in a flat in the 3rd floor of a building where the radiator clanks at two in the morning and I cannot sleep. I have just finished a book and I do not want to put the lamp out. I want to sit in the dark with the light on and think about a man on a rock who read my letters for twenty years before I existed.*

*I want you to know that you were there. I want you to know that I found the way. Not because you sent it. Because you were the shape of the space it had to pass through. I don't think I can explain that in a sentence. I think I can explain it in the fact that I am sitting here, alive, in a room that is warm, and the light is on, and I am not alone, and I am not alone because of you, not because of anything you did, but because you made the world wide enough for me to fit in.*

*I am not writing to ask for anything. I am writing to close the drawer. I am writing so that you can open it and see that I am here, and then you can close it again and go back to the log and the salt and the slow turning of the beam. You don't need to do anything. You just need to know that I am here, and that the letter in the drawer was real, and that the space between 1985 and now was not empty, even though no one was there.*

*Thank you, Mr. Wren. Thank you for being here before I was.*

*C.*

---

Aldous read the letter in the small of the night, the light turning above him, the sea muttering its unfinished sentence. He did not open the drawer. He held the letter in both hands, and the paper was soft in a way that old paper should be, and the ink had faded a little, and it smelled faintly of salt, and he sat at the table for a very long time.

Then he went to the drawer. He opened it. He took out the first letter, the one from November 1985, and he read it once, from beginning to end, and then he put it back. He put all of them back. He closed the drawer. He went up the spiral stairs to the gallery and stood in the cold and watched the beam cut the dark, and he thought, *She is here. She is here. She is here.* And the light turned, and the sea went on with its sentence, and the night went on with its keeping, and the light turned again.
