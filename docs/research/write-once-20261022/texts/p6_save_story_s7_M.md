On the Meridian, mornings began in the dark and ended with something burning.

Mara was awake in the galley before the agricultural ring's lights came on, turning the heat under a sheet of dough, listening through the hull to the distant pulse of the hydroponic pumps. Beyond the bulkheads, three thousand four hundred people were sleeping in berths like the ribs of a whale, drifting through another day of a voyage that would outlive all of them. The ship was carrying them to a planet they would never see — that was the promise of it, the original promise, made two hundred and ten years ago on a world she had only ever read about in a book. Her great-grandchildren would never see it either. She was fifty-three, and the children born this decade would be the first to cross the threshold, if the Meridian held.

It held. It always held. That was the first lesson of the galley: nothing burned forever.

The galley hung at the center of the agricultural ring, a cylinder of pale light. Behind her, the ring was waking. The tomato canopies were already stretching toward their UV lamps. The lettuce towers were being checked by scanners that read moisture the way a doctor reads a pulse. And beyond all of it, behind three locked bulkheads and a door as wide as a cardigan...

And on the shelf of honor, the ledger: the original recipe book, its leather cover worn to the color of a bruise, opened on a stand and left there because no one had the right to close it without permission.

The cabinet held a knife, a wooden spoon, a brass canister with a thread's worth of saffron inside, now empty, and a second ledger. Not the great one, but a smaller one, its pages softer, its handwriting shakier. On the cover, in ink older than the ship's hull: what the crew ate.

Mara sat on the cold floor of the reserve for a long time, and when she stood up, her hands were not steady.

"I found out who I'm feeding," she said.

The galley filled with a smell that was not in the ledger and was in the ship. It was a smell the children were going to carry in their memories, and when they were old, they would be able to say: I remember the taste of Sunday. It tastes like this.

They served it in the long hall of the agricultural ring, from four large pots, and the hall filled with people until the air tasted of broth and bodies and the low hum of the engines. Mara stood near the doorway and watched. Watched the old eat first, slowly, the way you eat something that belongs to them. Watched Dev pass the second pot to the children. Watched a girl of about nine take a spoonful, close her eyes, and then, after a moment, open them again and say to no one in particular, "It tastes like... home." The girl had never been to Earth. The girl was about to live in it, if the Meridian held.

Afterward, Dev found Mara near the pantry door. The great ledger was on its stand, open to the pilaf recipe, the line for the saffron waiting for a note.

"Do I write it?" he asked. "'Ingredient missing. See reserve?'"

Mara looked at the line. Two hundred and ten years of cooks had written in the margin. This year, she would write in her own hand, and after her, someone would read it and begin the search again.

"Write what we ate," she said. "And write that it was enough."

That night, Mara went to the pantry and opened the small ledger — the one that said what the crew ate — and wrote a single line, in ink that would fade within two hundred years.

# Sunday on the Meridian

On the Meridian, mornings began in the dark and ended with something burning.

Mara was awake in the galley before the ring's lights came on, turning the heat under a sheet of dough, listening through the hull to the distant pulse of the hydroponic pumps. Beyond the bulkheads, three thousand four hundred people were sleeping in berths like the ribs of a whale, drifting through another day of a voyage that would outlive all of them. The ship was carrying them to a planet they would never see — that was the promise of it, the original promise, made two hundred and ten years ago on a world she had only ever read about in a book. She was fifty-three, and the children born this decade would be the first to make the crossing, if the Meridian held.

It always held. That was the first lesson of the galley: nothing burned forever.

The galley hung at the center of the agricultural ring, a cylinder of pale light. Behind her the ring was waking: the tomato canopies stretching toward their UV lamps, the lettuce towers being checked by scanners that read moisture the way a doctor reads a pulse. And beyond all of it, behind three locked bulkheads and a door the width of two people, was the pantry. The pantry where the taste of Earth lived.

Inside the pantry were the jars. Four hundred and twelve of them, labeled in different hands, in ink that had faded, in handwriting that changed across the centuries: salt from the recycler's loop, bay leaf from a hydroponic trial that had ended three generations ago, caraway seed saved one seed at a time from a single plant. On the shelf of honor was the ledger: the original recipe book, its leather cover worn to the color of a bruise, open on a stand because no one had the right to close it without permission.

The rule was simple, and it had been broken exactly twice. Everything Sunday was cooked from the ledger, not closely, not with a pinch of this or a little of that, but from it, word for word. When an ingredient ran out, you wrote what was missing on the inside cover in your own hand, and the next cook would know where to start the search. No substitutions. The ledger was a monument, and the cook was its caretaker.

This year, the problem was the saffron.

Sunday's centerpiece was a pilaf the first cook had called "the color of the last autumn." The recipe was six lines long, almost shy about it: rice, butter, stock, and a thread's worth of saffron, bloomed in the hot liquid before the grains went in, so the dish came out the color of the sky just before the sun went down. Two hundred and ten years ago, the saffron had come from a field in the Mediterranean, carried aboard in a small brass canister. The first cook had written in the ledger: "I have not been able to grow it since. What I have is all I have."

By the third century of the voyage, the saffron had been grown in a reserve greenhouse on the Meridian: one plant in a small room where the heat and light were dialed in by hand. Fragile, ridiculous, and always the crop. This year the plant died. The technician reported it with a flat voice, like weather: the roots had collapsed. All the saffron had already been harvested, a thread's worth, in the pantry. One harvest. Enough for one Sunday. And then the ledger would go back to a line with a missing ingredient in its margin.

Mara stood before the fryer for a long time, watching the oil.

"She could just use the azafrane substitute," said Dev.

He was the Second Cook, nineteen, one of the children who would make the crossing. He had the cook's hands: quick, patient, not afraid of a dirty station.

"It tastes like saffron," he said. "The analysis says it tastes like saffron. It's the same."

"It is not the same," Mara said.

"That's not what the analysis said."

"The analysis can't taste it."

Dev leaned against the counter and looked at her the way the crew sometimes looked — not unkindly, but the way you look at a monument that has become inconvenient. "Grandma's Sunday isn't the one that matters," he said. "The people who are alive are going to eat it. Let them eat it."

Mara said nothing. He was right, and she hated that. The children on the Meridian had never seen an autumn. There was no Mediterranean aboard; the tomato plants bore fruit that was technically a tomato. Everything she cooked was an approximation of a memory that didn't belong to her. The ledger was the only thing tying her to the taste of a world she had never walked on.

She told Dev she was going to the reserve. He didn't stop her, which meant he'd already made up his mind.

The reserve lay below the agricultural ring in a part of the ship that smelled of metal and cold air. The archive was not large: the ship's history fit in a single room, most of it data she had never needed. But at the far end of the room, behind a rack of agricultural records, was a cabinet of the first cook's effects. She had always had a key.

The cabinet held a knife, a wooden spoon, a brass canister, now empty, and a second ledger. Not the big one, but a smaller one, its pages softer, its handwriting shakier. On the cover, in ink older than the ship's hull: what the crew ate.

Mara opened it.

The first page was dated three months after departure: "Salt cod, rice, water, a pinch of pepper. We ate it in the mess hall and no one complained. I wrote this down so it would be known that we were not starving, even though we were." The pages went on for years: the first tomato harvest, celebrated with a pot of soup and a slice of anniversary cake; the winter of the condensation failure, when they ate nothing but algae bread and no one wrote anything; a stew the crew ate every week for eleven years, because the cook who died had no recipe, and then they ate the stew until the saffron came back and no one said a word about the years.

And there, two pages from the end, in handwriting old and careful and faintly unafraid:

"Year 47. Saffron failed again. I did not cook the pilaf. I cooked the stew. The crew ate well, and I was not sorry. The recipe is not the dish. The dish is what the people at the table make of it. If I have left anything on this ship, it is this: feed the living."

Mara sat on the cold floor of the reserve for a long time, and when she stood up, her hands were not steady.

She found Dev at the fryer. He was looking at her; since the greenhouse failure, his face had held something that was not calculation.

"What did you find?" he asked.

"I found out who I'm feeding," she said.

They cooked the stew.

It was not a complicated dish. Salt cod, rehydrated until the flesh came apart; rice from the hydroponic bay; onion, pepper, and the azafrane substitute Dev had brought from the chem lab, stirred in at the end so the pot turned the color of the sky just before sunset. Mara added, at the last moment, a thing that had not been in any recipe for two centuries: the first ripe tomato of the ring that year, split and dropped in while it still held a little of its own heat.

The galley filled with a smell that was not in the ledger and was in the ship. It was a smell the children would carry in their memories, and when they were old they would be able to say: I remember the taste of Sunday. It tastes like this.

They served it in the long hall from four big pots, and the hall filled with people until the air tasted of broth and the low hum of the engines. Mara stood near the doorway and watched: the old eating first, slowly, the way you eat something that belongs to them; Dev passing the second pot to the children; a girl about nine taking a spoonful, closing her eyes, and then opening them and saying to no one in particular, "It tastes like... home." The girl had never seen Earth. She would live on it, if the Meridian held.

Afterward, Dev found Mara by the pantry door. The big ledger was on its stand, open to the pilaf recipe, the saffron line waiting for a note.

"Do I write it?" he asked. "Ingredient missing. See reserve?"

Mara looked at the line. This year she would write it in her own hand, and after her, someone else would read it and start the search again.

"Write what we ate," she said. "And write that it was enough."

That night, Mara opened the small ledger — the one that said what the crew ate — and wrote one line, in ink that would fade within two hundred years.

It read: "Year 211. The stew. We were not starving. The recipe changed. No one asked permission."

She closed it, put it on its stand beside the big one, and went out into the waking ring, where the tomato canopies were stretching toward their lamps.
