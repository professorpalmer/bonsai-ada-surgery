Chapter One
The Last Run to Blackbird Gorge

The first thing you learn on a mountain railroad is that the dead man does not lie, but the living do. The dead man's body gives up its angles to anyone willing to look; the living will stand in the same snow and argue about the weather, the price of coffee, or whether the dead man was wearing a coat.

Eli Cramer had spent thirty-one years on the Harrowgate Mountain line, so he knew how to tell the difference between a body and a body that had been moved. At sixty-seven he was a little too old to be a detective and a little too useful to be allowed to sit down. His knees clicked like the old wooden switches. His hands had the short, thick fingers of a man who had pulled brakes, counted tickets, and held on to railings in rain. Now he kept a feed store in the old depot town, where the rails had been ripped out and replaced by a dirt road, and he spent most of his days repairing the clock that had once stood in the station.

The clock had stopped at 11:45.

He had fixed it every spring for twenty years. No one had ever told him why it would not run after 11:45 except his wife, before the cancer took her, which was that the last train had left at 11:45.

The bell over the door rang. Sheriff Dana Voss stepped in without waiting to be admitted.

"Eli."

"Sheriff."

"Bad news."

"It's always bad news when a woman in a sheriff's jacket walks into my store in the middle of a snowstorm."

She looked at the clock. "You know why it stops."

"I know a lot of things, Sheriff. I don't know why you're here."

"Harold Wren is dead."

The name settled in the shop like a dropped hammer. Harold Wren had been the town's unofficial historian, the man who kept the railroad's memory in a shoebox under his kitchen table. He had written letters to the county, repaired the old station clock, and once tried to get the county to relink the old trestle over Blackbird Creek. He was seventy, thin as a rail spike, and the last person in Pine Hollow who still remembered the sound of the switchmen's lamps.

"Accident?" Eli asked, though he already felt the wrongness in the words.

"Found at the old trestle. They think heart attack."

"Found by whom?"

"A trail runner. She says he was slumped over the railing."

"And the snow?"

Voss hesitated. "Snow all over."

Eli reached for his coat. "Take me."

"You don't have to—"

"I know that trestle better than I know my own wife's face." He paused. "I know both. I'm taking the truck."

The ride out was a long line of bare pines and white sky. Pine Hollow clung to the valley floor like a barn owl to a post. Above it, the old mountain line ran through Blackbird Gorge, a place where the rail had been cut so sharply into the rock that even the creek below seemed to lean. The trestle had stood in the middle of the gorge for eighty years, a wooden ribcage of crossbeams and sleepers, now painted in peeling red. The rails had been pulled in 1974, but the roadbed remained: a shallow scar in the snow, the ghosts of ties showing under the crust.

Harold Wren lay at the edge of the scar.

He was face down, one arm folded beneath his chest, the other hanging loose over the old rail bed. His boots were laced. His coat was buttoned to the throat. His hat was gone. The snow had filled his coat sleeves, and the fresh storm had begun to cap him in white.

Voss had brought a coroner's bag and a young deputy with a camera. The deputy's name was Pike, and he had the look of a man who had just seen a body and had not yet learned how to stop looking.

"Mr. Wren," Voss said. "Discovered by a trail runner at about two o'clock. We pulled the tracks at eleven."

"Discovered," Eli said. "Found."

"Same thing."

"Not in a body."

Pike shifted. "Is that a problem, Mr. Cramer?"

Eli ignored him. He looked at the dead man. "He was wearing his winter coat. The one with the wool lining. I saw it on him last week."

Voss nodded. "The runner said he was alive. Walking slowly. Then she heard him fall."

"Did she see his face?"

"No."

"Did she see what he was looking at?"

Pike shrugged. "She said he was staring down at the creek."

Eli crouched. The snow crunched. His knees protested in a way that made him glad no one could see the tremor. He did not touch the body. He looked at the angle of the arm, the way the hand had come to rest. A fallen man's fingers usually opened. This hand had not. The thumb was bent slightly inward, as if it had been holding something. Or as if it had been let go only after something had been taken from it.

"He was standing here," Eli said. "He was looking down. Then he went over."

"Or slipped," Voss said.

"The old trestle is not a slippery place in this weather. The snow would have been packed by foot traffic. Look." He pointed to the trackbed. "There are no footprints leading to his body."

Voss followed his finger. The snow was clean except for the trail runner's prints farther along the road, and the deputy's own prints behind them. There was no track leading from the road to Harold Wren.

"Could he have come from the other side?" Pike asked.

"The other side is a rock face."

"Or the river?"

"The river is under the trestle and the ice is not solid. A man could have come from a ledge, but not into this place without leaving marks."

Eli looked at the dead man's head. "The hat is missing."

Voss's eyes went to the edge of the snow. There, half-covered, was the brim of a wool cap. It had not fallen from the body. It lay three feet below the railing, on the lower rail bed. If Harold had been looking at the creek and fallen, the hat would have gone with him, or it would be in the snow beside his head. Instead it had been pulled from his head and set down. Or picked up and dropped.

"Who else knew Harold was out here today?" Voss asked.

Eli thought. "The same people who always know: the county forester, because of the land survey; the trail club, because of the new path; and maybe his daughter, because he's always writing about the trestle."

"His daughter."

"She calls him every Sunday. She works in Seattle. She was in town last week. She was asking about the old conductor's office."

Voss made a note. "The old office is in the depot."

"The depot is my shop. I know the office. It's empty except for a desk and a shelf of books. No one can get in unless the back door is unlocked."

"Was it unlocked?"

Eli's stomach tightened. "I locked it after I closed last night. But the lock is old. A crowbar could do it."

Voss looked at him. "You didn't lock the front door?"

"The front door is on the public side. The back door is the office. I locked it. But I left the window cracked for the cat."

The cat? Pike said.

"The cat belongs to the postman. He brings in old newspapers and leaves a mouse or a cracker if the mail is late. It's a arrangement." Eli straightened. "I don't think this is the cat."

He walked to the edge of the trestle. The creek below was a dark line in the white rock. The old rail bed ran across the gorge, and beyond it the mountains rose in a wall of black pines. There was a narrow service path along the far side, hidden under snow. If a man had come from there, he would have crossed the old rail line on his feet. Eli looked for a boot mark. There was one. A single print, deep and clean, half under the snow on the far side of the rail bed. It pointed toward the body.

He looked at it again. "That's a size twelve."

Voss crouched beside him. "You know sizes?"

"I know boot sizes, train gauges, and which men in this town can lie while keeping their faces straight. Size twelve, but not a size twelve of a man who runs. The tread is worn on the inside. The person who made that print was carrying weight."

"Or dragging."

"Or both."

Eli did not touch the print. He looked at the dead man's hand. The thumb was still bent. He saw, under the sleeve, the faintest trace of dark: not blood, but a grey powder. Ash? Or the grey of old railway oil. He leaned closer and smelled it. There was a smell of smoke, thin and bitter, like a train that had stopped too long in a tunnel.

"What was he holding?" Voss asked.

Eli's throat closed. "I don't know. But his hand was holding it."

The coroner's assistant arrived, a small woman with a camera and a face like a closed gate. She put gloves on and opened the dead man's coat. There was no wallet, no phone, no key. In the left inside pocket, folded like a paper train ticket, was a brass object. She held it up.

It was a conductor's whistle.

The old brass whistle of the Harrowgate Mountain line, the one that had been lost in the wreck of '74, the one that had been in the county museum for twenty years, the one that had been stolen six months ago and never reported.

The shopkeeper's cat? no.

Voss took it. "The museum?"

"The museum. I got a call two days ago. The whistle had vanished from its case. The curator said it had been missing for a week and that he was afraid to say anything until he checked the log. He was wrong; the log had been signed out for repair. But no one had come back to collect it."

"Who signed it out?" Eli said. "Harold."

Voss looked at him. "You knew."

"Harold was the only one in Pine Hollow who knew where the whistle was kept. He had the key to the museum's back room. He had been asking about it for years. He wanted to get it back before he died."

The name died in the snow.

"Before he died," Voss repeated.

"He always said he had a right to it. The train had died, he said, but the whistle didn't. It was the last voice of the line. He was going to put it back in the station and ring it once."

Eli looked at the dead man. The whistle had not been in his hand when the trail runner found him. The coroner had pulled it from the coat. It had been hidden. But the hand had not been open.

"Someone put it there," Pike said.

"Or took it and left something else," Eli said.

"What else?"

Eli pointed to the dead man's right hand. The fingers were curled around nothing, but there was a small dent in the snow where the body had rested, and in that dent, half buried, was a thin brass rod. Not a key. A conductor's lamp switch. The kind that had been used to lift the old station lights.

Voss picked it up. "A switch."

"A conductor's switch. It fits the lamp post at the old depot."

"The depot is your shop."

"Yes."

"Did you take it out?"

"No."

"Could someone have?"

"I don't know. The lamp post is broken. The switch was hanging loose. Any one of us could have taken it."

The wind moved through the pines. The snow made small white flags against the black trees.

"Harold was here to get the whistle," Eli said. "He was here to put it back. But someone was with him. Or someone followed him. He didn't fall. He was put there."

"Put there?" Voss asked.

"Look at his hat. Look at the print. Look at the hand. He was standing at the rail. He was talking to someone. The conversation ended. The whistle went into his coat. The hat came off his head. And then the man who was with him, or the man who came after him, set him down."

"Set him down?"

"A conductor knows how to set a body. You put the feet where the eyes are not. You turn the face away from the water. You cover the hands if you can. You make it look like a man who fell, not a man who was made to fall."

The deputy's face was pale. "You're saying you can recognize the method."

"I'm saying I can recognize the manners."

Voss looked down at the dead man. "The coroner will need time."

"Yes."

"Who do we call first?"

Eli thought of Harold Wren's daughter in Seattle. He thought of the trail runner. He thought of the forester, who had been surveying the gorge all week because the county wanted to sell the old trestle to a ski resort. He thought of the museum curator, who had been trying to get a grant to restore the depot. He thought of his own cat, the postman's cat, which had no business in a dead man's coat.

"Call the trail runner," Eli said.

"And the daughter."

"And the forester."

"And the curator."

"And the man who signed out the whistle."

Voss wrote. "Harold."

"Harold signed it out because he was going to steal it." Eli's voice was quiet. "That's what I mean by manners. A man doesn't steal a whistle he loves. He takes it because he believes it has to be saved, and he believes no one else will save it for him."

The wind came down from the mountains. The snow fell harder. The dead man's face remained turned toward the creek, as if he were listening for a sound that had not yet arrived.

Eli looked at the brass whistle in the deputy's hand. It was tarnished, but bright at the rim, like a coin that had been carried in a pocket for a long time. He had not touched it. He did not need to. He knew the weight of it. He had carried that weight once.

"Who else knew Harold was coming here today?" Voss asked.

Eli looked at the single boot print on the far side of the rail bed. It pointed toward the body, but it had no return print. The person who had made it had either gone back the way they came or taken the river road. The river road was gone under snow, but it was not gone.

"The trail runner," he said. "She said she saw him walking. She said he was alone. But she was behind him. She was looking at the creek. She didn't see the man in front."

Voss stood. "We need to get her."

"Yes."

"And the print."

"We can't lift the print. The snow is too soft. It will be gone in an hour."

"Then we have one hour."

Eli looked at the trestle. The old wooden beams were black with age. The rail bed was a ghost. Below, the creek moved in the dark.

"One hour," he said. "But I want to walk the line. I know where a man can stand and not be seen."

Voss hesitated. "The coroner—"

"The coroner can wait. The snow can't."

Pike looked from one to the other. "Should I call it in?"

"Yes," Voss said. "But don't call the whole county. Call the lab. Tell them I want the whistle examined for prints. Tell them I want the boot print preserved if it can be done."

"It's already melting."

"Then do what you can."

Eli turned to the body. "Harold, if you can hear me, you had one more train to catch."

The dead man did not answer. The whistle lay in the deputy's glove, cold and small. The snow continued to fall, covering the tracks, covering the prints, covering the place where the man had stood and the place where the other man had been.

Eli Cramer, retired conductor of the Harrowgate Mountain line, took one step toward the far side of the trestle. His knees clicked. His heart beat. Somewhere in the mountains, a train that had not run for fifty years was waiting to be heard.

If Harold had been looking at the creek and fallen, the hat would have gone with him, or it would be in the snow beside his head. Instead it had been pulled from his head and set down. Or picked up and dropped.

"Who else knew Harold was out here today?" Voss asked.

Eli thought. "The same people who always know: the county forester, because of the land survey; the trail club, because of the new path; and maybe his daughter, because he's always writing about the trestle."

"His daughter."

"She calls him every Sunday. She works in Seattle. She was in town last week. She was asking about the old conductor's office."

Voss made a note. "The old office is in the depot."

"The depot is my shop. I know the office. It's empty except for a desk and a shelf of books. No one can get in unless the back door is unlocked."

"Was it unlocked?"

Eli's stomach tightened. "I locked it after I closed last night. But the lock is old. A crowbar could do it."

"Did you lock the front door?"

"The front door is on the public side. The back door is the office. I locked it. But I left the window cracked for the cat."

The cat? Pike said.

"The cat belongs to the postman. He brings in old newspapers and leaves a mouse or a cracker if the mail is late. It's an arrangement. I don't think this is the cat."

He walked to the edge of the trestle. The creek below was a dark line in the white rock. The old rail bed ran across the gorge, and beyond it the mountains rose in a wall of black pines. There was a narrow service path along the far side, hidden under snow. If a man had come from there, he would have crossed the old rail line on his feet. Eli looked for a boot mark. There was one. A single print, deep and clean, half under the snow on the far side of the rail bed. It pointed toward the body.

He looked at it again. "That's a size twelve."

Voss crouched beside him. "You know sizes?"

"I know boot sizes, train gauges, and which men in this town can lie while keeping their faces straight. Size twelve, but not a size twelve of a man who runs. The tread is worn on the inside. The person who made that print was carrying weight."

"Or dragging."

"Or both."

Eli did not touch the print. He looked at the dead man's hand. The thumb was still bent. He saw, under the sleeve, the faintest trace of dark: not blood, but a grey powder. Ash? Or the grey of old railway oil. He leaned closer and smelled it. There was a smell of smoke, thin and bitter, like a train that had stopped too long in a tunnel.

"What was he holding?" Voss asked.

Eli's throat closed. "I don't know. But his hand was holding it."

The coroner's assistant arrived, a small woman with a camera and a face like a closed gate. She put gloves on and opened the dead man's coat. There was no wallet, no phone, no key. In the left inside pocket, folded like a paper train ticket, was a brass object. She held it up.

It was a conductor's whistle.

The old brass whistle of the Harrowgate Mountain line, the one that had been lost in the wreck of '74, the one that had been in the county museum for twenty years, the one that had been stolen six months ago and never reported.

Voss took it. "The museum?"

"The museum. I got a call two days ago. The whistle had vanished from its case. The curator said it had been missing for a week and that he was afraid to say anything until he checked the log. He was wrong; the log had been signed out for repair. But no one had come back to collect it."

"Who signed it out?" Eli said. "Harold."

Voss looked at him. "You knew."

"Harold was the only one in Pine Hollow who knew where the whistle was kept. He had the key to the museum's back room. He had been asking about it for years. He wanted to get it back before he died."

The name died in the snow.

"Before he died," Voss repeated.

"He always said he had a right to it. The train had died, he said, but the whistle didn't. It was the last voice of the line. He was going to put it back in the station and ring it once."

Eli looked at the dead man. The whistle had not been in his hand when the trail runner found him. The coroner had pulled it from the coat. It had been hidden. But the hand had not been open.

"Someone put it there," Pike said.

"Or took it and left something else," Eli said.

"What else?"

Eli pointed to the dead man's right hand. The fingers were curled around nothing, but there was a small dent in the snow where the body had rested, and in that dent, half buried, was a thin brass rod. Not a key. A conductor's lamp switch.

Voss picked it up. "A switch."

"A conductor's switch. It fits the lamp post at the old depot."

"The depot is your shop."

"Yes."

"Did you take it out?"

"No."

"Could someone have?"

"I don't know. The lamp post is broken. The switch was hanging loose. Any one of us could have taken it."

The wind moved through the pines. The snow made small white flags against the black trees.

"Harold was here to get the whistle," Eli said. "He was here to put it back. But someone was with him. Or someone followed him. He didn't fall. He was put there."

"Put there?" Voss asked.

"Look at his hat. Look at the print. Look at the hand. He was standing at the rail. He was talking to someone. The conversation ended. The whistle went into his coat. The hat came off his head. And then the man who was with him, or the man who came after him, set him down."

"Set him down?"

"A conductor knows how to set a body. You put the feet where the eyes are not. You turn the face away from the water. You cover the hands if you can. You make it look like a man who fell, not a man who was made to fall."

The deputy's face was pale. "You're saying you can recognize the method."

"I'm saying I can recognize the manners."

Voss looked down at the dead man. "The coroner will need time."

"Yes."

"Who do we call first?"

Eli thought of Harold Wren's daughter in Seattle. He thought of the trail runner. He thought of the forester, who had been surveying the gorge all week because the county wanted to sell the old trestle to a ski resort. He thought of the museum curator, who had been trying to get a grant to restore the depot. He thought of the postman's cat, which had no business in a dead man's coat.

"Call the trail runner," Eli said.

"And the daughter."

"And the forester."

"And the curator."

"And the man who signed out the whistle."

Voss wrote. "Harold."

"Harold signed it out because he was going to steal it." Eli's voice was quiet. "That's what I mean by manners. A man doesn't steal a whistle he loves. He takes it because he believes it has to be saved, and he believes no one else will save it for him."

The wind came down from the mountains. The snow fell harder. The dead man's face remained turned toward the creek, as if he were listening for a sound that had not yet arrived.

Eli looked at the brass whistle in the deputy's hand. It was tarnished, but bright at the rim, like a coin that had been carried in a pocket for a long time. He had not touched it. He did not need to. He knew the weight of it. He had carried that weight once.

"Who else knew Harold was coming here today?" Voss asked.

Eli looked at the single boot print on the far side of the rail bed. It pointed toward the body, but it had no return print. The person who had made it had either gone back the way they came or taken the river road. The river road was gone under snow, but it was not gone.

"The trail runner," he said. "She said she saw him walking. She said he was alone. But she was behind him. She was looking at the creek. She didn't see the man in front."

Voss stood. "We need to get her."

"Yes."

"And the print."

"We can't lift the print. The snow is too soft. It will be gone in an hour."

"Then we have one hour."

Eli looked at the trestle. The old wooden beams were black with age. The rail bed was a ghost. Below, the creek moved in the dark.

"One hour," he said. "But I want to walk the line. I know where a man can stand and not be seen."

Voss hesitated. "The coroner—"

"The coroner can wait. The snow can't."

Pike looked from one to the other. "Should I call it in?"

"Yes," Voss said. "But don't call the whole county. Call the lab. Tell them I want the whistle examined for prints. Tell them I want the boot print preserved if it can be done."

"It's already melting."

"Then do what you can."

Eli turned to the body. "Harold, if you can hear me, you had one more train to catch."

The dead man did not answer. The whistle lay in the deputy's glove, cold and small. The snow continued to fall, covering the tracks, covering the prints, covering the place where the man had stood and the place where the other man had been.

Eli Cramer, retired conductor of the Harrowgate Mountain line, took one step toward the far side of the trestle. His knees clicked. His heart beat. Somewhere in the mountains, a train that had not run for fifty years was waiting to be heard.

## final text

Chapter One

The first thing you learn on a mountain railroad is that the dead man does not lie, but the living do. The dead body gives up its angles to anyone willing to look; the living will stand in the same snow and argue about the weather, the price of coffee, or whether the dead man was wearing a coat.

Eli Cramer had spent thirty-one years on the Harrowgate Mountain line, so he knew the difference between a body and a body that had been moved. At sixty-seven he was too old to be a detective and too useful to be allowed to sit down. His knees clicked like wooden switches. His hands had the short, thick fingers of a man who had pulled brakes, counted tickets, and held on to railings in rain. Now he kept a feed store in the old depot town, where the rails had been ripped out and replaced by a dirt road, and he spent most of his days repairing the clock that had once stood in the station.

The clock had stopped at 11:45.

He had fixed it every spring for twenty years. No one had ever told him why it would not run after that hour except his wife, before the cancer took her, which was that the last train had left at 11:45.

The bell over the door rang. Sheriff Dana Voss stepped in without waiting to be admitted.

“Eli.”

“Sheriff.”

“Bad news.”

“It’s always bad news when a woman in a sheriff’s jacket walks into my store in the middle of a snowstorm.”

She looked at the clock. “You know why it stops.”

“I know a lot of things, Sheriff. I don’t know why you’re here.”

“Harold Wren is dead.”

The name settled in the shop like a dropped hammer. Harold Wren had been the town’s unofficial historian, the man who kept the railroad’s memory in a shoebox under his kitchen table. He had written letters to the county, repaired the old station clock, and once tried to get the county to relink the trestle over Blackbird Creek. He was seventy, thin as a rail spike, and the last person in Pine Hollow who still remembered the sound of the switchmen’s lamps.

“Accident?” Eli asked, though he already felt the wrongness in the words.

“Found at the old trestle. They think heart attack.”

“Found by whom?”

“A trail runner. She says he was slumped over the railing.”

“And the snow?”

Voss hesitated. “Snow all over.”

Eli reached for his coat. “Take me.”

“You don’t have to—”

“I know that trestle better than I know my own wife’s face.” He paused. “I know both. I’m taking the truck.”

The ride out was a long line of bare pines and white sky. Pine Hollow clung to the valley floor like a barn owl to a post. Above it, the old mountain line ran through Blackbird Gorge, a place where the rail had been cut so sharply into the rock that even the creek below seemed to lean. The trestle had stood in the middle of the gorge for eighty years, a wooden ribcage of crossbeams and sleepers, now painted in peeling red. The rails had been pulled in 1974, but the roadbed remained: a shallow scar in the snow, the ghosts of ties showing under the crust.

Harold Wren lay at the edge of the scar.

He was face down, one arm folded beneath his chest, the other hanging loose over the old rail bed. His boots were laced. His coat was buttoned to the throat. His hat was gone. The fresh storm had begun to cap him in white.

Voss had brought a coroner’s bag and a young deputy with a camera. The deputy’s name was Pike, and he had the look of a man who had just seen a body and had not yet learned how to stop looking.

“Mr. Wren,” Voss said. “Discovered by a trail runner at about two o’clock. We pulled the tracks at eleven.”

“Discovered,” Eli said. “Found.”

“Same thing.”

“Not in a body.”

Pike shifted. “Is that a problem, Mr. Cramer?”

Eli ignored him. He looked at the dead man. “He was wearing his winter coat. The one with the wool lining. I saw it on him last week.”

Voss nodded. “The runner said he was alive. Walking slowly. Then she heard him fall.”

“Did she see his face?”

“No.”

“Did she see what he was looking at?”

Pike shrugged. “She said he was staring down at the creek.”

Eli crouched. The snow crunched. His knees protested in a way that made him glad no one could see the tremor. He did not touch the body. He looked at the angle of the arm, the way the hand had come to rest. A fallen man’s fingers usually opened. This hand had not. The thumb was bent slightly inward, as if it had been holding something. Or as if it had been let go only after something had been taken from it.

“He was standing here,” Eli said. “He was looking down. Then he went over.”

“Or slipped,” Voss said.

“The old trestle is not a slippery place in this weather. The snow would have been packed by foot traffic. Look.” He pointed to the trackbed. “There are no footprints leading to his body.”

Voss followed his finger. The snow was clean except for the trail runner’s prints farther along the road, and the deputy’s own prints behind them. There was no track leading from the road to Harold Wren.

“Could he have come from the other side?” Pike asked.

“The other side is a rock face.”

“Or the river?”

“The river is under the trestle and the ice is not solid. A man could have come from a ledge, but not into this place without leaving marks.”

Eli looked at the dead man’s head. “The hat is missing.”

Voss’s eyes went to the edge of the snow. There, half-covered, was the brim of a wool cap. It had not fallen from the body. It lay three feet below the railing, on the lower rail bed. If Harold had been looking at the creek and fallen, the hat would have gone with him, or it would be in the snow beside his head. Instead it had been pulled from his head and set down. Or picked up and dropped.

“Who else knew Harold was out here today?” Voss asked.

Eli thought. “The same people who always know: the county forester, because of the land survey; the trail club, because of the new path; and maybe his daughter, because he’s always writing about the trestle.”

“His daughter.”

“She calls him every Sunday. She works in Seattle. She was in town last week. She was asking about the old conductor’s office.”

Voss made a note. “The old office is in the depot.”

“The depot is my shop. I know the office. It’s empty except for a desk and a shelf of books. No one can get in unless the back door is unlocked.”

“Was it unlocked?”

Eli’s stomach tightened. “I locked it after I closed last night. But the lock is old. A crowbar could do it.”

“Did you lock the front door?”

“The front door is on the public side. The back door is the office. I locked it. But I left the window cracked for the cat.”

The cat? Pike said.

“The cat belongs to the postman. He brings in old newspapers and leaves a mouse or a cracker if the mail is late. It’s an arrangement. I don’t think this is the cat.”

He walked to the edge of the trestle. The creek below was a dark line in the white rock. The old rail bed ran across the gorge, and beyond it the mountains rose in a wall of black pines. There was a narrow service path along the far side, hidden under snow. If a man had come from there, he would have crossed the old rail line on his feet. Eli looked for a boot mark. There was one. A single print, deep and clean, half under the snow on the far side of the rail bed. It pointed toward the body.

He looked at it again. “That’s a size twelve.”

Voss crouched beside him. “You know sizes?”

“I know boot sizes, train gauges, and which men in this town can lie while keeping their faces straight. Size twelve, but not a size twelve of a man who runs. The tread is worn on the inside. The person who made that print was carrying weight.”

“Or dragging.”

“Or both.”

Eli did not touch the print. He looked at the dead man’s hand. The thumb was still bent. He saw, under the sleeve, the faintest trace of dark: not blood, but a grey powder. Ash? Or the grey of old railway oil. He leaned closer and smelled it. There was a smell of smoke, thin and bitter, like a train that had stopped too long in a tunnel.

“What was he holding?” Voss asked.

Eli’s throat closed. “I don’t know. But his hand was holding it.”

The coroner’s assistant arrived, a small woman with a camera and a face like a closed gate. She put gloves on and opened the dead man’s coat. There was no wallet, no phone, no key. In the left inside pocket, folded like a paper train ticket, was a brass object. She held it up.

It was a conductor’s whistle.

The old brass whistle of the Harrowgate Mountain line, the one that had been lost in the wreck of ’74, the one that had been in the county museum for twenty years, the one that had been stolen six months ago and never reported.

Voss took it. “The museum?”

“The museum. I got a call two days ago. The whistle had vanished from its case. The curator said it had been missing for a week and that he was afraid to say anything until he checked the log. He was wrong; the log had been signed out for repair. But no one had come back to collect it.”

“Who signed it out?” Eli said. “Harold.”

Voss looked at him. “You knew.”

“Harold was the only one in Pine Hollow who knew where the whistle was kept. He had the key to the museum’s back room. He had been asking about it for years. He wanted to get it back before he died.”

The name died in the snow.

“Before he died,” Voss repeated.

“He always said he had a right to it. The train had died, he said, but the whistle didn’t. It was the last voice of the line. He was going to put it back in the station and ring it once.”

Eli looked at the dead man. The whistle had not been in his hand when the trail runner found him. The coroner had pulled it from the coat. It had been hidden. But the hand had not been open.

“Someone put it there,” Pike said.

“Or took it and left something else,” Eli said.

“What else?”

Eli pointed to the dead man’s right hand. The fingers were curled around nothing, but there was a small dent in the snow where the body had rested, and in that dent, half buried, was a thin brass rod. Not a key. A conductor’s lamp switch.

Voss picked it up. “A switch.”

“A conductor’s switch. It fits the lamp post at the old depot.”

“The depot is your shop.”

“Yes.”

“Did you take it out?”

“No.”

“Could someone have?”

“I don’t know. The lamp post is broken. The switch was hanging loose. Any one of us could have taken it.”

The wind moved through the pines. The snow made small white flags against the black trees.

“Harold was here to get the whistle,” Eli said. “He was here to put it back. But someone was with him. Or someone followed him. He didn’t fall. He was put there.”

“Put there?” Voss asked.

“Look at his hat. Look at the print. Look at the hand. He was standing at the rail. He was talking to someone. The conversation ended. The whistle went into his coat. The hat came off his head. And then the man who was with him, or the man who came after him, set him down.”

“Set him down?”

“A conductor knows how to set a body. You put the feet where the eyes are not. You turn the face away from the water. You cover the hands if you can. You make it look like a man who fell, not a man who was made to fall.”

The deputy’s face was pale. “You’re saying you can recognize the method.”

“I’m saying I can recognize the manners.”

Voss looked down at the dead man. “The coroner will need time.”

“Yes.”

“Who do we call first?”

Eli thought of Harold Wren’s daughter in Seattle. He thought of the trail runner. He thought of the forester, who had been surveying the gorge all week because the county wanted to sell the old trestle to a ski resort. He thought of the museum curator, who had been trying to get a grant to restore the depot. He thought of the postman’s cat, which had no business in a dead man’s coat.

“Call the trail runner,” Eli said.

“And the daughter.”

“And the forester.”

“And the curator.”

“And the man who signed out the whistle.”

Voss wrote. “Harold.”

“Harold signed it out because he was going to steal it.” Eli’s voice was quiet. “That’s what I mean by manners. A man doesn’t steal a whistle he loves. He takes it because he believes it has to be saved, and he believes no one else will save it for him.”

The wind came down from the mountains. The snow fell harder. The dead man’s face remained turned toward the creek, as if he were listening for a sound that had not yet arrived.

Eli looked at the brass whistle in the deputy’s hand. It was tarnished, but bright at the rim, like a coin that had been carried in a pocket for a long time. He had not touched it. He did not need to. He knew the weight of it. He had carried that weight once.

“Who else knew Harold was coming here today?” Voss asked.

Eli looked at the single boot print on the far side of the rail bed. It pointed toward the body, but it had no return print. The person who had made it had either gone back the way they came or taken the river road. The river road was gone under snow, but it was not gone.

“The trail runner,” he said. “She said she saw him walking. She said he was alone. But she was behind him. She was looking at the creek. She didn’t see the man in front.”

Voss stood. “We need to get her.”

“Yes.”

“And the print.”

“We can’t lift the print. The snow is too soft. It will be gone in an hour.”

“Then we have one hour.”

Eli looked at the trestle. The old wooden beams were black with age. The rail bed was a ghost. Below, the creek moved in the dark.

“One hour,” he said. “But I want to walk the line. I know where a man can stand and not be seen.”

Voss hesitated. “The coroner—”

“The coroner can wait. The snow can’t.”

Pike looked from one to the other. “Should I call it in?”

“Yes,” Voss said. “But don’t call the whole county. Call the lab. Tell them I want the whistle examined for prints. Tell them I want the boot print preserved if it can be done.”

“It’s already melting.”

“Then do what you can.”

Eli turned to the body. “Harold, if you can hear me, you had one more train to catch.”

The dead man did not answer. The whistle lay in the deputy’s glove, cold and small. The snow continued to fall, covering the tracks, covering the prints, covering the place where the man had stood and the place where the other man had been.

Eli Cramer, retired conductor of the Harrowgate Mountain line, took one step toward the far side of the trestle. His knees clicked. His heart beat. Somewhere in the mountains, a train that had not run for fifty years was waiting to be heard.
