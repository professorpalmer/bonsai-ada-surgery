# The Lettering

## Part One

Mara Voss had been a dead woman for eleven years before anyone found her.

Not in the way people mean. Not a body in a ditch, not a name on a list of missing. She had simply *stopped*, one October morning in the small coastal town of Harrowgate, and no one in the next decade could quite decide whether to call that an absence or a kind of death.

The town had moved on, as towns do. The house on Linden Street still stood, though the windows had gone dark after the third winter and the garden, which she had kept with the obsessive care of a woman who understood that order was the only prayer that answered, had grown over itself into a tangle of foxglove and bramble. The postman stopped leaving letters there around year four. By year eight, the mailbox had been stolen, probably by a teenager who needed the copper for a set of keys to a bike shop.

Mara Voss, when she did exist, had been a letterer.

Not a person who lettered things — not a decorator or a sign-painter. She was a *letterer* in the oldest, most specific sense of the word. She kept letters that had been lost. Not in the romantic sense of love letters found in old books. She kept letters that people had *lost* — dropped, misfiled, mailed to the wrong address, left on a bus seat, slid from a coat pocket in a rainstorm and swept into a gutter. Letters with no address, or addresses that had been torn away. Letters that were simply *orphaned*, sitting in the wrong place in the wrong world, and she gathered them up.

Her shop, *The Lettering*, occupied the ground floor of a narrow building on the corner of Linden and Chapel. There was no sign on the window, because there was never a sign on the window. People found her the way you find a thing you were always meant to find: a neighbor mentioned her once, or a lost letter appeared on a doorstep with a small slip of paper tied to it that read *Mara Voss, The Lettering. You will not be asked for money. You will only be asked for the truth of why it is yours.*

She kept maybe three thousand letters in a building that was no larger than a church vestry. They were stored in acid-free folders, arranged not alphabetically but by what she called *resonance* — the particular frequency of sorrow or hope or ordinary Tuesday-morning grief that each letter carried. A love letter from 1973 went in the same drawer as a thank-you note from a man to his surgeon after a kidney operation, because both of them contained, at their core, the same thin wire of *thank you, I was afraid I would not be here.*

Her hands were steady. Her tea was always Earl Grey, three sugars, no milk. She read each letter exactly once before shelling, because she believed that a letter read a second time by a stranger's mind belonged to the stranger, not to the letter. She believed in the one-reading. She had no other rules, really. The one-reading was the rule.

On the October 14th that she stopped, she was shelving a batch of twenty-three letters that had come in over the course of the week. They were all ordinary. A birthday card that had never been opened. A grocery list written in a dead woman's handwriting that was never sent to a store. The town did not know how to handle this. The police file said *missing, presumed living, no foul play indicated.* The town, which had loved her in the small, quiet way towns love their useful people, simply… didn't know what to do with the space she had left. So they left it. The house stood. The shop stood, locked, the windows boarded after the first winter when a storm took the glass. That was year one.

By year seven, the house on Linden Street had begun to decay in a way that made neighbors uneasy. Not dramatic decay. Not fire, not flood.
