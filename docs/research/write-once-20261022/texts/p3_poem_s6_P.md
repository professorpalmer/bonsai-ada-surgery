## final text

# The Last Pick

The last crate of nectarines sits heavy in my hands,
a rusted basket holding the sweetest, bruised-up days.
My shoulder aches with the specific weight of work
done in the long, bright arc of July’s heat.
I leave the picking sticks leaning against the oak
that has held up the whole orchard for decades,
and the dust from the dirt road coats my boots
like the earth is trying to hold me here a bit longer.

My cousin calls me from the barn, his voice a low hum.
"You got the good ones?" he asks.
"I got all of 'em."
The air is thick with the smell of the trees,
a cloying, green-sweet fog that sticks to the skin
and makes you taste the summer on your tongue.
The peaches are too ripe to keep, and that is fine.
The market will take them, or we will make jam.

I look up the ladder I climbed an hour ago
to reach the highest, darkest branch.
There, in the shadows, a few final fruits
hang like small, soft lanterns.
I don't go up for them. The day is done.
The sun is a red, hot coin sliding down the hill,
and the shadows are stretching across the rows
like dark rivers flowing back to the creek.

The truck idles, the engine rough in the cooling air.
My cousin hands me a glass of lemonade,
sweet and cold, with a mint leaf floating on top.
We drink in silence, watching the sky turn violet.
The other workers are gone, their voices fading
into the treeline, the heat finally breaking.
We pack the last crate, the one with the dented lid,
and I feel the season slipping away like a loose thread.

I climb into the cab and close the door.
The mirror reflects my sunburned face,
my hands cracked and stained with the dirt and the juice.
It is a good ache. It is a real one.
The engine coughs, and we pull out onto the road.
The orchard falls away behind the trees.
I drive on, carrying the heavy, sweet memory
of the last day in the orchard, in my hands.
