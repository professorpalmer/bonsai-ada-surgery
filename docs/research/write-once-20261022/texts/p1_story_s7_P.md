## final text

The first envelope arrived on a Tuesday, tucked into the brass slot of the postbox as if the wind itself had decided to deliver it. The paper was cream, heavy, and smelled faintly of salt and ozone, though the day was dry and the wind was a low, gray hum. There was no return address, only a name in a script so precise it looked like it had been drawn rather than written.

*To Elias.*

He opened it on the gallery, sitting in the rocking chair that he used to dry his boots. He was sixty years old, a keeper of a light that had not needed a keeper in twenty years, and yet the old stone demanded his presence like a hunger.

Inside the envelope was a single sheet of paper.

*Dear Elias,*

*It is raining today. I know this because I can hear the water dripping from the eaves of the room where I am writing, and because the glass of the harbor below is the color of hammered tin. I am writing this because I am afraid. I am afraid that the storm coming this week will break the foundation of the lighthouse where you live, and I am afraid that you are still waiting for a rescue that will not come.*

Elias stared at the words. The ink was still wet. He turned the paper over. On the back, there was a date written in pencil, small and careful.

*October 12, 2056.*

He looked at the calendar on the wall. It read *October 12, 2026.*

Elias put the letter in his pocket and went back inside to light the lamp. The great Fresnel lens caught the dusk and threw the beam out over the jagged teeth of the rocks that guarded the harbor. It was a simple, mechanical thing. It did not care about dates or futures. It just turned, steady and indifferent.

The next letter arrived three days later.

*I have seen you in a photograph,* Clara wrote. *You have white at your temples and your shoulders are hunched. You are standing in front of the lighthouse, looking at the sea. I know you are lonely because I can hear the way you clear your throat before you speak to yourself in the mirror. I am coming to you. I don't know how. I don't know what year I am in when I write this, only that the clock on the wall of your lighthouse is broken. It stopped at 4:14 PM on a Tuesday. I am going to fix it. I am going to bring tools. I am going to bring you coffee. I am going to bring you a dog, because I know you have lost yours.*

Elias had a dog. A scruffy terrier-mix named Barnaby who had died two winters ago of a broken heart, or perhaps just old age. He had not spoken of the dog in a long time. He had stopped keeping a watchman’s log three years prior, claiming the sea no longer needed a witness.

He read the letter four times. Then he held it up to the lamp light. The ink glowed, translucent and alive.

He began to wait.

He did not tell anyone. There was no one else. The island was a sliver of rock and brackish water, home only to the lighthouse and a few barnacles. The supply ship came once a month, bringing mail that was usually just invoices and government notices. He waited by the postbox. He checked the horizon for a boat that was not the supply ship.

The letters kept coming.

They described a woman. She was an architect. She worked in a city that was half underwater and half glass. She wrote about the rain, which seemed to be a constant in her life. She wrote about the fear of the storm that was coming to his island. She said that the storm was a "great erasure." She said that if the lighthouse did not shine during the night of October 29th, the ships would crash on the rocks, and the men and women on those ships would die. She said that her existence depended on that light.

*If the light goes out,* the next letter said, *the timeline collapses. I will not be born. You will die alone on the island, and the island will be swallowed by the sea. I am writing from the edge of the cliff.*

Elias looked out at the sea. The water was gray and choppy. There was no cliff. There was only the lighthouse, the rocks, and the endless, indifferent water.

He began to work.

He checked the fuel for the oil lamp. He polished the lens until it was so clean it was a second sun. He cleaned the gears of the rotation mechanism. He checked the battery for the backup generator. He fixed the clock on the wall. It was 4:14 PM, and the second hand was ticking again.

The night of October 29th arrived.

The sky was a bruise. The wind picked up, howling through the gallery like a ghost in pain. The waves were high, throwing green foam over the rocks. Elias climbed the spiral staircase, his old knees aching. He checked the lamp. The wick was low. He topped it off.

He stood in the gallery, looking out into the dark. The rain began to fall, thick and heavy. He could feel the vibration of the sea in the stone floor.

A boat appeared on the horizon.

It was not a supply ship. It was small, wooden, and fighting the waves with a desperate strength. It was moving toward the rocks, toward the lighthouse, toward the only land in sight.

Elias screamed. He didn't know what he was screaming for. He slammed his hand against the glass of the gallery window.

*Don't crash! Don't crash!*

The light of the lighthouse swept out. It was a blade of white gold, cutting through the storm. It hit the boat, and for a moment, the boat was illuminated, a tiny splinter of light in the dark. The boat turned, using the light to find the safe channel.

It came through.

It rounded the headland and disappeared into the fog.

Elias stood there, trembling. He waited for the letter. He waited for the confirmation. He waited for the rain to stop.

The storm lasted for three hours.

When the fog lifted, the sea was calm. The sun was breaking through, weak and pale.

Elias went to the postbox. There was nothing.

He went to the lighthouse office. There was nothing on his desk.

He looked at the clock. It was 4:14 PM.

He sat in his rocking chair. He picked up the pen. He wrote the date: *October 12, 2026.*

Then, from the postbox, came a sound. A soft, fluttering noise.

A letter.

He pulled it out. It was the same cream paper. It smelled of salt and ozone.

He opened it.

*Dear Elias,*

*I am in your lighthouse. I am sitting in the chair. I am writing to you. The dog is sleeping at your feet. The light is on. The storm is over. I don't know how I got here. I don't know how I wrote to you. But I am here. I am not coming in a few years. I am here. I am not a future. I am a present.*

*Come downstairs.*

Elias closed the envelope. He looked at the clock. 4:15 PM.

He looked at the door. It was closed.

He looked at the window.

There, on the rocks below, standing in the rain, was a woman. She was wearing a coat. She was holding a small, wooden crate. She was looking up at him.

Elias opened the door and ran.

***

**Postscript:** The lighthouse was automated in 2028. Elias died in 2031. No grave was ever found on the island. The logbook for 2026 was found in 1985, filled with the handwriting of a man who had not been born yet.
