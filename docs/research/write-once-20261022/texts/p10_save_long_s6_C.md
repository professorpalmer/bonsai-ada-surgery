# Two Ovens

Two ovens stood in the back room of the bakehouse on Gull Street, and for twenty-eight years they had argued over the temperature. The first was a steel deck oven built in Dayton, Ohio in 1962, which Nora Calloway's mother had bought for two hundred and ten dollars and a bus ticket out of Portland, Maine. The second was what everyone called the old girl: a wood-fired oven with a brick face, a cracked crown, and the particular gift of letting in the smell of the sea whenever the rain came off the cove. Nora fed the old girl oak and driftwood. The deck oven had a dial. They disagreed most mornings about whether the rye was ready, and Nora decided.

It was 5:20 in the morning and the rye was almost ready. Nora's hands were in the dough, doing the slow fold her mother had taught her — press, turn, fold, rest, and never once check the clock. Cobble rye, the town called it, because the crust went up in dark, bumpy plates like stones after a flood. No sugar. No eggs. Flour from the Brine Mill down the river, stone-milled since 1911, salt from the sea, and water. That was the whole recipe, which was the problem people had. They wanted a whole recipe. They wanted a list of things they could take home. Nora's bread was a habit you had to be in.

She slid the pan into the old girl and set a timer on the shelf above it, an alarm clock with a broken face that she'd never replaced. The bakery smelled of yeast and smoke and the brine that got in under the door from the street. That was her house's smell, the one her daughter had worn home from college and her husband had complained about for six years before he left for Boston and the sea between them became a metaphor. Nora had not replaced him. She had replaced the lock on the back door because the seagulls got in.

At 6:05 the bell above the front door made its one small note, and Mrs. Gable came in with her thermos and her reading glasses on her collar and the exact same order she had made every Saturday morning for eleven years: two slices of cobble rye, warm, with sea salt on the plate because Nora's salt was the only kind the Gables had ever trusted.

"It's good," Mrs. Gable said.

"It's rye," Nora said.

"Same thing, to us."

Outside, the harbor was taking its morning. Boats came in off the water in the color of a bruise, and the lighthouse on the point stood white against a sky that was not sure yet whether it wanted to be weather or not.

Three streets north, where the harbor's shadow ended and the square's morning sun began, Eli Marchetti was weighing butter to the tenth of a gram.

His shop was called Marchetti, and it was all white tile, brushed steel, and a single glass case that ran the length of the front window like a river you couldn't cross. His bread came in rows. His pastries came in rows. Even his apron was ironed to a crease. When a customer asked him, as they did most days, whether the croissants were "the good ones," he smiled with his mouth closed and said, "They're all the good ones, Mrs. P." That was the thing about Eli Marchetti, the things he never said aloud: everything he made was the good thing, and he had been making it this way for a year and a half and nobody in town had yet admitted how.

He had come to Cormorant Cove from Hartford two years ago, on a bicycle and with a box of his mother's recipes. His mother, Lucia, had run the pastry counter at the Cormorant Grand hotel for thirty-one years. She was the reason the hotel's wedding cake had a reputation, and the reason half the town remembered her hands more than her face. She had died when Eli was twenty-six, in her own kitchen, the way they say people die when they don't want you to cry: quickly. A croissant had been rolling on her counter, and she had never finished it. Eli had come to the cove because the flour here was good and because his mother had said, when the last time she'd been well, "the sea will teach you more patience than the oven ever will." He had come to be taught. He had not expected the teaching to arrive the way it did: as a town that split itself in half, as Nora's card on the counter the day he opened, addressed to her in her name. *Mrs. Calloway, neighbor. Come taste.*

He had left it there. It had sat on the flour bin for four days.

Nora had said it was a war of the kind they didn't have anymore.

The trouble had been building for months before that. Eli's opening week had gone well. Too well. By the second week, people were lining up for the croissant before the shop even had a sign that said *open*. They were calling it the Cove Cloud, because it rose in pale, uneven shelves, the way cloud banks lift off the water, and because if you bit into the inside and found nothing but butter and air you could forgive the rest. Nora's rye was still sold out by 9 a00, but the word "rustic" was doing work it never had done before, and Nora could feel it in the way the regulars's eyes moved, just slightly, toward the square when they passed.

Then came the cannery.

The old cannery had been sitting on the east end of the cove like a long gray tooth for twenty years, and then one Tuesday a man in a suit from a city Nora couldn't name came out of the post office and bought it, and the next month the sign changed from CANNERY to MARKET. A long lease. A food hall. A permanent stall for the bakers who won the bake-off at the Cove Festival, which fell in three weeks, on the third Saturday of September. The prize was the golden kneader, a brass trophy shaped like two hands in a fist, and the right to be the market's headline baker. Nora had stood at the post office window and read the notice twice. Her hands had not trembled. She had only said, "They always say headline."

Mrs. Gable had nodded.

It was a small town. It had always been that. A headline was not a thing that happened; it was a thing that was decided. The question was only by whom and by when.

By the second week of August, the square had begun to argue. Fishermen said the new croissant was good bread, which in this town was as high a compliment as you could give. Nora said it was butter with a point of view. Mrs. Gable said it was fine that two bakers could be good. Nobody asked who was better. Nobody had to.

Nora noticed the gulls first, because the gulls were hers.

Every Sunday at half past eight, after the last of her discards had gone down to the harbor wall — the misshapen loaves, the over-fermented rye, the edges that had gone too dry — the cormorants came in over the water in a black line and took the bread. It was not charity. It was a rhythm. Nora had been feeding them for twenty-eight years. She had a name for the oldest one, a fat gray one with a white throat, and the old girl's chimney had a draft that she blamed, and she blamed the weather, and she blamed the tide, and the only thing she did not blame was the gulls, because the gulls never asked why.

But the third Sunday in September, the line was empty.

Petrucci, the old shipwright who kept a bench by the cannery's old loading door, told her what he'd seen. "Marchetti boy's been buying the gull loaves," he said. "Says they're for a new thing."

Nora did not ask what the new thing was. She asked her hands, which were already folding.

The next Tuesday she went to Marchetti's shop before it opened. The back door was open, propped with a bag of flour, and Eli was inside, standing on a step stool, reaching for the top shelf of his display case. He turned. For a moment, neither of them moved, because they both knew exactly what the other's face was about to say.

"That's my Sunday bread," Nora said.

Eli set the bag down. "My mother gave it to me," he said. "Every Sunday for eleven years. Before she died."

Nora looked at him.

"She came to your window," Eli said. "She took the rye and the edges and the one with the flat crust, and she stood out there for ten minutes eating it, and then she came in, and you asked her if it was too salty, and she said no, it's the salt, and then she put a piece of the edge into a bag and said, Nora, this is the best thing in this town, and you don't know what it is yet, and I was twenty-two and I didn't know her, and she told me to protect it."

The bakery was quiet. The display case hummed.

"I have a starter," Eli said. "She called it Bess, because she liked the name, and I feed it every night, and I've been putting a pinch of your Sunday bread into the dough, because it's the only thing in this town that rises like that, and I've been hiding it from you because I was afraid you'd stop feeding the gulls."

Nora looked at the bag on the step.

She took the bread. She ate two bites of it. It was not good. It was not good at all. It was warm, and salted, and a little too sweet, and it had the taste of butter trying to be something else. She gave the rest back.

"You can have it," she said. "But not the discards. The gulls have a right to them."

"I know."

"Good." She turned to go, and then: "Your mother had good taste."

He nodded, and did not look like a man who had won anything.

On the fourteenth of September, the gale came in off the cove with the kind of noise that made the windows sit down. It was the storm they called the September push, the one that turned the water into a gray floor and made the lighthouse beam sweep the town's roofs like a hand over a table. At 2:00 a.m. the power went out, and the square went dark, and the market, with its new electric ovens and its long rows of glass, went silent in the middle of its first week as a food hall. The festival was on Sunday. The boats were coming. The lease was still up for vote.

Nora walked to Eli's shop in the dark, with a flashlight and a bag of bread under her arm. The front window had taken a branch from the maple on the square, and the case was broken in one corner, and the croissants were in rows still, but the rows were crooked, and the lights were off, and there was no one inside except him, sitting on the floor, holding one croissant under a towel.

It was still warm. He had been holding it warm for four hours.

"What are you doing?" Nora asked.

"Waiting for you," he said.

She sat down across from him. The flashlight made a small pool of light on the tile.

"The ovens are dead," she said. "The gas lines are shut. The festival is on."

"My mother used to say the oven is a person. You can't argue with it. You can only ask."

Nora looked at the broken case.

"I have the old girl," she said. "But I can't bake two kinds of bread."

Eli set the croissant down on the tile between them.

"My mother's notebook," he said. "She wrote everything in pencil, like she was afraid the words would burn. I have the lamination, the proof, the temperature. I can make them at home. You can bake them in the old girl."

Nora picked up the croissant.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's yours," he said.

It was not warm. It was only held.

They baked together from 4:00 a.m. to 8:30. Nora fed the old girl oak and driftwood and did not check the clock. Eli weighed the dough to the tenth of a gram and did not check the oven. When the first pan of rye went in, the steam came out in a white cloud and the door of the bakery swung open and the sea smell came in, and Nora stood back and let it. When the croissants went in, Eli set them on the shelf, and Nora set them on the shelf, and they did not touch.

The festival was at the cannery's loading door, which had been cleared and paved and strung with lights that did not work. The judges were three: Petrucci, the mayor, and a retired cannery cook named Hattie, who had never eaten a croissant and had opinions about both of them. The line was out the front of the market and up the block. Nora had the rye on the left of the shared shelf. Eli had the croissants on the right. Between them was a small white plate with a slice of rye and a croissant on it, cut in half, and a knife.

Hattie tasted the rye first. She chewed, and she closed her eyes, and she said, "That's the salt."

Petrucci tasted the croissant. He chewed, and he opened his eyes, and he said, "That's the sea."

The mayor looked at the two of them, and he looked at the plate between them, and he said, "What is that?"

Nora said, "It's the whole thing."

The mayor set the knife down.

The vote, when it came, was not a vote. It was a decision. The market would have two stalls. The golden kneader would go to the shelf, between the rye and the croissant, and it would not be picked up by either of them. The line did not move. The boats came in. The lighthouse beam swept the water.

In the morning, Nora stood in front of the old girl, and Eli stood in front of the deck oven, and the two ovens argued over the temperature, and nobody decided. Mrs. Gable came in at 6:05 with her thermos and her glasses on her collar and her two slices of rye, and she asked, "Is the croissant there yet?"

"Not yet," Nora said.

"It's fine," Mrs. Gable said.

Outside, the gulls came in over the water in a black line, and the old one with the white throat took a piece of the edge from the wall, and the sea went on, and the ovens went on, and the bread went out into the hands of the town, one at a time, like the boats coming in.
