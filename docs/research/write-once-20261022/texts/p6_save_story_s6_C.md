# The Salt Garden

In the galley of the *Caravelle*, the air tasted of lemon peel and old copper. Marisol Vega stood at the third range, the same range her grandmother had used, and stirred a pot of tomato sauce that had been simmering for nine hours. The tomatoes were red from the hydroponic bays, the basil torn by hand, and the garlic still warm from the soil. There were only a dozen people in the kitchen that shift, and all of them were listening to the ship.

For four hundred and one years, the *Caravelle* had carried ten thousand souls across the dark, and the galley had always been its heartbeat. Every meal was an act of remembering, of a world they had only ever known through recordings. When children asked where the flour came from, the answer was "the mills." Where the salt came from, the answer was "the ocean." But the ocean was a coordinate three thousand light-years back, a place Marisol had never seen, and only her grandmother ever had.

Grandma's recipes were not in the ship's database. The database had everything: nutrition, calories, protein yields. But Grandma Vega had carried a book in her handbag for forty years. It was her mother's book, and before that her grandmother's, its pages soft and its corners bent, written in three hands and two languages. *A ship can carry a thousand years,* Grandma had written on the inside cover, *if one person remembers how to make soup.* Marisol had read that line every morning for six years, ever since Grandma had died of a heart that had carried the salt of four generations.

Today was the day. In the morning, the *Caravelle* would dock at Proxima b, and every soul on board would be on the observation deck for the landing. But the kitchens stayed open all night. That was the custom Grandma had started after the Long Winter of '212, when the hydroponic bays had failed for two months and the children had been fed on reconstituted powder, and no one had cried, because no one knew what real food tasted like anymore. She had said, *Let us never forget the taste of bread.* And she never had.

Marisol opened Grandma's book to the last recipe: tomato soup, served with flatbread. Simple. Nothing the ship's nutrition computer could not produce in a tenth of a second. But the computer's soup had no memory. It did not know about the morning the bays had flooded, when Grandma had climbed to the upper decks to check the algae tanks and come back with mud on her boots and had still made soup for the children, because *food is not about the ingredients, it is about the person who cooks it.*

Outside the galley, the ship hummed its low morning song. In the mess hall below, the first line for the landing had already formed: families, engineers, teachers, and children pressing their faces to the glass, their breath fogging the panels. They did not know what they were waiting for, not really. They were waiting to become people who had ground. Marisol understood what that felt like. Her own feet had only ever known the deck plating of the *Caravelle*. All of Earth was a song in her grandmother's voice.

She began. The onions went into a pan of oil—real oil, pressed that spring in the agave bay, the first oil pressed by hands. The garlic went in whole. She stirred the tomato paste, the dried thyme, the red pepper flakes Grandma had kept in a glass jar, a jar that had survived the voyage because her mother had wrapped it in wool. The smell rose in a column of steam and filled the galley, and for one minute, Marisol was standing in a kitchen she had never seen, in a city she had never seen, in a life she had inherited.

A small girl, maybe five, was watching from the doorway with her father. "Cook," the girl said, "is this the Earth soup?" Marisol stopped. The girl had never seen Earth. None of the children had seen Earth. "Earth?" Marisol asked, and the word felt strange and heavy in her mouth, like a stone in the pocket of an old coat. "Yes," the girl said. "My mom says it's Earth soup." Marisol looked at her, and suddenly the weight of it settled over her: all of her, every recipe, every spice, every burnt loaf of bread, was not the Earth. It was the *Caravelle*.

Marisol set the ladle down. "It is not Earth," she said. "It is the ship. Earth was a long time ago. This is our soup. The soup of the *Caravelle.* And soon it will be the soup of a new world, too." The girl considered this. "Can I taste it?" "After landing." The girl nodded, serious as a contract, and ran off to join the line. Marisol's eyes burned. She had spent six years afraid of landing, the day the ship would be only a ship, only a vessel, and nobody's home. But the girl had already moved the boundary of her home, and she didn't even know it.

The soup went into the bowls. Flatbread, torn, not cut. A little oil, a little salt, and the green she had kept for a special day: fresh mint, the last of it, grown in a clay pot Grandma had carried. The mess hall filled, and the smell went out with the bowls, and the first families took their tables, and the second families pressed forward, and no one talked, because the landing light was on the horizon. The ship's bell rang once.

Marisol stood in the doorway and watched. A mother held a bowl for her child. An engineer lifted a piece of flatbread and bit into it, and his face did something it had never done before. The girl from the doorway stood in the middle of the hall, holding her bowl with both hands, looking up, listening to the ship. "Is this what the ship tastes like, Cook?" she called, her voice small in the vast hall. Marisol smiled. "That is what it has always been."

When they landed the next morning, Marisol would be the first one on the ground. She had saved two seeds for the soil—basil and a tomato vine—seeds that had traveled in the dark for four hundred years. And the girl would be with her, bowl in hand, ready to learn what the Earth tastes like, and ready, at last, to become the next grandmother.
