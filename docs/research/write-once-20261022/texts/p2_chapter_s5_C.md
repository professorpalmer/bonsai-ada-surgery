Chapter One

The mountain had a habit of speaking before it acted. First came the sound of wind pulling through the pine tunnels. Then the smell of wet stone, followed by the small silver tick of ice loosening itself from the eaves. After that, silence, and in that silence something else: a shape that might have been a man, standing on the old rail line that ran across Blackpine Creek and disappeared behind the north ridge.

Eliot Crane had been a train conductor for thirty-one years, and in that time he had learned that mountains were patient liars. They said yes to a thousand small promises and kept them all. A rockslide might not arrive until morning. A man standing at the edge of a dead rail line might simply be waiting for a bus that had not run since 1998. Still, Eliot stopped on his porch, coffee cooling in his hands, and watched.

Blackpine was the kind of town that fit into one long, crooked street and three hills. The post office had a copper roof. The hardware store sold snow shovels, batteries, and the same three kinds of coffee. The only real motion for most of the winter came from the school bus, a single dog that crossed Main Street as if it owned it, and the blue glow of the police car parked at the depot. Eliot had lived in the same house since his wife, Mabel, died in the spring, when the ice had been thick enough to walk on and thin enough to kill.

He was sixty-eight, broad in the shoulders but not in the spirit. His hair was the color of ash, cut short by his own hands in the mirror, and his hands themselves had the look of old leather after being used too many times for the same work. The conductor’s cap hung behind the door, and his badge was in a drawer with a bottle of hand sanitizer and a photograph of Mabel at the edge of the railroad bridge, grinning as if the water beneath them were made of glass.

Eliot stepped off the porch and pulled his coat tighter. The cold came up through the floor of the house, not as a threat but as an old argument. It had been waiting for him all morning.

At the depot, the snow lay in low, blue mounds against the side of the building. The old locomotive, the *Blackpine*, stood beneath a shed with no glass, only weather. Its red paint had faded to the color of dried blood, and the wheelhouse windows were dark. A small sign on the door read: HISTORICAL RAILROADS INCORPORATED — PENDING RENEWAL. The word pending had been taped on three times, each time by a different hand, each time with a little more desperation.

The door was unlocked, which it usually was, because the depot had no employees and only one visitor each week. A boy from the school came with his history project every November and left behind a note: “I think the conductor’s office smells like cinnamon and old socks.” Eliot had once written back: “The socks are the truth. The cinnamon is the lies.” The boy had not heard that, of course. He was twelve, and he had no business knowing that a building could smell like memory.

Inside, the air tasted of iron and cold. The floorboards groaned under Eliot’s shoes. He passed the ticket booth, where the glass had fogged in the shape of a face that was not there. On the wall behind it, the old schedule from 1974 still hung, its edges brittle as dried leaves. The times were all the same as they had been for years: 7:10 AM, 12:45 PM, 6:20 PM. The route had been cancelled decades before, but the schedule had not been removed. People liked a promise that had stopped keeping itself.

Eliot stopped at the conductor’s office. The desk was still set exactly as he had left it: a brass lamp with a cracked base, a cup holding pencils, a ledger open to the last entry, a chair with the wheel bent to the left. He had not entered in three years. He had been too busy burying a life and pretending not to notice that a man can learn to walk in a straight line, but then stop, and then forget which direction was home.

The window above the desk looked out over the tracks. In the daylight, the rails were bright with frost. They curved gently through the valley and vanished behind the ridge, where the mountain folded over the road like a hand closing over a coin. On the far side of the valley, a dark shape moved. It was not a train. It was a man in a long coat, standing still in the snow, facing the depot.

Eliot’s pulse did not quicken. It slowed, which was worse.

He remembered the last man who had ever stood in that exact spot, watching the depot with that kind of stillness. His name had been Dale Harker, and he had been a surveyor for the county. Harker had come to Blackpine in the fall, when the pines went black with snow and the creek ran low enough to show the stones beneath it. He had walked the rail line every day, measuring the curve, asking questions about the old switch, asking about the bridge that had been closed after the flood, asking about the sound of ice in the tunnel and whether it was “the same kind of sound you hear when a building is about to fall apart.”

Eliot had liked him. That was the trouble.

Harker had been friendly in a way that made the town suspicious. He smiled too evenly, asked too many questions, and never once complained about the cold. He had sat in the depot café, if that was what they called it, and ordered coffee black and said he liked to think in black things: black bread, black ink, black silence. He had laughed at his own joke and then forgotten it. Eliot had noticed. Conductors notice. A man who forgets his own joke has usually decided to stop telling you who he is.

The next week, Harker was gone. The police had a file with no real contents: missing, not reported missing, last seen at the depot, no indication of foul play, possibly moved to the city, possibly to the coast, possibly to any of the places where a person can go without explaining. Eliot had wanted to ask the officer, a young woman named Mara Soto, what it was like to stand over a body and decide that the body had not yet arrived. She had given him the same answer she gave everyone: we are looking into it.

He had not gone out to the track after that. For a long time. Then one night, when the wind had stopped and the snow was falling in a soft, even sheet, he had gone. He had walked the line alone, counting ties in the dark, and at the spot where Harker had last been seen, he had found a single boot in the snow. Not Harker’s boot, or so they said. Harker had worn brown shoes. The boot was black.

They said it was a farmer’s boot. They said it had probably washed in from the creek. They did not say it had been placed there with the toe pointing up the line, as if waiting to be picked up by someone who had not yet arrived.

Now, standing at the window, Eliot saw the distant figure again. Stillness. No movement. The kind of stillness that made a man’s breath feel borrowed.

He went out to the track.

The snow crunched under his boots with a sound too loud in the quiet. The rails were slick, and the ties were blue with cold. He moved carefully, because the line was not a straight line. It was a question the mountain had asked for a hundred years, and the answer had been buried under snow. At the curve, where the track dipped and the creek ran below it, Eliot stopped.

The figure was closer than it had been from the porch. He could see the shape of the coat, the way it hung over one shoulder, the hand held at the side as if the arm were full of something invisible. There was no face. Only the suggestion of one, turned away, or perhaps not there at all.

Eliot did not run. He was not the kind of man to run. Running was for people who had not yet decided what they were afraid of. He called out, but his voice came out thin and strange, like a wire pulled too tight.

“Who’s there?”

The figure did not move.

Eliot took another step, then another. The snow swallowed the sound of his boots. When he reached the point where the track curved, he found the boot.

It was black.

Not a farmer’s boot. Not a boot that had come from the creek. It was a work boot, worn at the toe, with a sole pattern he knew: the same pattern he had seen on the boot at the end of the line the night after Harker vanished. The same pattern he had told Mara Soto had been planted.

The boot was warm.

That was the thing that broke him open. It was not warm with heat. It was warm the way a stone is warm after the sun has touched it, or a body is warm after it has stopped. It was warm with the memory of something that had just been there.

Eliot knelt in the snow. His knees ached. His hands trembled, and he did not care. He picked up the boot, and in the bottom of it, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth, was a piece of paper. He unfolded it with the care of a man who had spent a lifetime handling tickets and schedules, documents that said: this happened at this time, to this place, under this name.

The paper had only one line.

The train is coming back.

Below that, in smaller letters:

Do not let the conductor run alone.

Eliot stared at the words for a long time. The mountain wind began again, a low, pulling sound through the pines. Somewhere in the valley, a dog barked once and stopped. The figure on the track was still there, farther down the line, now so close that the coat had become a coat and the hand had become a hand.

Then the figure turned.

For a moment, there was no face at all. Only the white of the snow behind the head, and the dark of the coat around the shoulders. Then the face appeared, and Eliot’s breath left him all at once.

It was Dale Harker.

Not a ghost. Not a trick of the light. A man, alive and breathing, standing in the snow with a face that looked like it had been carved from the same stone as the mountain. His eyes were red-rimmed, his mouth tight, and in his hand he held a small brass key.

“You came back,” Harker said.

Eliot did not answer.

“I told you the train was coming,” Harker said. “I just didn’t say when.”

The wind rose.

The snow began to fall again, thick and fast, covering the track, covering the footprints, covering the boot in Eliot’s hand. Somewhere far above them, the mountain shifted, and for the first time in Eliot Crane’s life, the silence that followed did not feel like a promise.

It felt like a warning.
