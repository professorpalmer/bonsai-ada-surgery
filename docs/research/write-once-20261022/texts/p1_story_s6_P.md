## final text

**The Correspondence**

The post arrived on the third Tuesday of October, when the wind had dropped to a moan and the sea lay flat as hammered copper.

Elias Finch had kept the light at Cape Merrin for twenty-two years. He knew every creak of the gallery rail, every draft that found the kitchen through the window beneath the stove, every particular way the foghorn complained in the dark. He did not know, however, to whom to attribute the small envelope that sat on the step like a pale animal that had been waiting.

No return address. His name on the front in a hand so neat it looked typeset: *Mr. E. Finch, Keeper.* Inside, a single sentence.

*The storm you will meet in two weeks is not the one in the records. Tell your daughter the light will hold. It always has.*

Elias read it three times, then held it up to the window. The paper was stiff, cream-colored, the kind of stock used in hotels. He folded it and placed it in his coat pocket, and went to do what he had done every evening for more than two decades: climb the ninety-one steps, wind the clockwork, and light the lamp.

When he descended at midnight, his daughter's name sat in the pocket like a cold stone.

His daughter was a schoolteacher in the city. She wrote on the first of every month, on lined paper, in ink that smudged if it rained. She had not written for three weeks. She had not written, in fact, since the accident in April, since the ambulance, since he had stood at the hospital corridor door with his hands on the glass and watched them take the girl who used to braid his hair on summer evenings. He had told himself, in the months since, that he was not angry at her, only that he had not yet found the words.

Now, standing in the kitchen with the salt wind trying to lift the door, he understood he would never find them. The letter in his pocket knew something he did not: that the light would hold.

---

The second letter came on a Friday, eleven days before the storm the first one had promised.

It was a postcard this time. Both sides printed on the same cream stock. On one side, a photograph: the lighthouse at sunset, the beam cutting through amber light. The angle was wrong. It could not be taken from the gallery. It could not be taken from the path, or from the road, or from any place Elias knew.

The photograph showed him.

Not from behind. Not small and distant. It showed him on the gallery rail, seen from the side, his face turned toward the horizon. He was older in the picture. Not much. A few years, perhaps. His hands were wrapped around the railing, and he was smiling in a way he had not managed since the schoolchildren stopped coming on the summer tours.

The words on the back were written in that same perfect hand.

*Your name is Mara Voss. I am sorry I cannot say this from a place that exists. The storm is Thursday. Bring the spare wick. Do not go to the town after. The road will be gone by morning.*

Elias sat at the table with the postcard in front of him. Outside, the sea had begun to churn.

He should have thrown it in the fire. He should have written to his daughter, to the harbor master, to the little church with the broken bell. He should have done the thing he was paid to do, which was keep the light and say nothing to anyone about a woman named Mara Voss.

Instead, he took down a drawer of supplies, oiled the spare wick, and hung it on the hook by the door.

---

Thursday came early.

The storm was worse than anything in the records, worse than the winter of '09, worse than the old man at the harbor office had ever seen. The sea came over the headland like a crowd with its faces turned to the sea. The beam swung and swung. The house groaned. Elias stood at the window with his hands on the railing, and he did not go out. He did not go to the town. He did not go anywhere.

By morning the sea was flat again, and the road out of Cape Merrin was gone.

The postman's horse had gone, the postman had gone, and the road had gone, and Elias stood at the top of the headland and watched the place where the road had been, now a scar of broken rock.

He went back to the kitchen. There was a letter on the table.

He had not put it there.

*You are doing it correctly.*

Elias read it, and then he sat down at the table and wept, because he was alone, and because the weeping was not for his daughter, and because he could not name the thing that had come for him from a place that did not exist.

---

The next letter came six weeks later. It was not cream-colored. It was a pale blue, and the handwriting was sloppier, as if it had been written by a hand that was still learning.

*The lighthouse is not where you think it is. I know. You have been keeping a light that belongs to someone else for a long time. I am sorry. I did not know it would take this long to tell you.*

Elias did not know what to make of that. He kept the letter in a drawer with the others. He wrote to his daughter, in a hand that was his own, and the words were plain: *I am fine. The light is fine. I would like to see you.*

She did not write back.

He wrote again. And again. And again.

He kept the light. He kept the letters. He kept the spare wick on the hook.

---

In the winter, a man came to the harbor office and asked about the lighthouse at Cape Merrin.

The office was closed, the harbor was closed, the road was still gone. The man waited by the window, watching the headland, watching the light come on at dusk, watching the beam cut through the fog.

He was a young man, in his mid-twenties, with a long face and a careful way of speaking. He said his name was Mara. He said he had been looking for a lighthouse, and that someone had told him the light was still there, and that someone had told him a woman named Mara Voss had kept it for a long time.

He said this to no one. He said it to the glass, and to the window, and to the fog.

The light came on. The beam cut the fog.

The man stood there a long time, and then he walked back the way he had come, and he did not come back.

---

Elias kept the light through the winter.

The letters stopped in the spring. There was no final one, no farewell, no explanation. There was only the last of them, dated three weeks in the past, and in his hand, in a hand that was his own, written in ink that had not yet dried:

*I am here now. I am here now. I am here now.*

Elias read it once, and then he folded it, and he put it in the drawer with the others.

He kept the light. He kept the letters. He kept the spare wick on the hook.

And on the third Tuesday of October, when the wind had dropped to a moan and the sea lay flat as hammered copper, a small envelope sat on the step, pale as an animal that had been waiting.

No return address. His name on the front, in a hand so neat it looked typeset: *Mr. E. Finch, Keeper.*

Inside, a single sentence.

*You have been waiting a long time. I am sorry. I am coming. I am coming. I am coming.*
