# The Last Batch

Mara Venn had cooked the same breakfast for 212 years on the Aethel, but no one was the same. The oatmeal, grown in the humid black soil of deck nine's hydroponics bay, came out the same every morning, warm and soft, with a pinch of salt and a spoonful of reconstituted honey that the fermentation vats in the ship's belly had slowly produced since her great-grandmother's time. The passengers lined up at the long table in the mess hall, their faces pale with the ship's low sun, and she ladled the bowls without looking at them. They had never once asked for a different dish. That, too, was a kind of prayer.

Every galley on the Aethel was identical to the last, a rule written into the ship's charter before the first children had learned to speak. There were only two cooks, and Mara's partner, Doss, was an old man who had inherited her station from his own father, and his father's father. He handled the protein reclamation, the quiet machinery that turned the waste of bodies and leaves into the gray slurry from which the Aethel ate. "Don't get clever," he had told her the year the children had first been born with grayish lips. "The formula is the family."

Recipes were not written down on paper; paper had died three generations back. They were written on the walls of the fermentation room, in chalk that the cooks refreshed each month with the same hands that stirred the vats. Mara's hand had added a line this morning: oatmeal, two parts to one, honey to taste. Above her, a line from Doss's father: fishless broth, never; the salt must be exact. Above that, a line older still, in a hand so careful it was almost script: *remember that we were once people who walked on land.* No one remembered land. Not really. They remembered maps and stories and a word the old ones used to say when the ceiling lights flickered, as if the sky itself had coughed. "Storm."

The Aethel carried forty thousand souls, and all of them slept, ate, and died inside its belly. It had been launched from a dying Earth three hundred years before Mara was born, when the last of the founders had sealed the hatches and the sky had closed behind them like a mouth. Now the ship moved through a dark that no one had ever measured, and the only proof of direction was a single star that the founders had named and which, after their deaths, had become simply the One. Children grew up naming the stars after the food they ate: the oat star, the honey star. Only Mara still knew its old name, because she had found it in a data core years ago, and no one had ever asked why she kept it.

That morning, the ship shuddered. Not the deep rolling motion the passengers had learned to sleep through, but a sharp, short lurch, like a hand pressed flat against the hull. The oatmeal in the bowls turned to milk. Mara set down the ladle. Doss, across the counter, did the same. The lights flickered, and for a moment the mess hall was dark, and in that dark a sound rose from somewhere below the decks: a long, low tone, neither bell nor alarm, that went on for a full minute and then stopped, as if the ship had drawn a breath and decided to hold it.

Mara did not ask the navigator what the lurch meant. She did not need to. She knew, in the way the body knows before the mind, that the Aethel had arrived. The ship had been traveling for three hundred and twelve years, and the founders had built it with a margin, a small buffer, so that the One would never be reached by accident. The margin had not been a waste. It had been a warning. "I'll bring you soup," Mara said to no one in particular, and walked the long corridor with her hands in her pockets, because there was no one else she could tell, and because the formula, whatever else the formula was, had always held one more ingredient than any line on the wall could name.

The navigation chamber was on the lowest deck, a round room whose walls were windows into the void, and the void looked exactly as the charts had promised: black, vast, and still. At the far end stood the captain's chair, empty, because the captain's chair had been empty for a century and no one had the courage to sit in it again. The navigator, a young woman named Sela, was already there, staring at a single readout. "We're on course," she said, without turning. "We've always been on course. That's what the margin is for. It was not oatmeal, though it began as oatmeal; it was the broth from Doss's slurry, simmered for three hours with the last of the honey and a pinch of salt so exact it could have been weighed in memory. Forty thousand people stood in the corridors of the Aethel with their hands in their pockets, and no one asked what the tone had meant, because no one could say it either. Mara ladled. The bowls were not the same bowls of the first morning of the world, and the soup was not the soup the founders had carried in their data cores, but it was warm, and it was made by hands that had never once known land, and that, she told herself, was enough.

She wrote: honey to taste. And beneath it, in a hand that was no longer steady, she added the one ingredient no wall on the Aethel had ever held. The salt. The exact salt, weighed in memory, the salt of the last morning on Earth, carried across the dark by a woman who had once walked on land and knew, before the ship launched, exactly what the world would be missing. The line on the wall glowed, and the Aethel, for the first time in three hundred years, hummed, not like a machine, but like a hearth.
