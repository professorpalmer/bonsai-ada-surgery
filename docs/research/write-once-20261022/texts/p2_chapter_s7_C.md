Chapter One  
The Last Stop

The fog came in from the valley the way it had for as long as Elias Crane remembered, sliding over the pines in thick gray sheets and muffling the world until the town of Harrow Creek felt less like a place on a map and more like a rumor. From his kitchen window he could see only the tops of the houses and the iron curve of the rail line disappearing behind the ridge. For thirty-four years, that line had been the backbone of the town. Now the passenger service had ended two winters past, but the freight still came on certain nights, a low rumble that rattled the windowpanes and set Elias’s old bones humming with a kind of tired respect.

Elias sat at the table with a cup of black tea cooling in front of him, his hands wrapped around the mug. They were not young hands. The knuckles were swollen, the skin loose around the wrists, and there was a scar across the left palm where a door had once closed faster than he had expected. He had been a conductor on the Harrow Creek branch line for over three decades, and most of his life had been spent in the space between doors: checking tickets, calling passengers, watching the lamps go by, listening to the rhythm of the rails beneath him. Retirement had been supposed to be a quieting, but quiet, Elias had learned, was only a room with fewer sounds. It made you more aware of what you had left behind.

The phone rang at a quarter to noon, which was wrong. In Harrow Creek, people did not call at a quarter to noon unless something was badly wrong. Elias stood, the floorboards groaning under him, and walked to the parlor. The receiver felt cold in his hand.

“Crane.”

There was a pause, then the thin, reedy voice of Deputy Mara Quinn, the sheriff’s best-remaining man on the line.

“Mr. Crane, there’s a body at the old switchback.”

Elias felt something pull tight behind his ribs, old and familiar, like a brake line being pulled too hard. “Which switchback?”

“The one past the creek bed. Near the abandoned freight yard. We found it about an hour ago. A hiker.”

“A hiker?”

“We’re calling it a man, sir. Not sure yet. But you might want to come see.”

“I’m not the law.”

“No,” Mara said. “But you’re the only one who knows this line better than the men who run it.”

The line went dead before Elias could answer. He stood in the room for a long moment, the silence filling in around the receiver. Then he reached for his coat.

Outside, the air had that sharp, thin quality that came before a storm. The fog was thinning now, breaking into strands that clung to the fence posts and the bare branches of the maples. The road down to the town square was slick with melt and ice, and Elias drove the old sedan slowly, one hand on the wheel and the other resting on his knee. He had not been to the switchback in years. The tracks there had been cut from service after the collapse of the old bridge, but the rails still gleamed faintly beneath the snow where the wind had not filled them in. He had walked that stretch a hundred times, maybe more, and he still knew the exact place where the ballast shifted, where a boot could slip, where a man could fall and think he had made it to the edge before the ground opened under him.

When he arrived, the scene was already marked by a yellow rope that looked almost absurd against the dark pines. Sheriff Dale Okafor stood near the tree line with his hands in his pockets, looking more uncomfortable than Elias had ever seen him. He was a large man, broad-shouldered and blunt-faced, with a habit of squaring his mouth when he was trying not to show he did not understand something. Behind him, a young paramedic was taking notes on a tablet, and two of the town’s younger men stood at a distance, not quite close enough to help but not quite far enough to be respectable.

Elias got out of the car and buttoned his coat.

“Mr. Crane,” the sheriff said. “Thank God you could get here.”

“Save the thanks,” Elias said. “Show me.”

The body lay at the edge of the switchback, half covered in a shallow drift where the slope dropped away toward the creek. The snow around it had been disturbed, not by wind, but by something heavier. Elias stopped ten feet away and let his eyes move slowly over the scene the way he had learned to move over a track: first the lines, then the angles, then the small things that did not belong.

The man was perhaps in his forties, wearing a dark wool coat, a gray scarf, and boots that had seen better winters. His face was turned away from the slope, and a thin line of dark had already begun to stain the white snow beside his head. There was blood in his hair, at his throat, and where the sleeve of his coat had ridden up, the skin beneath was bruised in a blue-black pattern.

Elias crouched, then straightened because the cold had begun to bite at his knees. “How did he get here?” he asked.

Sheriff Okafor glanced at Mara, who was standing nearer the rope. “We don’t know. The hiker says he came around the lower trail and saw him first. Thought it was a log at first.”

“A man who has been dead long enough to be mistaken for a log usually has a story for it,” Elias said.

“That’s not what I mean.”

“I know what you mean. I mean, if he was killed here, someone had to bring him here.”

The sheriff frowned. “And if he was already dead when they brought him?”

“Then the question is why.” Elias looked down at the body again. “Where did you find him in relation to the tracks?”

“Five feet from the rail,” Okafor said. “No more, no less.”

Elias gave a short nod, as if he had expected nothing else. “The snow has been falling since the night before. If he had fallen from the track, you’d expect more disturbance.”

“Yes.”

“If he had been dragged, there would be a line through the drift. There isn’t.” Elias pointed with his chin toward the upper part of the slope. “He was placed here. Gently. Deliberately.”

Mara took another step forward. “What’s the significance of the distance?”

Elias looked at the rail, then at the body, then at the angle of the slope. “This is an old switchback,” he said. “The train used to slow when it came through here. Passengers could step off in a hurry if they were in a rush, but the platform was farther up the curve. If a body is placed just here, it looks like it was thrown from the train.”

“Or staged,” Mara said.

“Exactly.” Elias straightened and turned to the sheriff. “When did you get the report?”

“Ten past ten this morning. Hiker was about a mile down the trail.”

Elias nodded. “Who is he?”

The sheriff’s face tightened. “That’s the thing. That’s what we can’t decide. His coat is torn, so we took what we could. A name tag in the pocket.”

“Whose?”

Okafor pulled a small folded piece of card from his pocket and held it up. The letters were black and plain.

**Julian Vane**

Elias’s breath slowed. He did not say anything for a moment, but his eyes fixed on the card, and Mara noticed the way his jaw tightened.

“Do you know him?” the sheriff asked.

Elias folded the card once and handed it back. “My brother.”

The words came out quieter than he intended, but in the stillness they sounded like a door closing. The sheriff stared at him, and for a moment the only thing moving in the world was the thin thread of steam from Elias’s mouth.

“Your brother,” Okafor repeated.

“He’s been dead,” Elias said, “as far as I knew.”

That was not true, and they both knew it. Julian had not been dead. He had been absent. Five years ago, when Elias had been on his final year before retirement, Julian had come to town unannounced and demanded to see him. They had argued in the parlor, and the argument had ended with Julian walking out into the cold and not coming back. The police had looked. The police had found nothing. The town had whispered. Elias had stopped going to the switchback.

Now his brother’s name was folded in a pocket and held up in the morning fog, and the distance between then and now had been filled with silence.

Mara crouched near the body and knelt beside the head. She held her notebook open and said, “There’s a cut here, at the throat. Not deep enough to be the only cause of death, I think, but enough to make it quick if he bled out slowly.” She looked up. “Any other marks?”

Elias had already seen them. His gaze dropped to the man’s hands. The fingers were curled slightly, and the nails were dirty, as though Julian had been gripping something hard before death or shortly after. One hand lay across his chest; the other rested near his hip.

“Hold,” Elias said. “Look at the left wrist.”

Mara turned Julian’s hand over gently. Around the wrist was a leather band, faded and frayed at the edges. It was not a watch. It was a token, of the kind conductors used to keep in a loop for emergencies, though no conductor had worn one like that in decades. The leather was cracked, but the metal ring was still bright where the snow had touched it.

Elias’s throat tightened. He reached into his own coat and drew out a similar ring, old and tarnished, tied to the edge of his shirt with a thin cord. He had kept it all these years without knowing why.

The sheriff saw the ring and frowned. “Where did that come from?”

“A habit,” Elias said. “Old habit.”

“No,” Mara said, looking at him sharply. “That is a conductor’s emergency ring. From the Harrow Creek line.”

Elias did not answer. He had long since stopped pretending that the past was something separate from him. The past was the track: it went on ahead of you, whether or not you got on.

“Who had access to that?” Okafor asked.

“Anyone who worked the line,” Elias said. “Anyone who was close enough to steal one.”

The sheriff looked at him, and there was a question in his face that he did not put into words. Elias met it anyway.

“I’m not saying he was killed by someone connected to the line,” Elias said. “I’m saying this is a place where things from the line do not stay buried for long.”

Mara was already pulling her phone from her pocket. “We should have him bagged and moved to the station. Forensics can start the formal work.”

“Not yet.”

Okafor looked up. “Excuse me?”

Elias was kneeling again, his eyes moving along the edge of the rail. The snow there had been packed smooth, but not completely. In one narrow place, where the metal met the earth, there was a faint smear of something darker. It could have been dirt. It could have been rust. It could have been old blood.

“Wait,” he said.

“Mr. Crane—”

“I said wait.” Elias looked up. “This line used to have a night schedule before the cuts. The last passenger train left Harrow Creek at eleven-fourteen and came through the switchback at twelve-fifty. If Julian was killed here after midnight, and the snow had already begun to fall, the only way someone could have used the tracks to get here without being seen for a long way would be to wait for the freight.”

Mara’s brow furrowed. “The freight doesn’t stop here anymore.”

“No. But the yard still gets inspected on certain nights. There’s a signalman’s cabin three miles up the ridge. The inspection runs on a schedule, and if the inspection man is lazy, the yard is dark.” Elias’s voice was low and steady, the way it had been when he was calling the last stop on a late train. “Julian was not thrown from a moving train. He was placed here after the fact. By someone who knew the schedule.”

The wind stirred the fog, and for a second the pines seemed to lean closer. Elias looked at his brother’s face, pale and still, and felt the first cold knot of suspicion settle into his chest.

“Who else knew the schedule?” the sheriff asked.

“Everyone who used to work the line,” Elias said. “And everyone who remembered it.”

Mara wrote something down. “We need to talk to the former crew.”

“You’ll find them,” Elias said. “Most of them are still here. In Harrow Creek, people do not leave easily when the mountains are high enough to hide a secret.”

The sheriff studied him for a long moment. “You’re not a detective, Elias.”

“No,” Elias said. “But I’ve read a lot of endings.”

As if in answer, a distant whistle cut through the fog. It was not the train Elias had expected. It was shorter, sharper, and came from the direction of the creek, not the line. The sound rolled over the snow and settled into the trees, and for a breath none of them moved. Then it faded, leaving only the creak of branches and the soft whisper of the fog lifting.

Elias looked at his brother again.

“Time to move the body,” he said. “And Sheriff, check the lower trail. Whoever found him came from below.”

The sheriff nodded. “You’re coming?”

Elias did not answer at first. His mind was already turning over the last things he remembered about Julian: the way he had laughed too loudly in the kitchen, the way he had hated the sound of the train at night, the last thing he had said before he walked out into the cold.

*You kept my seat, but you never kept my name.*

He buttoned his coat.

“I’m coming,” he said.
