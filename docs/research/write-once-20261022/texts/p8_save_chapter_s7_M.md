# Chapter One — The Breathing Shell

The city of Thelun had no horizon. That was how the elders told it to the young, and how the young believed it, until they grew old and learned that some stories are only names for what people are not allowed to see. Thelun was built upon the shell of a creature the size of a mountain range, a creature whose true name no one dared say aloud. The people called her Mother. She lay upon her belly in the dark ocean and swam so slowly that a year passed while she crossed the distance of a village.

The city was an island of stone and copper set upon the green-veined plates of the shell, and its streets curved with the shell's curvature, so that if you walked east long enough you would arrive back at the west gate, a little higher up the curve. At the city's highest point, a temple called the Spine stood on a ridge that ran down the middle of the back like a second, smaller spine. At its peak, the temple bells were not cast from metal but from the bones that Mother shed, and when the tide in the deep wells rose, the bells rang without wind.

Cael had been born during a shake, which meant that, by the counting, he was a child of the Deep Tide. His mother, a shell-smith's daughter, said the baby had come into the world between two tremors and had been wrapped in a sheet of the bone the bell had cast off. In the stories, a child born during a shake was said to hear Mother's breathing before they heard their own name. Cael could not say that it was true. What he did know was that on certain mornings, when the air in the market square was so cold it tasted of salt, the whole city hummed beneath his feet, low and slow and patient, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

He was fourteen the morning the tide went wrong. Cael had gone down to the bone wells with the other well-tenders to draw the morning's water, and he had been the first to notice. The wells ran deep, far down into the shell's living bone, and they were fed not by rain but by Mother, by her own slow blood seeping up through cracks in the carapace. On this morning, the water that surfaced in the well was red. Not the rust-red of old pipes, but the clean, bright red of blood still warm. The other well-tenders froze. The foreman, an old woman named Brant, looked at the water, then at Cael, and her face did something that none of the younger well-tenders had ever seen it do before. It went still. Brant reached into her apron, pulled out a length of copper wire, and tied it to the rim of the well with three knots that were too many for a rope and too few for a prayer.

By noon the bells were ringing by themselves, the bone bells at the top of the Spine, one after another, though no hand was on the rope. The whole city seemed to lean inward toward them, and in the market square the fish-sellers pulled their nets of kelp out of the cisterns, and the kelp came up dark and slick, and something in the kelp moved. A man who sold bread said he had seen the water in the fountains run backward. A child said she had seen Mother's eye, if she had an eye, open beneath them. Nobody spoke of what the shaking had meant. The elders of the Spine, three of them in their black robes, sealed the temple doors and rang the silence bell, a bell that no one was supposed to be able to hear but, that morning, everyone heard.

That evening Cael climbed the Spine. It was forbidden for a well-tender, and he knew it, and it did not matter. He climbed through the dark stone, through the cold of the bell chambers where the bones of Mother's old years hung like the ribs of a drowned man, and at last he reached the open air at the top, and the sky was still there, the same sky it had always been, and beneath it the rim of the shell curved away into a darkness that the old maps had always called simply the sea, and Cael stood at the edge of his whole world and, for the first time in his life, did not look away from what lay beyond it. Below him the city was a lattice of lamplight and smoke, and for a moment he forgot where he was. The wind pulled at his coat. Somewhere far below, in the bones of the city, something very large turned over in its sleep, and the waking city shook.

He did not go back to the well the next morning. He did not go back for three mornings. On the fourth morning the elders opened the temple doors, and the silence bell was silent again, and the tide in the wells had gone back to its pale, ordinary grey, and the city had done what cities do: it had decided that nothing had happened, and it would go on selling its fish and ringing its bells and calling its mountain Mother. But Cael had seen the dark beyond the rim, and he had felt the turn in the bones beneath the street, and he knew, with the certainty of a child who has been told a story all his life and has just, for the first time, been allowed to look, that the world of Thelun was only the part of the turtle the turtle had been willing to show them, and that something had, that morning, decided to show more.
