# Chapter One: The Bone Market

The first thing you learn in Tessera is that you are never truly above anything. The second thing you learn is that you are never truly safe above it.

Kael knew both lessons by heart, which made the morning she fell through the floor of the Bone Market feel, for one long second, like the sky had simply forgotten to be there.

She had been hawking salted fish from a stall that technically belonged to her dead mother. The market spread across the city's central plate in a wide crescent of stone, its stalls carved into the living shell of the turtle the way old houses are built into cliffsides—carefully, and only because the alternative was falling off the edge of the world. Around her, vendors called prices in the low singsong of the low districts, and the smell of brine hung in the air so thick it felt like breathing through a damp cloth.

Then the floor dropped out from under her feet.

Not collapsed. Dropped. As if the stone slab she had been standing on were a door swinging open on a hidden hinge. Kael went down over the sound of her own yelp, past the smell of fish, past a layer of cold air, into a dark that smelled of salt and something older—something like iron and wet earth and the deep, patient breath of something enormous.

She hit a ledge of rough-hewn rock. Her lantern shattered. She lay there in the black, listening.

Above her: the muffled chaos of the market resuming. A woman's curse. The thud of a crate.

Below her: a vast, hollow darkness, and from somewhere far, far down, a slow sound that made the hair on her arms rise.

Thump.

A pause so long it could have been minutes.

Thump.

Kael had never heard the turtle's heart. Every child of Tessera was taught that the great beast—Vareth, the old songs called him, or simply *the Under*—slept on forever, a god of stone and patience whose shell had been worn into habitable ground by three generations of builders. The Under's heartbeat was said to be a myth, a lullaby trick to keep little ones from peering at the cracks and asking questions.

But the sound that climbed up through the dark was no myth. It was slow, enormous, and unmistakably alive.

She scrambled up the ledge and peered into the opening. A shaft of morning light fell down the hole she had fallen through, cutting through the dust. Below, the darkness stretched into distance. And along its lower edge, if she squinted, she could see something that made her stomach turn: ribs. Not stone ribs. *Bone* ribs, curved and pale, each one wider than a market hall, connected by ligaments that swayed with every thump-thump of the heartbeat.

Vareth was not asleep.

He was walking.

Kael's throat tightened. The city above her swayed—a subtle, almost imperceptible tilt, like a cup of wine being carried by a man whose steps were too long for his pace. She had felt it a thousand times and never called it by its name. Tides of the road. That's what the Elders called it. The gentle rocking of the world.

Now she knew it was a step. And the next one was coming.

A shadow passed over the opening above, and a voice from the market ground her into the ledge: "Kael? Kael, are you down there?"

Her grandmother's voice. Old Maeta's voice, thin as thread and twice as brittle.

"Grandmother," Kael whispered. "You need to come here."

There was a long silence. Then, very softly: "Don't look down, child. Not yet. You don't need to see the bones until you've earned them."

Kael didn't answer. She was too busy watching the darkness below, where, far off in the gloom, a shape was moving.

Not a rib. Not a shadow.

A limb.

And something was crawling along it.
