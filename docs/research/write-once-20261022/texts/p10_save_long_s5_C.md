# The Salt Line

Morning in Brinewick arrived on the wind.

The town sat in a bend of the coast where the water turned from grey to green and the gulls had opinions about everything, from the weather to the weather's weather. June Calloway knew the hour by the smell of the tide and the number of ovens she'd need, then by the number of ovens she refused to fire, then by the first boat bumping against the pilings of her pier.

Her bakery, the Calloway Bakehouse, had occupied its crooked little corner at the end of the pier for fifty-three years. Its windows faced the water, so that the salt got into the flour, which meant her sourdough carried a faint brine note that the townspeople called "the taste of home" and the tourists, who could not pronounce Brinewick, called "the taste of home." The shop was old in the honest way of old things: a wood-fired oven the size of a small boat's cabin, shelves that sagged under the weight of burlap sacks, and a till that had not changed since the war, its brass worn soft as skin by every hand that had ever pressed a coin into it.

Two blocks up the street, on the corner where the road climbed toward the lighthouse, stood Vega Bakery.

Vega Bakery was not old. It was bright. It had floor-to-ceiling glass, a steel counter that hissed when the steam hit it, and an electric proofer that glowed blue in the early dark like a small, patient heart. It had opened four years earlier, on a Tuesday, with a ribbon cutting that drew fewer people than the ribbon cutting that had opened Calloway Bakehouse in 1953, when June's grandmother had tied it herself and the whole town had turned up with chairs.

Tomás Vega had not tied a ribbon. He had cut one, at a sharp angle, and then, with the easy cruelty of a man who had always known the exact angle, he had said, "Old things break slower, but they break anyway," and gone on to bake.

For three years that sentence had hung over the town like a low cloud.

June read it every morning out of the corner of her eye when she walked the short stretch to the fish market, which was between the two shops, on the bend of the road where the street name changed from "Pier Row" to "Lantern Row," because that was where the light came from. The street name was a small joke the town told itself. The lighthouse had been there since before any of them, and so had the bakehouse, and so, in a sense, had the bakery at the end of the pier, which was what the lighthouse was, a keeper's light, except that instead of keeping people from the rocks, it kept them fed.

June did not like the sign above Vega Bakery. It said: FRESH FROM THE OVEN — EVERY MORNING AT SIX. The "every morning at six" part was a fact. The "fresh from the oven" part was a matter of perspective, since Tomás had been a pastry chef in Lisbon before he'd come to Brinewick, and his ovens had been running since five, and the bread that came out of them at six had been sitting in a warming cabinet since five-fifty, which was not the same as fresh, but the townspeople who lined up at six and were told they'd "just come out" did not, as a rule, ask the question.

June liked to ask the question. She asked it every day, in a small, polite voice, to every customer who came in with a bag of Tomás's brioche, and she asked it in a way that was not quite a joke and not quite not a joke, which meant that it was always true and never, technically, anything anyone could be charged with.

She was, the townspeople said, difficult.

Tomás, the townspeople said, was impossible.

They said both things at the same table, at the same time, as if they were describing the same weather from two shores.

They had known each other for longer than the rivalry, and the rivalry, for all its heat, was only a thin layer over something that had been warm and unremarkable for most of their lives.

June's grandmother, Rosa Calloway, and Tomás's grandmother, Dolores Vega, had shared the same wall at the lighthouse. Rosa kept the bread and the jam; Dolores kept the fish and the story of the lighthouse, which was that the first keeper had never once been on time, and that the light had gone out for an hour every year on the winter solstice, "to keep the boats honest." June had been three years old when she learned it, and she had believed it, and she still believed it, in the way one believes a story that has been told to you by someone who believed it.

Tomás had been nine. He had come to the bakehouse on a Saturday morning, in his grandfather's old boots, and stood by the wood stove, watching Rosa shape the bread with the slow, deliberate hands of a woman who had no reason to be in a hurry because she had already finished everything that mattered.

"Bread is not fast," Rosa had told him. "It is only patient."

Tomás had stayed for the whole morning. He had eaten three slices of Rosa's rye with a spread of honey and salt that was not a spread but a kind of promise. He had left at noon, and he had not spoken to another human being before he went back to the school in Lisbon where his mother had taken him, because his mother had wanted him to learn a language that was not English and a set of hands that were not his, and he had gone, and he had not gone back for four years.

He had come back when the bakery was empty, which was to say, when the wall had gone and Rosa was gone and the street had changed its mind about what it wanted. He had come back with a business plan in his pocket and a pastry case at his hip, and he had walked into the bakehouse that had once been his grandmother's and his, and said, "I'll take the corner," and meant it as an invitation, and it landed like a slap, because the corner had not been empty, it had been occupied, by June, who was his childhood neighbor and his friend and, in the years before Lisbon, the only person in the world he had ever thought to leave and not miss.

The corner had been his. The corner had been, for a brief and bright period, theirs.

They had parted on a Thursday, in the blue hour after the bakery closed, when the wood stove was down to embers and the flour on the floor caught the light from the streetlamp and made the room look, for a moment, like the inside of a shell.

"You want to turn this place into a pastry counter," June had said. It was not a question. It never was.

"I want to build a bakery," Tomás had said. "There is a difference."

"There is not a difference," June had said. "There is only a matter of whether you are still here."

He had left without the door closing.

She had not opened it for him for four years.

The Festival of Tides came to Brinewick in October, when the water at the end of the pier went silver and the gulls went quiet, because they had learned, in the long way that animals learn, that the gulls who go quiet are the ones who get fed.

The festival had been in the town for a hundred years, and its rules had been written into the lighthouse logbook by a keeper who had since become, in the way that keepers do, a kind of myth. The rules were simple, in the way that the sea is simple: two bakers, one lighthouse, one loaf that would be placed in the window and would remain there until the next year's tide came in and took it, or the next year's keeper took it, or the next year's town did something else with it, because the logbook, after the first entry, was mostly blank.

The prize was not money. The prize was the silver bowl that had been the first thing built in the lighthouse, and it was kept in the window, behind glass, next to the tide loaf, and it was worth, in the way that things are worth when they have been held by a hundred hands and no one can say for certain who held it first, everything that matters.

The townspeople called it the Tide Cup.

The bakers called it "the thing I am not going to lose."

This year, for the first time since the festival had a name, the town had decided to put both of them in the contest. It had been a long conversation, in the long way that the town talks, and it had been carried, as all the important things are, by a few people who did not mean to be important but were, in the way that people at the center of a town are, accidentally at the center of the town's decisions.

June heard about it at the fish market, from old Marta, who sold the fish and the oysters and the salted cod and the small, salt-crusted prawns that the gulls fought over, and who had the habit, which June found, in a way, kind, of knowing everything that happened at the fish market and telling her, in the small, polite way that Marta told everything, as if she were telling a neighbor about a neighbor, as if June were a stranger, which she was not, and had never been.

"The lighthouse is going to be open for the festival," Marta said. "Two bakers. You and Vega."

June did not answer.

"You and Vega," Marta said again. "The window is empty. They said they'd put the bread in the window. Both of them. Side by side."

June looked at the water. The water was doing what water does, which is nothing, and also everything.

"And the judges," she said, "are who?"

"The keepers," Marta said. "Old Okafor and Dot Ferreira. You know the keepers."

June knew the keepers. The keepers were the ones who kept the lighthouse, which was to say, they were the ones who kept the light, and the light was the thing that told the boats where the rocks were, and the rocks were the thing that told the boats where they should not go, and the boats were the thing that brought the fish, and the fish were the thing that fed the town, and the town was the thing that fed June, and June was the thing that had not, in four years, said a kind word to Tomás Vega.
