# Chapter One — The Trembling

The world of the shell hums.

It is the first thing every child of Orenthal learns, before names, before counting, before the older law that you never lean over the rim and never look too long into the water below. The hum begins in the bones, low and endless, a note so deep it lives more in the chest than the ear. Your grandfather called it the deep song. Mirel's mother called it the tide. She called it nothing at all, the way people in Orenthal called everything: with an economy of words that made the whole city sound smaller than it was.

Orenthal was smaller, in fact. That was the trouble with Orenthal. It was a city of half a million souls crowded onto a single curved mile of shell, no wider than a long hand, built into the broad back of a turtle so large that no living person had ever seen its head, nor its feet, nor the place where its tail met the sea. You could walk from the Rim of Dawn to the Rim of Dusk and return before the second bell. The houses rose in terraces down the slope of the shell, carved from the same black chitin as the walls that held them, and between the terraces the green grew — the moss farms that fed the city, the lichen gardens, the hanging pools of brine where the small fish were caught by hand in the early light. Everything in Orenthal grew out of the turtle. Even the mortar in the walls was made from the shell's own dust.

For as long as anyone remembered, the turtle had moved east.

That was the second thing you learned. The turtle moved east, and east was always the way the water ran thin and bright, the way the wind came warm off the warm side. East meant the spring fish. East meant the long days. East meant the future, the word the priests used when they wanted to sound like they knew what tomorrow held.

Mirel was on the rim that morning, as she was every morning, pulling the gill-ropes down through the sea mist. The rim was a narrow walkway, no wider than a shoulder, and she had a rope in each hand, knotted at the ends, and below her the water slid past in a long gray sheet. She had pulled those ropes since she was six, when her father still carried her on his hip. The water below the rim had no name. Not a proper name. The children called it the Under. The old women called it the Unseen. The priests called it what it was, which was that no one had ever seen the bottom of it, and the question of the bottom had stopped being asked a long time ago.

It was the hum that got her.

Not louder. Not weaker. Just — wrong. A shift so small it was barely a difference, the way a song goes off-key in the last measure and you can't tell if it's the singer or your own ear. Mirel stopped pulling. The rope slipped through her knuckles. She stood very still with her hands open, and the hum went on in her teeth, in her ribs, in the green moss beneath her feet, and it was not the hum she had been born to. It was a tremor under a tremor, a second note sliding under the first, and it came from below. From deep below.

Below the shell.

She did not scream. She did not call for anyone. She climbed.

The climb back from the rim takes forty steps on the good day, and Mirel took them the way she had taken them ten thousand mornings, up through the moss terraces and over the lichen bridges and down through the upper city, where the houses were new and the walls were still smooth, until she reached the Spire of the Rim, the tallest building in Orenthal, and the bell tower in it rang the dawn bell, and the bell — which had been ringing every morning for three hundred years — rang a wrong note, flat and thin, like a bell cracked down the middle.

The people were already in the streets. She could hear them.

The tremor was in the streets too. The people had not yet stopped talking, but they had stopped, and a city that stops talking is a city that has heard something it does not understand. Mirel climbed the last of the stairs with her hands on the cold stone and her heart in her throat and the wrong hum in her teeth, and she went out onto the spire, and the rim was there, and the mist was there, and the water was moving.

It was moving up.

Not the tide. Not the wind. The water itself, in the long gray sheet below the rim, was climbing the side of the world, a slow green wall rising against the shell, and in the water she saw the first thing anyone in Orenthal had ever seen beneath the rim: a shape. Dark. Enormous. With eyes.

The eye opened.

And the turtle turned.

Mirel stood on the rim of the world and the whole city of Orenthal groaned around her, the stones beneath her feet sliding like ice, and somewhere far below — deeper than the shell, deeper than the Under, in the black place no one had ever seen — something vast and older than the city was lifting its head from the deep, and opening its eye, and looking up.

For the first time in a thousand years, the turtle looked up.

And something, in the water, looked back.
