## final text

The sun hangs low over the last row we’ll walk,
where apples blush like tired cheeks in heat.
We fold our shears and carry buckets back
to the truck with the loose, bruised fruit at its feet.
My palms are split where the stems have bitten deep,
and my shirt smells of juice and green and sun.
The orchard hums with birds who know no hours,
while I count the hours I have been one.

A peach falls soft at the heel of a stranger’s boot,
and for a second, I want to stay,
to press my thumb in its flesh and taste the year
that summer left behind in every way.
But the foreman calls from the gate, the shade is short,
and the sky is turning to a copper sky.
We sign the books by the pump and the shade,
and the day is done, and we are through, and by.

The tractor idles, a low, patient sound,
as if it, too, could rest in the field’s last breath.
My hands ache in the way that work can make,
not pain, but a kind of borrowed length.
The crates are stacked in rows like small white houses,
each one holding something sweet and round,
and I feel a strange relief to leave them there,
as if the orchard will keep them safe, not found.

The road out of the grove is paved with dust,
and the fields are stitched with gold and green.
I look back once through the window glass
where the summer I lived still leans.
The radio plays a song from home I half-remember,
and the engine pulls us toward the town,
toward the street, the door, the waiting light,
toward the life that never lets you down.

Yet in my coat pocket, a seed remains,
small and dry as a secret I won’t tell,
and I know that when spring comes, if I’m brave,
I might bury it and see what will swell.
For now, the day is ending in the dark,
and the orchard holds the shape of what was here,
and I carry its weight, though I cannot name it,
like a word I almost heard.
