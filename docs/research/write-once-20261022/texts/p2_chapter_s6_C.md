# Chapter One: The Dead Man's Mile

The first thing you learned in the conductor's chair, on any line cutting through the mountains, was that the weather announces itself before it arrives. First the light goes wrong. Then the silence shifts in tone, the way a held breath changes pitch. Then the wind picks up in the alders along the cut, and you know the storm is an hour out at best, and you check your gloves and your hat and your boots, and you go on about your rounds.

Forty-three years on the North Star Line, from the depot at Harrow all the way down to the lowland junction at Alder Creek, and Frank Calloway had learned to read a mountain the way a man reads a ledger. Every gully had a voice. Every switch at the junction had a temper. The creek crossing at Mile Fourteen flooded every year in April without exception, and the switchmen up in the high country always swore the same three words when the water came: *Don't let her sit.*

Forty-three years. And now he was seventy years old, and the line was gone, and the mountain didn't need reading anymore, just sitting and letting it do its thing.

The morning the body came out of the pines, the sky had been doing its thing all night, too. A front had been building off the Cascades since two in the afternoon, and by dawn the fog had crept up from the valley floor and settled in the trees like a held hand. Harrow, three thousand houses—no, three hundred and twelve houses, a church, a post office that had a coffee machine that worked most of the time, a hardware store that had been a hardware store since before the interstate was paved in—Harrow sat in the bowl of the mountain with the fog in it, and the only sound was the church bell at six and the distant cough of a logging truck up the ridge road.

Frank was on his back porch when the bell stopped. He'd been drinking coffee out of the good mug, the one his wife had given him the year before she died, and the fog had come in so thick that the porch lights at the next house were just pale smears in the white, and the pines on the east side looked like they were breathing. He set the mug down and went to look.

The fog wasn't fog. Or it was fog, but something was wrong with the shape of it. The trees on the north side of his property—the old growth he'd bought for the shade and the silence—had a hole in them. A dark place. And in the middle of that dark place, something was moving.

Not a deer. Not a man with a dog. It was moving slow, and it was low, and it was *wrong*.

He should have gone to bed. He should have put the kettle back on and called his sister in Ellensburg and told her he'd seen a strange thing in the trees and probably not slept well that night, the way you say it about a dream, the way you say it about a thing that isn't real. He was seventy. His knees clicked when it rained, which was most of the time in this valley. His heart had a flutter that the doctor at the clinic in town said was "within parameters" in a way that made you want to put your head in your hands.

He went anyway.

The path behind his property had been a logging road for forty years and a hiking trail for the last twelve, since the Timberline Group bought the east slope and turned the road into a trail and put a little sign at the bottom that said *Harrow Overlook Trail – 4 miles to summit – Please stay on the path*. Frank knew every rock on that path. He'd walked it the first week he got the house, three years after the line shut down, the first spring that the cold didn't come until October because of what the newspapers called "shifting patterns" and he just called the old way of the mountains forgetting its own schedule.

He reached the pines in about two minutes, the way he always did, past the split oak with the lightning scar, past the boulder where the marmots dug in July. The path narrowed where the trees closed over it, and the fog here was thicker, and the leaves on the ground were so wet they squelched under his boots in a way that was almost like the ground was breathing.

And there, in the middle of the path, three yards from the split oak, was a man.

He was on his back. Facing up. In the pale grey light filtering through the canopy, Frank could see the colour of him was wrong, the way you know a colour is wrong before you can name it. The shirt was dark, a work shirt, and the collar was up around the neck, and the face was... Frank stood still for a long time. The fog moved. The leaves on the ground made small sounds. Somewhere a bird was calling, a single note, thin and high, and then it stopped, as if it had remembered what it was looking at.

The face was turned toward the sky, and the eyes were open, and the lips were pulled back in a way that was not a smile and was not a scream but was something in between that Frank did not have a name for. The man's hands were at his sides, and the fingers were spread, and the dirt under the nails was dark and wet, not the brown dirt of the hillside but a darker red-brown, and the man's shoes were laced up and clean, the way you lace them in the morning and don't unlace them unless you're going to walk a long way and you're going to be gone a long time and you don't know where you're going to be.

Frank stood there. The fog thickened. The bird did not call again.

He did not scream. He did not call out. He was a train conductor, not a movie actor, and he understood that in the conductor's chair, when something goes wrong, you do not panic, you assess, you call for help, and you do not stop moving until the thing is dealt with. He had seen bad things on that line. A freight derailment in '87 that killed two men and broke his spine in a way the doctors couldn't fully fix. A woman walking the track at the Alder Creek junction in a blizzard, half-dead, half-mad, the train having just passed her, the engine light catching her face as the cab passed. He had carried her down the grade to the switch house and held her hands while the ambulance came. He had not screamed then. He would not scream now.

He pulled out his phone. It rang once on the first try. The number he'd saved under *Sheriff – Dale* had been his wife's number before it was Dale's, and sometimes it still rang through to the voicemail that said *This is the Calloway residence, please leave a message*, and Frank, standing in the pines with dead fog and the smell of pine sap and the colour of a man's face he did not want to look at anymore, did not want to leave a message.

Second ring. Dale answered on the second ring.

"Hello?" Dale's voice was thick with sleep. "Yeah?"

"Something's on the Overlook trail," Frank said. His voice was flat. The voice he used when there was a problem on the line and he had to tell the dispatch office that the schedule was going to be late and the passengers were going to be unhappy. "Past the split oak. About a hundred yards. I think he's dead."

A long pause. Then Dale said, "Frank. Are you—"

"I'm not going anywhere. I'm going to stand here until the light gets better and I'm going to make sure the path is clear behind me. If you need me to talk you through something I'll do it. But I'm not moving that man. I'm not touching that man."

Another pause. "How old do you think he is?"

"Thirty. Maybe thirty-five. Work shirt. Clean shoes. He was walking. He was walking and then he was not walking." A small sound came out of Frank, almost a breath, and he let it go. "Dale. Look, I need you to tell me who this is. I don't know him. I don't know him and I need to know. He's got a watch, Dale. A real watch, not the cheap kind, and the watch is still on his wrist and the hand is stopped, and if you can tell me who that is—"

The line crackled. A sound like a truck shifting gears. "Okay, okay. I'm calling it in. I'm calling the state. Hold on. Don't move. Don't touch anything. Frank, are you alone out there?"

"Yeah."

"Stay where you are. I'll be there in fifteen. Twenty at the worst, depending on the fog."

"I'll be here."

The line went dead. Frank stood in the fog with his phone in his hand and his coffee mug still warm in the other pocket of his coat, and the pines on all sides of him breathed their slow, green breath, and the dead man on the path looked up at the canopy like the canopy was the only thing left that could tell him something, and Frank thought, *Mile Fourteen*, because the pines there, the split oak, the boulder, the little bend where the trail ducked behind the trees—all of it was Mile Fourteen, the crossing where the creek used to flood and the men swore three words and never let the train sit, and he thought *Don't let her sit*, and he thought *don't let me sit here with this*, and he stood there, and the fog came in thicker, and the light did not come, and he waited.

In Harrow, the church bell rang at seven and no one went to mass. The coffee machine at the post office was broken, which meant the mail carrier had to use the little thermos he kept behind the counter, which meant he was going to be three minutes late, which meant he was going to be late on the *Harrow Ledger* delivery, which was not the first thing in the world anyone was going to be thinking about at seven in the morning when the fog was in the trees and the overpass was white and the road up to the summit looked like a river of nothing. But it was going to happen. It always happened. The thermos would be empty by eight-thirty and the mail would be late and nobody would care because the thing that was coming through the fog was bigger than the mail, and the thing that was coming through the fog was a man in a work shirt with clean shoes and a watch that had stopped, and the town of Harrow, three hundred and twelve houses, was going to learn what it meant to have a dead man in the pines, and it was going to learn it in the same way it had always learned anything, which was slowly, and all at once, and with the coffee getting cold.
