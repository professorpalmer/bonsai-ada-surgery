## final text

The lighthouse stood at the edge of the world, a jagged tooth of white stone biting into the sky. For fifty years, Elias had climbed its spiral staircase, polishing the glass, winding the clockwork, and watching the grey, churning ocean swallow the horizon. The ships were gone, replaced by the humming, iron leviathans that no longer needed his light. The lighthouse was a relic, a monument to a time when the sea was a monster and the keeper was its warden. But Elias did not mind the silence. He liked the rhythm of the wind and the smell of salt.

One evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon and painted the sky in bruises of purple and orange, Elias heard a knock.

It was not a knock on the door. It was a sound that seemed to vibrate inside his bones, a deep, resonant thud that shook the dust from the shelves. He turned off his lamp, assuming it was the wind pressing against the glass, but when he walked to the window, the sea was flat and still, like a sheet of hammered iron.

The sound came again, softer this time. *Tap, tap, tap.*

Elias froze. He lived alone. He had no visitors. Slowly, trembling, he walked to the heavy iron door at the base of the tower. He unlatched it, the metal groaning in the cold air. When he opened it, a young woman was standing on the stone steps. She wore a dress made of what looked like wet seaweed and kelp, and her hair was a tangle of silver foam. She was shivering, though the air was mild.

"I need help," she whispered. Her voice sounded like bubbles rising to the surface. "I can't get back to the surface."

Elias stepped aside, his heart hammering against his ribs. "You must be very tired, child. Come inside. There is tea."

She entered, and the temperature in the room plummeted. Elias shuddered, pulling his thick sweater tighter. "Who are you?" he asked, his voice barely a whisper.

"My name is Elara," she said. Her eyes were the pale, empty color of the deep sea. "I was a sailor. I fell into the water a century ago. I am... lost between the living and the drowned. I cannot return to the living world, and I cannot fully enter the deep. I am stuck in the in-between. The wind pushes me here, to the light. It is the only thing that calls to me."

Elias looked at her, truly looked at her. He realized then why he had kept the light burning all those years. It was not for the ships. It was for them.

"I have seen you before," Elias said softly. "Not in flesh, but in the glass. I thought it was a trick of the fog. You are the Drifters. We have been here for a thousand years, the ones who could not let go."

Elara nodded, tears of liquid light rolling down her cheeks. "Every night, the light guides us. But it does not save us. It only keeps us tethered. We are trapped in the beam, waiting for a release that never comes."

Elias looked around his small, lonely home. He had spent fifty years keeping a light that no one needed. He had been a guardian of the lost, a lighthouse for the souls of the drowned. He had not known.

"What must I do?" he asked. "How can I free you?"

Elara shook her head. "It is not a simple thing. To release us, the light must not just shine outward. It must shine *in*. It must be a door, not a beacon. You must open the light, Elias. You must let it pour into the sea, into the earth, into the very fabric of the world. But if you do that, the light will go out. The lighthouse will go dark. And you... you will be alone."

Elias looked at the great lens in the center of the room. It had been his companion for half a lifetime. To let it go dark meant the end of his purpose. But he looked at Elara, and at the countless other Drifters who gathered around her, their translucent forms flickering in the gloom. They were so sad, and so tired, and so longed for peace.

"Okay," Elias said. His voice was steady, but his hands were shaking. "Show me how."

Elara placed her cold, wet hand on his. She led him to the controls of the lens. With a series of complex, almost musical twists of brass gears, they redirected the beam. Instead of sweeping the horizon, the light focused, condensed, and then shattered, pouring out in a million tiny, radiant sparks.

The light flowed out of the lighthouse, over the water, across the sky. It touched Elara and the Drifters, and they gasped in unison. Their forms began to glow, turning from the grey of the drowned to the gold of the sun.

"Thank you, Keeper," Elara whispered. Her form began to dissolve into the light. "It is so warm."

One by one, the Drifters were consumed by the radiance. They laughed, a sound like wind chimes, and then they were gone. The sea, previously a sheet of iron, began to ripple and dance under the fading glow.

The lens clicked. The clockwork stopped. The light went out.

The lighthouse fell into darkness. Elias stood in the small, black room, listening to the sound of the ocean against the stone. For the first time in fifty years, he was truly alone. He walked to the window and looked out. The stars were out, brilliant and countless, scattering across the velvet of the sky.

He was lonely. But he was free. And he smiled.
