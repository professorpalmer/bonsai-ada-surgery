# Chapter One: The Shell of the World

In the beginning, there was no land. There was only the Sea of Heralds, a black-green expanse that stretched beyond the memory of any sailor. And then, after an age no one could name, the Turtle rose.

No one living had seen it rise. The old maps did not show the moment it happened, only the aftermath: the city of Tessaly, carved and walled and planted with ten thousand houses across a shell the size of a continent, while the sea rolled up and over its edges in eternal white fury.

Kael Venn had never seen the edge of the world. He lived in the Underhold, three levels down from the Market of Salt and Bone, where the city's foundations pressed closest to the living shell and the floorboards hummed with the slow pulse of the creature beneath them. The hum was so constant that children were born with it in their bones and died with it in their throats. The old women called it the Heartbeat of Tessaly. The engineers called it tectonic resonance. Kael called it the thing that kept the kitchen warm in winter, because he worked the heat-forge, and the forge ran warm when the pulse ran deep.

On this morning, the heartbeat stuttered.

He noticed it while he was stoking the bellows for the third forge. The hammer strikes of the Underhold's forges had a rhythm, and so did the hum beneath them, and for three generations the two had walked side by side. Now, the hammer fell in time and the hum arrived a half-beat late. Kael set his tongs down. He pressed his palm to the floor.

The turtle was sleeping badly.

The market above was already awake, and the smell of frying kelp and charred fish oil drifted down through the vent shafts, a thick greasy perfume that clung to your hair and your clothes. Kael climbed the spiral stairs two at a time, his work gloves still black with soot. The Market of Salt and Bone was a warren of stalls carved from the inner ribs of the shell, their arches curving over like the bones of some second, smaller creature. Fishwives bellowed. A tinker juggled three brass coins while selling them. A bard with a broken jaw played a lute with his teeth.

And everywhere, people were looking up.

That was the strange thing. Tessaly had no sky. For four hundred years, the Underhold citizens had lived in a city whose roof was the underside of the world itself, a vaulted darkness studded with the city's own lamps. No one looked up at the roof the way people on a flat ground look up at the sun. But this morning, the lamps were not the point.

A patch of the vault above the central fountain had gone clear.

Not broken. Not cracked. *Clear.* Through it, for a few hundred feet around the fountain, the stone was transparent, and beyond it moved something vast and slow and impossibly old. An eye. The size of a temple, golden and slit-pupiled, blinked once above them all, and the market fell silent in the way that only a full crowd can fall silent: not all at once, but in a wave, stall after stall, voice after voice.

Kael stood in the middle of the fountain and felt the old fear that belonged to every Underhold child before they could speak: the fear of what lay above the roof.

The Eye stared. The market held its breath.

Then, impossibly, gently, a scale the size of a district shifted, and the clear patch sealed shut again. The lamps flickered. The fishwives resumed. The tinker's coins clinked. A child asked her mother if the Turtle was angry, and the mother said it was only hungry, and the child said it never used to be hungry before.

It had been a hundred years since the hunger was a rumor. Now it was a fact, written in the silence.

By midmorning, the city had organized itself around the fear. The Guild of Scale-watchers, a company of tower-keepers who lived at the highest arches and spent their days polishing the underside of the roof for some reason no one could explain, lowered their lanterns to signal an all-circuit. The Archon's ship slid out of the Dry Dock and cut through the harbor of the central cistern, its white sails catching the forge-light, and every face in the Market turned toward it.

Kael watched from the edge of the fountain with soot still on his hands. He was twenty-two, lean in the way of forge-work, with a burn scar running from his wrist to his elbow that the city's healers had once called "a promise from the deep." He had no ambition beyond keeping the forges warm and his sister Maren out of the debt collectors' hands. He had never climbed above the Underhold on purpose. He had no reason to.

The Archon's ship had not gone up. It had gone *forward*, toward the city's edge, where the old stories said the shell ended and the sea began.

That was an hour away by foot, through the Nine Wards, past the salt farms, past the lighthouse that had no light, to the Breakwater.

Kael's heart gave a strange little knock, in time with the delayed hum beneath the floor. He picked up his satchel. He thought of Maren, sleeping in their attic room with the leaky window. He thought of the forge, which would be cold by nightfall if he did not tend it. He thought of the Eye.

He had not meant to follow.

By the time the crowd reached the Breakwater, Kael was already climbing.

The shell curved upward at the city's edge, its surface the color of wet charcoal, and the wind that came from beyond it tasted of iron and salt and something sweet, like old flowers left in the sun. Kael climbed the rope ladders that the Scale-watchers had long since abandoned, his soot-stained fingers finding holds in the shell's grooves. Below him, the city of Tessaly shrank to a painted hill, and beyond him the sea stretched out black and endless, and for the first time in his life he understood what the Underhold songs had meant when they sang of the world's underside.

They had been right.

They had all been climbing from beneath a world.
