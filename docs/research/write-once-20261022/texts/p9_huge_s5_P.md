## final text

The city of Oakhaven did not have streets, but strata.

It was built into the sides of a colossal, geologic scar in the earth, a vertical canyon where the walls were lined with thousands of dwellings, hanging like barnacles on the ribcage of the world. We called it the Scar, and we lived in the ribs of it. The air grew thinner the further up you went, but it was also cleaner. Down in the Dregs, where the sun never touched, the air tasted of rust and ozone.

I lived in Stratum 404, the mid-levels, where the light was a permanent, bruised amber. It was the height of my existence, both literally and metaphorically. My office was small, a box of polished wood and brass, tucked away behind a heavy iron door. It was a private practice. I was an Archivist.

In Oakhaven, memories were not just stories. They were currency. They were the literal fuel that kept the city’s massive atmospheric scrubbers humming and the lights burning in the lower strata. The rich up top hoarded their happiest, most vivid recollections, bottling them and saving them for decades, while the poor below were forced to sell their happiest days just to keep warm.

I was not rich. I was not poor. I was a middleman. I appraised memories. I determined their "Vividness Rating"—a measure of emotional weight, sensory density, and narrative coherence. A simple afternoon of eating an apple might be worth a few credits. A first kiss, a birth, a moment of pure, unadulterated grief—these were worth fortunes. But I had a secret, a flaw in my own composition, which I had learned long ago not to discuss with clients.

I was running out of self.

***

The rain in Oakhaven didn't fall; it was pumped. It was recycled from the condensation of the great atmospheric scrubbers, a thin, grey mist that clung to the skin and dripped from the eaves of the strata above. I was standing at my window, watching the water slide down the glass, when the buzzer on my door vibrated.

It was a high-pitched, urgent trill. I had set it to that tone only for the emergencies.

I wiped the glass with my sleeve and peered out into the narrow walkway that served as my street. A figure was crouching in the shadows, hooded in a patchwork of grey fabric that was too heavy for the weather. They were breathing hard, a mist forming in front of them, a testament to the cold that lived in the mid-levels.

I opened the door. The woman inside was small, sharp-featured, with a face that looked like it had been carved from the same stone as the canyon walls. She was soaked to the bone, her hair plastered to her skull.

"You have an appointment?" I asked, my voice flat.

"I have an emergency," she said. Her voice was a rasp, like dry leaves skittering over pavement. "My name is Elara Vane. I’m here to sell."

I looked at her. She was trembling. Not from the cold, but from something else. Something internal.

"We deal in appraisals, Ms. Vane," I said. "Bring your memory."

She nodded, and from the inside of her coat, she produced a small, glass vial. It was the standard size for a single memory, a "drop." But the liquid inside was thick, almost viscous, swirling with a deep, murky violet hue.

"What is this?" I asked, leaning closer.

"My brother’s death," she whispered.

I flinched. That was a dangerous kind of trade. Grief was heavy. It had a lot of weight to it, and it often carried a lot of baggage. But it was also a high-vividness asset. The emotional resonance of loss was one of the strongest fuels in the city. The upper strata loved their tragedies, consuming them like sweet wine.

"I’m not here to sell it for money," she said.

I stared at her. "Then why are you here?"

"I need you to buy it. But I don’t want credits." She held the vial out. "I want a favor."

"A favor is not a standard currency."

"Can you hold it?" she asked. "Can you store it for me? Just for a few days? I need to buy a specific kind of memory first. I need a 'First Memory' from someone of high rank. I need to buy a key, Archivist. A key to the Vault at the top."

The mention of the Vault made my blood run cold. The Grand Archive. It was the heart of Oakhaven, a vast, humming machine where the memories of the elite were stored. To break into the Vault was not just a crime; it was a myth, a story told to children to keep them from the upper strata.

"You’re asking me to steal for you," I said.

"I’m asking you to help a desperate woman," she said, her eyes locking onto mine. "And I’m offering you the one thing I have that’s worth more than the sum of all my other days. The most vivid, the most painful, the most *real* thing I own."

I looked at the vial again. The violet liquid swirled, and for a second, I saw something in it. A flash. A shadow. A small hand reaching out in the dark.

"Come in," I said.

***

Elara Vane had a story of her own.

She sat at the small table in my office, her hands wrapped around a mug of hot broth that I had brought her. She didn't drink it. She just held it to keep the cold from her fingers.

"Tell me," I said.

"I was twelve," she started. "My brother, Kael, was ten. We lived in the Dregs, Archivist. Stratum 12. It’s where the air scrubbers dump their waste. The water there is black, and the walls sweat. We ate the algae that grew on the pipes. It was sour, and it made you sick, but it filled your stomach."

She paused.

"We had nothing. Nothing at all. Just each other."

I nodded. I had seen this type. The Dreg-born were a rare breed. They were resilient, hard to break, but they carried a weight that the upper-level folk never could. They had lived their whole lives in the dark, and they had a fierce, defensive love for each other that came from that survival.

"My mother was sick," Elara continued. "She had a cough that sounded like a broken bell. It would wake us up at night. I would hold her head while Kael slept in the next alcove. We would count the minutes until the air scrubbers cycled. We knew when the good air was coming, and we held our breath during the bad cycles. It was a game. If you held your breath too long, you passed out. If you passed out, the bad air got you. We would wake up, gasping, and our mother would be there, coughing, smiling, telling us we had survived another round."

I felt a chill run down my spine. I had appraised many memories. I had seen love, hate, joy, and terror. But the sheer density of this story, the way it was built on layers of small, painful moments, was unusual.

"She died in the bad air," Elara said. Her voice didn't waver. "It was a Tuesday. I remember it was Tuesday because Kael had a bruise on his arm that was the shape of a Tuesday. He had fallen off a pipe. I was twelve, and I was so angry with him for being alive when our mother wasn’t. I remember the exact shade of the bruise. It was purple, and then green."

I closed my eyes. I was seeing it too. It was a powerful memory. It was a "heavy" one. The vividness rating would be off the charts.

"And then?" I asked.

"And then Kael and I had to survive without her. And we did. For three years. We kept going. But then, the sickness came back. Not to us, but to the city. The air filters started to fail. The upper strata sent down 'Cleansers'—men with white coats and gas masks. They came for the Dregs. They said we were a 'hazard.' They took Kael first."

Elara’s hands were shaking now.

"He was ten. He didn't understand what a Cleanser was. He thought they were going to take him to the hospital. I ran after them. I ran up the strata, climbing the pipes, the ladders, the anything I could find. I climbed for hours. My hands were bleeding. And I saw him. They had him in a van. I saw his face through the glass. He looked so small. And then the van doors closed, and the lights in the van went off. And I have never seen him again."

I let the silence fill the room. It was heavy, a physical thing.

"So," I said softly. "You want to sell his death. To buy a key to the Vault."

"I want to be up there," Elara said. "I want to be where they live. I want to be where they have air that doesn't taste like rust. I want to be where they are safe. And I want to know if he’s alive. If he’s in the Vault, maybe he’s in there. Maybe I can save him. Or at least, I can see his face. I need to know if he’s dead."

I looked at the vial in my hand. It was warm. I could feel the pulse of it, a low thrumming against the glass. It was the most powerful thing I had ever held.

"Ms. Vane," I said. "I am an Archivist. I deal in appraisals. I don't deal in favors. I don't deal in theft."

"Then you are a liar," she said, her voice rising. "You’re an archivist. You know where every memory in this city is stored. You have the codes. You have the access. You're sitting in Stratum 404, drinking broth, and you have the keys to the city. You're just too cowardly to use them."

I stared at her. She was right. It was a dangerous accusation, but it was a true one.

"I am not a thief," I said, my voice hard.

"Then what are you?"

I didn't answer. I just looked at the vial. And in that moment, I saw it. The flash I had seen before returned, sharper this time.

I saw a room. A large, circular room with walls made of glass. Inside the glass were thousands of vials, glowing with different colors. I saw myself, standing in that room. I saw my hands, younger, stronger, holding a pen. I saw a name on a file.

*Jorin Vale. Archivist.*

I had a memory of that room. But it was not a memory of the Vault. It was a memory of my office.

I realized, with a jolt of horror, that I had been a client.

***

The name in the file was my own.

I pulled the file from the cabinet under my desk. It was a thin, brass folder, the kind the old archives used before the digital transition. It was labeled with my name, in my own handwriting.

I opened it. Inside was a single sheet of paper.

*Client: Jorin Vale.*
*Date: 2145.*
*Item: Childhood, Pre-Scar. Age 0-14.*
*Vividness Rating: 9.8 (Exceptional).*
*Notes: Client wishes to offload all pre-Scar memories to avoid 'Emotional Congestion' in preparation for the high-pressure environment of the Grand Archive. All sensory details from the pre-Scar era to be removed from consciousness. Client to be compensated in credits and access to the upper strata.*

My hands were shaking. I looked at the date. 2145. That was twenty years ago. I was twenty-five then. I had just started working at the Grand Archive.

I remembered the process. I remembered the chair. I remembered the cold metal on my scalp. I remembered the feeling of a part of me being peeled away, like a layer of skin. I had been so young, so hungry for success, so desperate to be part of the elite. I had thought I was trading away a childhood I didn't even like. I had thought it was a bargain. A way to buy my way up.

But I had been wrong. I had sold the very foundation of who I was.

And now, I was an empty shell. A vessel. I had no childhood, no parents, no hometown. I had no name. I was just a job.

I looked at Elara. She was watching me, her eyes wide.

"Who am I?" I asked, my voice sounding strange in my own ears.

"You are Jorin Vale," she said softly. "But you're not just Jorin Vale. You are the Archivist. You are the one who sold the city’s heart."

I closed the file. The room felt smaller. The amber light seemed harsher.

"Why?" I asked.

"You are afraid," Elara said. "You are afraid that if you remember, you will be weak. You are afraid that if you are weak, you will fall. So you sold your weakness. But you sold it to the wrong people, Archivist. You sold it to the Broker."

The Broker. I knew the name. I had heard it whispered in the lower strata. He was the man who owned the Grand Archive. The man who controlled the flow of memories in Oakhaven.

I had sold my childhood to him. And now, he had the keys to my life.

***

I walked out of my office and into the amber-lit walkway. The mist was heavier now, or maybe I was just more sensitive to it. I could feel the weight of my own absence. I felt hollow.

I went to the lower strata. I didn't know why. I just knew I had to go there. I had to find something.

I took the lift down, the metal box rattling as it descended through the dark. The lights flickered and died as we passed Stratum 200. I was in the Dregs now. The air was thick and suffocating. I could taste the rust in my mouth.

I walked through the narrow alleys, my shoes splashing in the black water. I didn't know where I was going. I just followed a feeling. A pull in my chest, like a hook.

I found a small shop. It was tucked away in a corner, barely visible. A sign above the door read: *The Broker's Store.*

I pushed the door open. The bell above it gave a sharp, lonely chime.

Inside, the shop was dim. Shelves lined the walls, covered in thousands of glass vials. They glowed with various colors: red, blue, green, purple. It was a beautiful and horrifying sight. I was surrounded by people's souls.

A man was sitting at the counter. He was tall, thin, with a face like a knife. He wore a white coat that looked clean, too clean, against the grime of the shop.

"Archivist Vale," he said. His voice was smooth, like silk. "You look well."

"You have my childhood," I said.

"I have a great many things," he said. "You came to talk about it."

"I came to buy it back."

He laughed. It was a dry, humorless sound. "Your childhood? Jorin, my dear boy, your childhood is gone. I sold it to a man in Stratum 1. He wanted a 'pre-Scar' memory for his collection. It is now a part of him."

I felt a wave of nausea.

"Then give me the key to the Vault," I said.

"The Vault," he said. "Ah. You are thinking of Elara Vane. The little thief. I like her. She has potential. She has a lot of vivid grief. But I will not give you the key."

"Why?" I asked.

"Because I am not a thief," he said, and the irony of his words hung in the air. "I am a steward. I manage the city. And the city needs balance."

"What kind of balance?" I asked.

"The balance of pain," he said. "The city runs on pain, Jorin. The Dregs sell their grief, the mid-levels sell their love, the top levels sell their joy. It is a cycle. If you break the cycle, the city collapses."

I looked at the vials on the shelves. I saw a red one. I knew what it was. I had sold my mother’s death. I saw a blue one. I had sold my first kiss. I had sold my name. I had sold everything that made me human.

"I am not human," I said.

"No," the Broker said. "You are a machine. A very efficient one. And you are still useful."

He handed me a key.

I stared at it.

"Go," he said. "Find her. Find the girl with the violet memory. She is in Stratum 1. I will be watching."

I took the key and ran.

***

I ran up the strata, the key burning in my hand. I didn't know how I found Stratum 1. I didn't know how I found Elara Vane. I just knew I had to be there.

I burst into a small, cramped room in the highest stratum. The air was sweet and clean, smelling of flowers and rain. It was a stark contrast to the Dregs.

Elara Vane was sitting on a bed, her head in her hands. She was weeping.

"Elara," I said.

She looked up. Her eyes were red.

"You're late," she said.

"I have the key," I said. "The Broker gave it to me."

"Why?" she asked.

"I don't know," I said. "He said I was a machine."

She stood up. "What did you do? Did you sell your childhood?"

I nodded.

She looked at me, and for the first time, I saw something like pity in her eyes.

"Come on," she said. "We have to go."

I followed her out into the sunlight. For the first time in my life, I saw the sun. It was a blinding, white light, and it hurt my eyes. I blinked, and the tears came.

We went to the Vault.

The Vault was not a building. It was a machine. A massive, humming, metallic beast that sat in the center of the upper strata. It was covered in pipes and cables, and the air around it was thick with the ozone smell of charged energy.

The doors were open.

We walked inside.

It was a vast, circular chamber. The walls were lined with thousands of vials, glowing with a soft, pulsing light. It was a sea of memories. I saw a blue one. I saw a red one. I saw a golden one, pulsing with a warm, rhythmic light.

And there, in the center, was a single vial. It was clear, almost invisible. It was sitting on a pedestal of white marble.

I walked up to it.

"Kael," I whispered.

Elara stood beside me. Her face was pale.

"I don't know if he's inside," I said.

"We have to try," she said.

I lifted the vial. It was warm. I could feel the pulse of it. I looked into the glass.

And I saw him.

Kael was ten years old. He was standing in a white room. His eyes were wide. He was looking at me.

"Jorin?" he said. "Why is the light so bright?"

I didn't answer. I just watched him. He was smiling. He was afraid, but he was smiling.

"Who is that?" I asked Elara.

"It's me," I said.

"What?"

"I am the one who sold him," I said. "I sold him to the Broker. I sold his life."

Elara’s eyes widened. "That's a lie."

"I'm not a liar," I said. "I'm a machine. And I sold his life to buy my career."

Elara grabbed the vial from my hand. She held it up to her face. "This is not true. This is a fabrication. You are broken, Jorin. Your mind is broken. You are not who you say you are."

I looked at the vial. I saw Kael’s face. I saw his smile. And I saw something else. I saw a door. A dark, iron door, at the end of a long hallway.

I remembered.

I remembered the day I sold Kael’s life. I was twenty-five. I was hungry for success. I had met the Broker. I had offered him a memory. But I didn't have one that was good enough. So I had taken Kael. I had taken the only person in this world who was good enough. I had sold him.

And the Broker had given me a key. A key to the upper strata. A key to success.

I had done it. I had sold my brother's life to save myself.

I felt the weight of it. It was the heaviest thing I had ever carried.

"Give it back," I said.

Elara looked at me. "Give what back?"

"The key," I said. "The Broker gave it to you. He wants you to take it."

Elara’s eyes were wide. "He's lying to me. He's using me."

I took the key from her. "You are right. He is lying. He is using us."

I walked to the center of the chamber. The sea of memories swirled around me. I could see my life in them. I could see my brother’s life. I could see the city’s life.

I placed the vial of Kael’s memory on the pedestal.

And I broke it.

The glass shattered. The light from the vial burst out, a blinding, white flash that filled the room. I felt a wave of energy wash over me. I felt my childhood rush back into me. I felt my name. I felt my mother’s face. I felt my brother’s laugh.

And I felt the pain.

It was a physical blow. I fell to my knees. I gasped, and the air in the chamber felt thin.

I looked up. Elara was standing there. She was weeping.

"What did you do?" she asked.

"I bought back my soul," I said.

The lights in the chamber flickered. The humming of the machine stopped.

The doors began to close.

***

I ran out of the Vault. I ran into the sunlight.

Elara ran beside me.

"The Broker," she said. "He's coming. He knows."

I looked at the sky. It was clear, blue, and vast. It was the first time I had ever seen it without the amber tint of the scrubbers.

I ran.

I ran up the strata, running as fast as my legs would carry me. I could hear the sound of the lift behind me, a heavy, metallic thud as it ascended.

We reached the top. Stratum 1.

We stood on a platform that overlooked the entire city. The Scar stretched out before us, a vast, vertical canyon. It was beautiful. It was terrible. It was home.

The lift doors opened.

The Broker stepped out. He was alone. He looked at us, and he smiled.

"You've made a mess," he said.

"I've broken the cycle," I said.

The Broker’s smile faltered. "You've broken the machine."

He reached into his coat and pulled out a vial. It was filled with a swirling, black liquid.

"Give me the key," he said.

I looked at the key in my hand. It was the key to the Vault. The key to my own life.

I looked at Elara. She was looking at me with a fierce, desperate hope.

I looked at the city. I looked at the Dregs, the mid-levels, the upper strata. I looked at the people who were selling their memories to survive.

I realized that I couldn't give the key to the Broker. I couldn't give it to anyone.

I crushed the key in my hand. The metal bent, and the sharp edges cut into my skin. I bled, and the blood dripped onto the key.

The Broker’s face changed. His smile vanished. His eyes went wide.

"You idiot," he said. "You've destroyed the only thing that keeps this city together."

"I've freed it," I said.

The Broker looked at the key in my hand. He looked at the broken metal. He looked at the city below.

And he began to laugh.

It was a horrible sound. It echoed through the canyon. It was the sound of a man who had lost his world.

He turned and walked back to the lift.

"Goodbye, Jorin," he said. "Goodbye, Elara."

The lift doors closed.

We stood alone on the platform.

The wind blew across the city. It was cold, but it was clean. It smelled of rain and flowers.

I looked at Elara. "Are you going to sell your memory?" she asked.

"No," I said. "I'm going to keep it."

She looked at me. "And me?"

"You don't have to sell anything," I said. "I have the code to the Archive. I can unlock the vault. I can give people their memories back."

She smiled. It was a small, fragile smile. But it was real.

"Then let's get to work," she said.

I looked at the city. I looked at the thousands of glowing lights in the windows. I looked at the people who were selling their souls for a few credits.

I took a breath. It was clean. It was sweet. It tasted like freedom.

We began to climb.
