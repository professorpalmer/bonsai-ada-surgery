By the time Silas Wren reached the last stair of the lighthouse, the sea had already begun to argue with the rocks. He climbed with the slowness of a man whose knees had learned the steps better than his mind, hand on the rail, breath making small clouds in the lamp room’s cold. Gull’s Point stood alone where the Atlantic opened into the Channel, a white tower on a black spine of rock, and for eleven years Silas had been its only shadow.

The light turned at night. The logbook asked for wind, temperature, fuel, and the condition of the lens. On quiet days it asked for nothing else. On the first morning of November, it asked for a letter.

It lay on the landing outside his door, a plain envelope the color of weak tea, addressed in a child’s careful hand: The Keeper, Gull’s Point Lighthouse. There was no postmark, no stamp, no return address. When he opened it, the paper smelled of rain and iron, as though it had come from inside the fog.

My name is June, the letter said. I am not yet. I am writing to you before I am born.

Silas read it three times. Then he walked down to the village, where the postman, a thin man named Mr. Ayles, was wiping salt from the postbox. A letter for you, he said, as if the sea had only delivered it late. Which one? Silas held up the envelope. Ayles looked, then went pale. I didn’t post it, he said. There is no June in the village. No Hart, either.

Silas returned to the lighthouse and set the letter on the table beside the logbook. He considered writing a reply. The ink was still wet. By noon it had dried without smearing, and the handwriting had grown sharper, as if the girl had thought better of the message while he was gone.

A new sentence had appeared under the first. If you let the light go out on the fourteenth, I will never be.

He laughed at first. The light never went out. That was the one promise that held the island together. But that night, while trimming the wick, he heard the lamp room speak. Not words, exactly. The glass lens murmured, the oil drum ticked, and the wind found a crack in the tower’s wall and sang in a voice that might have been a child's. Silas climbed higher until he could see the sea like a black map. Far out, something moved. A buoy? A gull? His hand closed around the emergency flare, though he had no reason to throw it.

Over the next few days, the letters multiplied. They arrived one each morning, always the same envelope, always with the scent of rain. Some were full; some were only a single line.

The fog bell is stuck on the third note. Fix it before Thursday.
My mother mends your nets on Tuesdays. Her hands are red from the cold.
Do not tell them I am not yet. It will frighten them.

Silas began to notice things he had previously ignored. Mrs. Hart, who mended his nets, had a swell in her waist and a way of pressing her palm to it when she thought no one was looking. Her husband, Maren, was away on the fisher Sula. The innkeeper said Maren would be home in two weeks. Silas wrote their names in the margin of the logbook, though the logbook had no place for them.

At night, when the light swept the water, he thought he saw the girl’s face in the beam. Not a ghost. A child with dark hair and a scar on her chin, older than she had any right to be. He stopped seeing her after the third letter, but by then the idea had taken root.

The fourteenth arrived with a sky the color of a bruise. The barometer fell so fast the windows seemed to bow inward. Silas checked the lamp four times before dark. He filled the oil drum, cleaned the lens, wound the clockwork, and left the service door unlocked. He told himself it was practical. A storm might take the lock. A storm might take a man.

He lit the light at six. By eight the sea had become a thing with teeth. The light threw its beam over black water and found nothing for a long while. Then, a quarter mile out, something broke the surface: a hull, low and black, with a man clinging to it.

Silas ran for the dinghy. The sea threw it against the rock twice before he could launch it. His arms burned. His lungs filled with cold. The man was Maren Hart. His coat was soaked, his face grey, but his eyes were open.

When Silas pulled him aboard, Maren gripped his hand so hard the knuckles hurt. I need to find my daughter, Maren said. Not yet, I think.

Silas did not understand. Then the lighthouse lamp gave a lurch. A crack ran down the tower’s wall. The light wavered, and for one terrible second the beam went out.

The letters came to him in that second, all at once. Not paper, but a voice: I am not yet. Save her. Save the light.

He threw himself at the emergency crank. The lamp shuddered, caught, and swung back across the water. Maren’s eyes widened. Silas pulled him closer. The storm roared above them, but the light held.

Afterward, the storm spent itself. By morning the sea was only grey and patient, and Maren lay on the kitchen floor with a blanket around his shoulders. Mrs. Hart arrived by rowboat, her face wet with more than rain. She held Silas’s hand for a long moment, then turned to her husband and said nothing. She only put her palm against her swollen stomach and whispered, I did not know if she was still coming.

Silas looked at the letters on the table. They were gone.

The next day, a single envelope appeared where the last had been. The handwriting was the same, but smaller, as if the writer had used the tip of a pencil. I am not yet. I will be soon.

Silas did not sleep that night. He stood at the lamp room window and watched the light turn. For the first time in eleven years, the turning felt less like a duty and more like a promise. Below, in the village, the Hart cottage window was lit. At two in the morning, he heard a cry, thin and clear, carried up by the wind. He did not know if it was a gull or a child. The next morning, Mrs. Hart stood at the lighthouse door with a bundle wrapped in a red shawl.

She looked at Silas for a long time before she spoke.

Her name is June, she said. She has your letter.

Silas looked at the bundle. The baby’s hand, tiny and closed, rested on the paper. On the paper was a new sentence, in a child’s careful hand:

Dear Mr. Wren. I am not yet.

Years passed. The lighthouse was automated, then sold, then rebuilt into a museum with glass windows and a plaque for old men who had once kept light. Silas stayed on, though no one asked him to. The villagers brought his tea and asked after the light with the same ritual as they asked after the weather.

When June turned five, she came up the lighthouse stairs with her mother’s hand in hers. Her hair was dark. Her chin was smooth. She carried a blue envelope, the same color as weak tea, and she did not open it.

I know why you are here, she said. She stood under the lamp and looked up, though she was too small to reach the lens. I am not yet, she said. I am not yet.

Silas knelt. His knees ached. He took the envelope from her small fingers and felt, beneath the paper, the faint warmth of a child who had not been born.

I know, he said.

They opened it together. Inside, in June’s neat hand, was a single line:

Thank you for keeping the light. I was not yet.

Afterward, they sat in silence while the lamp turned. Outside, the sea made small, respectful sounds against the rocks. The museum visitors were far below, laughing at a model boat. June placed her hand over the paper as if it were a sleeping bird. Silas thought of every night he had kept the light, and he understood at last that the light had never been only for ships. It had been for the child not yet, the name not yet, the door not yet opened.

The lamp turned once more, and for a moment the beam passed through June’s face, through the letters, through the years, until all that remained was light, patient and sure. It held, and the dark stayed outside for now.
