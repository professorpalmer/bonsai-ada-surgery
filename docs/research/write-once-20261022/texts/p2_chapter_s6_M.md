# Chapter One: The 6:15 to Nowhere

The first thing Cole Marren noticed every morning was the temperature. Not the temperature, exactly—the way it pressed against the glass of his back porch door like a palm testing for a pulse. In October, that was the particular cold that smelled of woodsmoke and turned the creek below the town into a silver wire between the firs.

He stood with his coffee in both hands, reading the valley the way he had read tracks for twenty-two years: left-to-right, counting the gaps, finding the rhythm underneath the noise. The smoke from the Delgado house rose in a lazy column. The Henderson kids had left the screen door open on their porch, which meant they were up, which meant school would start in forty minutes, which meant the town would be half-asleep and half-arguing by six-thirty. Nothing was out of order. Nothing was missing.

Nothing was *wrong*.

He took a sip. The coffee was too hot, the way it always was. He had stopped adjusting for that years ago, in the long silence after the last run. His knees ached where the concrete of the platform used to vibrate through them. He was sixty-four. His hair had gone the color of the gravel along the old line—grey with flecks of rust—and he kept it short because it was easier than pretending he was someone else's age.

The Hollow, as the postmark still called it, was not a town you would find on a map unless you were looking for a particular kind of lost thing. Two thousand feet of elevation, one two-lane road in and out, a mill that made paper that mostly went back out to the mill, and the old rail yard that had gone quiet in 1998 when the line was cut. Four miles of ballast and dead track. Four miles where the mountains pressed close enough to touch if you squinted. Cole had walked it most mornings for the last decade, not out of nostalgia—out of habit, out of the need to know that the world still ran on a schedule even when nothing ran on it.

He turned from the porch and started down the walk toward the yard. His boots made the same small *crack-crack-crack* against the flagstones. He counted the steps. Forty-three to the gate. He had counted them since 1973, and he still came up forty-three.

The yard gate was where he'd stopped it every morning for two thousand and forty-seven mornings. A rusted chain, a padlock he'd never used, a little swing that made a sound like a tongue clicking against teeth. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* He paused.

Not the gate. Something behind it.

He stood still. The coffee sloshed. The birds were at it again—crows on the upper ridge, a red-tailed hawk turning slow circles over the far slope. Normal weather sounds. Normal dead things moving through the world.

But there was something else. A low, metallic sound, almost beneath hearing. Like a coin dropped on a stone, repeated very slowly, very far away. He tilted his head. Listened the way a conductor listens for a wheel out of true on a track—half in the body, not in the ear.

The sound was coming from the old water tower.

The tower was at the far end of the yard, half a mile out on the spur, leaning a few degrees east in the way all the things in the Hollow leaned, as if the mountains were pulling them downhill. Cole had not walked past it in years. He'd stopped after the last run, stopped going where the work used to be. But the sound had got into his skull, small and sharp as a pin, and he found his legs walking before he'd made the decision.

The gravel crunching under his boots was louder than it should have been. He walked the same way he'd walked the platform every shift—shoulders loose, eyes level, not looking at the ground unless he needed to, because the ground told you the same thing the wheels did: where something had been, where something was going, whether the rhythm held.

The water tower was a steel cylinder the color of dried blood, its legs spread wide against the hillside, a walkway of catwalk and ladder clinging to the side. It had been decommissioned so long the paint was mostly dust. A few dead vines had grown into the seams.

Cole stopped thirty feet short of the ladder.

The sound was stronger here. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* And under it, a smell. Iron. Old blood, maybe, or rust, or both—up here, the line between them was thin as paper.

He looked up.

The walkway wrapped three-quarters of the way around the tower. At the far end, where the walkway met the platform at the top, there was something that was not a vine and was not a piece of the structure.

He looked again. Closer.

It was a dress. Blue. The color of the creek in August, maybe darker, or maybe that was the shadow. The fabric had been pulled up over the railing and was knotted at the top, the kind of knot a hand would make in a hurry, or maybe the kind a hand would make without a hurry at all, because the hand that made it had stopped working before the thought finished.

Cole's coffee had gone cold in his hands. He could feel it. He set the cup on the gravel with the careful precision of a man who has spent three decades setting a lantern on a platform where a train would come through at eighty-four miles an hour. *Steady. Steady. Don't let it slide.*

He looked at the body. He did not go up the ladder.

He stood there for a long time. The crows had gone quiet. The hawk had dropped out of view. The Hollow was very still, the way the Hollow got still when the weather turned, and the mountains seemed to lean in just a little, listening.

Cole Marren had walked the rail line for twenty-two years. He had seen derailments, and he had seen the morning after, the white flowers at the crosses and the family members sitting in the waiting room with their hands on their knees. He had seen men fall into the yard. He had seen what happened to people when the world went wrong at the speed of a thing built to move.

He had not, in all those years, seen this.

This was not an accident of speed. This was a choice. Someone had picked this spot. Someone had walked up the ladder. Someone had knotted that dress in a specific way and stepped off, or had pushed, and the only sound that came out of the whole operation was the *tick tick tick* of the fabric against the steel railing, wind in the dead leaves, the slow creak of the tower settling in the October cold.

Cole looked up. He looked at the ladder. He looked at the ground at the base of the tower, where the gravel was disturbed, where two pairs of footprints led up from the road and disappeared at the first rung.

He did not go up.

He walked back down the spur, slow, counting the gravel stones under his boots the way you count the ties on a track to know how far you've gone. Six hundred and twelve to the gate. He opened the gate. He did not close it behind him.

By the time he was on the main road, his phone was in his hand. He dialed the number for the county sheriff's office, not the local marshal—the local marshal was a cousin, and a cousin would have known him too well, would have asked how the knee was and what the weather was doing, and by the time the conversation got to the business the body would still be up there and the footprints still fresh and the wind might have started to blow and the evidence would be a suggestion rather than a fact.

The sheriff's office answered on the fourth ring. A woman. Young voice, probably the dispatcher.

"County Sheriff's Office, this is Daley."

"Hi. I need to report a body. There's a woman, or a person, hanging from the old water tower at the end of the rail yard. Off the north spur, about a mile past the Henderson place." He gave the name. "Yes, the one that used to be the line. The yard. The water tower at the far end. She's wearing a blue dress. I haven't gone up. I haven't touched anything."

A pause. "Sir, can you come down to the office and talk to me?"

"I'm not going to the office."

"Sir—"

"I'm going to stay right here on the road where I am. You send someone, and when they get up there they find what I found. And when they get back down the ladder, they don't touch the footprints. The footprints are the only thing that tells me where the other person came from."

Another pause. "Sir, the footprints. You don't know the person."

He did.

The footprints were size seven. Women's size seven, in the shape of a left-to-right gait, the right foot landing slightly ahead of the left, which meant she favored the left side and walked fast, which meant she was either in a hurry or she had been in a hurry her whole life. He'd seen that gait on the platform in the early nineties. He'd seen it in the doorway of the general store. He'd seen it in the line for the Friday market, where the town stood in a long, quiet line and nobody said anything and the whole valley smelled like apples and the conductor's whistle blew and you just waited for your turn.

The person in the blue dress. The person he'd seen on the platform.

"Shall I keep you on the line, sir?" the dispatcher asked.

He didn't answer. He was already walking, back toward the town, back toward the porch where the coffee had gone cold and the flagstones were forty-three steps to the gate and the crows were coming back to the upper ridge and the world was running on a schedule that something had just, very deliberately, broken.

He counted the steps to the gate. Thirty-two.

He stopped.

Thirty-two.

He stood on the gravel at the yard gate and counted again. The gravel was the same gravel. The flagstones were the same flagstones. The gate was the same gate.

Twenty-nine steps from the gate to the porch.

Thirty-two steps from the gate to the yard.

Forty-three steps from the porch to the gate.

He looked at the gate. He looked at the road. He looked at the water tower, half a mile out, where the blue dress was knotted at the railing like a small, bright, terrible question mark.

Forty-three.

The number had always been forty-three.

He walked the forty-three steps one more time.

Forty-three.

He went inside. He put a fresh pot of coffee on the stove. He stood in the kitchen and watched the water boil and listened to the Hollow, to the creek, to the smoke, to the Henderson kids starting to shout in the yard of their house, and he thought about the number of years he had counted that number and never once counted it wrong.

And he thought about the person in the blue dress, who had walked up a ladder and knotted a knot and stepped off into the thin October air, and how the last thing the wind had done was carry the sound of her breathing down to the gravel where a retired train conductor was counting steps and finding, for the first time in twenty-two years, that the rhythm had changed.

The coffee boiled. He poured it. It was too hot.

He drank it anyway.
