## final text

# The Two Ovens

The town of Calabrese smelled of two things at a time: salt spray, and bread.

It was a small place on the western coast, all crooked lanes and whitewashed walls, where the morning fog rolled in off the Atlantic and sat on the rooftops like a sheet that had forgotten to be taken off. The sea was the landlord here. Everyone else was a tenant.

On one side of the market square, in a building older than the square itself, with a door so low you had to duck under it, was the bakery of Marta Voss. On the other side, in a building newer than the square itself, with glass doors that squeaked, was the bakery of Elias Fenn.

They had been rivals since before they could agree on what a loaf was for.

Marta baked by the old method. Her sourdough had a starter named Nana, who had been alive in the kitchen since the 1970s and had outlived four owners of the building. The bread was dense, dark-crusted, faintly sour, and it kept for a week on a shelf. Marta's sign read: *Marta's — Baked the way your grandmother's bread was, not the way the magazines say it should be.*

Elias baked by the new method. His croissants were laminated to such precision that the layers could be counted on a macroscopic lens. His baguettes were long, thin, and crackled when they cooled. He imported flour from France and butter from Ireland, and he had a pastry chef on the payroll named Dev who spoke to the oven like it was a horse he was training. Elias's sign read: *Elias Fenn — Where Bread Meets the Future.*

Between the two buildings, in the middle of the square, was a stone fountain that had stopped working in 1983, and the two bakers had a standing, if unspoken, agreement: the fountain belonged to neither of them.

The first real argument between them happened the year the town was asked to choose a bread for the annual Harvest Festival. The mayor asked both of them to enter a loaf. They both entered a loaf.

Marta entered a country loaf, dark rye and wheat, with a sprig of rosemary in the top. It was humble. It was good. Elias entered a brioche, the kind with enough butter in it that it could be called a small crime. It was beautiful. It won.

The vote was nine to five in Elias's favor. Marta stood in the doorway of her bakery for a long time afterward, holding the empty tray, and she did not smile. She did not leave a note. She simply went back inside and fed Nana a little more rye, and the next morning her bread smelled stronger than it ever had before.

That was three years ago.

What had changed was that the festival had grown. What had not changed was that the two bakers still could not stand each other.

Every morning before the fog lifted, Marta would be at her counter, shaping loaves with hands that looked like they had been carved from the same wood as the kneading table. Elias would be behind his glass doors, in a white coat that cost more than Marta's kneading table, plating pastries that looked like they had been assembled by a surgeon. The regulars in the square knew both. The fishermen's wives bought Marta's bread. The tourists bought Elias's croissants. Nobody, it seemed, bought both.

And yet.

And yet, every Tuesday and Thursday at 6 a.m., a small woman with a blue apron would appear at both doors. Her name was Greta, and she was the owner of the old harbor bakery, the one on the end of the lane where the road fell away to the cliffs.

The harbor bakery was not doing well. Its ovens were old and temperamental, and its pastries were good but not good enough. Greta's daughter had left for a city and would not come back. Greta's husband was dead. The harbor bakery was, in the quiet way of all things that are not being replaced, dying.

Greta came to Marta first, every Tuesday and Thursday, and asked for a few loaves to sell at the harbor. She paid half. Marta did not ask why. She just gave the loaves.

Greta came to Elias next, and asked for a few croissants to sell. He paid half. Elias did not ask why. He just gave the croissants.

Neither of them knew what the other was doing. Or at least, neither of them had said so.

It was on a Thursday in November that the secret came out.

The storm came that night. Calabrese got storms, but not like that storm. It came out of the west with a fury that was almost personal, the kind of storm that makes the sea look like it is trying to get in. The wind tore a shingle off the roof of the harbor bakery by 10 p.m. The harbor bakery's main oven cracked at 11. The generator failed at midnight.

Marta was up, because Marta was always up. She heard the wind and went to her window and watched the rain come down in sheets. She thought of the harbor bakery. She thought of Greta. She put on her coat.

She did not go to the harbor. She went to Elias's door.

Elias opened it in his coat, and his face went through about three expressions in about two seconds.

"You can't," he said.

"I need to know if your ovens are working," Marta said.

"The storm—"

"I know. I'm not here for the storm. I'm here because Greta's bakery needs bread on Sunday, and I can't do it alone."

Elias stood in the doorway for a long time. The rain was coming down hard. His hair was wet.

"Why would I do that?" he asked.

Marta looked at him. "You would, because you gave her those croissants and she hasn't paid you back. And because you're a coward and you're going to be too proud to ask."

Elias did not say anything. He went inside and came back with a flashlight and a coat that had not been on the hook.

They went to the harbor.

The harbor bakery was half in dark. Greta was standing in the middle of the kitchen with her arms crossed, and the water from the cracked oven was pooling around her feet. She did not look surprised to see them. She looked tired in the way people who have been tired for a long time look tired.

"I don't want your bread," she said to Marta.

"I know," Marta said.

"I don't want his croissants."

"I know that too," Marta said.

Greta looked at Elias. He was standing in the doorway, trying not to look at her. He was failing.

"The generator's out," Greta said. "The oven's cracked. If I don't sell on Sunday, I'm closing."

"So what do you want from us?" Elias asked.

Greta looked at both of them. "I want you to bake my bread. My croissants. My everything. And I want you to do it together, because you're the only ones in this town who know how to do it well, and I can't be the one to tell you that."

They looked at each other. The rain was still coming in.

"We can't do this," Elias said.

"You can," Marta said.

"I can," Elias said, after a pause. "But if this goes wrong—"

"It's not going to go wrong," Marta said.

They worked.

They worked for six hours. They baked in the harbor bakery's second oven, the one that still held heat. They used Marta's rye and Elias's butter. They used Marta's shaping and Elias's lamination. They argued about the salt, and then about the temperature, and then about whether the loaves should be baked in the morning or the evening, and then about nothing at all, which was harder than arguing about anything.

Greta watched. She did not interfere. She brought them water and tea and, at some point, a plate of the harbor bakery's old-fashioned honey cakes, which were good and had not been eaten in a while.

When the last loaf came out of the oven, it was 3 a.m. The rain had stopped. The loaves were dark and golden, with a crust that cracked when you touched them. They were, to look at them, a compromise. They were, when you cut them, something else entirely.

They ate one together, standing in the kitchen, with butter and salt and no plates.

"It's good," Greta said.

"It's very good," Marta said.

"It's the best bread this place has ever made," Elias said.

Greta nodded. She did not say anything more. She did not need to.

On Sunday, the harbor bakery sold out. Not by evening. By afternoon. The line went all the way to the fountain and turned down the lane toward the cliffs. People stood in the cold and bought loaves and croissants and everything in between, and they ate them on the benches and on the steps and on the cobblestones, and the town of Calabrese smelled, for the first time in a long time, of both things at once.

Marta stood in the square with her arms crossed. Elias stood by the fountain with his hands in his pockets. They did not speak. They did not need to.

That evening, after the crowds had gone and the street lamps came on, Elias came to Marta's door. He was still in his coat. His hair was still wet.

He held a loaf under his arm.

"It's not a country loaf," he said.

"I know."

"It's not a brioche either."

"I know that too."

He set it on her counter. "Greta said you could have it."

Marta looked at the loaf. She looked at Elias.

"Thank you," she said.

"You're welcome," Elias said.

He turned to leave. Then he stopped.

"Next Tuesday," he said. "We're doing it again."

Marta nodded. "Next Tuesday," she said.

Elias went inside. He did not knock.

Marta stood in the doorway for a long time afterward. The loaf was still warm. She put on her coat. She went outside.

The fog was coming in. The sea was loud. And the town of Calabrese, with its two bakers and its one fountain and its one small harbor bakery, smelled, at last, of bread.
