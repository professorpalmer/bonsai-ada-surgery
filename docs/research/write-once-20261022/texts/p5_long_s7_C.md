## final text

# The Cartographer of Small Hours

---

**I.**

The job was posted on a corkboard in the staff lounge of a library in Bruges, between a notice for a lost cat and a "Help Wanted" for a part-time coffee machine attendant. It was not the kind of thing people saw, because it was not written in Dutch or English or French, but in a kind of small, careful handwriting that looked as though the author had been afraid the ink might stain the paper. It said, in a language that Maren Voss had not heard in eleven years:

*We seek a mender of broken mornings. The hours in our town keep slipping out of sequence. A woman is needed. The pay is fair. The work is quiet. Do not ask what the town is called. You will know when you arrive.*

Maren read it three times. The first time she thought it was a joke. The second time she thought she was tired. The third time she noticed that the ink in the letter, where the author had paused to think, had pooled into tiny dark moons in the corners of the letters, and she recognized the way her grandmother used to write.

She had not spoken her grandmother's language in eleven years, not since the funeral, not since the house in the village where she had learned to tie knots in yarn and to listen to rain on a tin roof and to understand that some silences were not empty but full, like a held breath. She had moved to Bruges two years earlier to escape the shape of that grief, which sat in her chest like a stone that would not be swallowed. She had told herself she wanted work. She had told herself she wanted a city where she would not be the only one who knew the old words.

But the letter was not a joke. The third reading of it had a texture to it, a pressure behind the teeth, and she understood that if she did not go, the letter would dissolve, and the town it described would remain unnamed, and the broken mornings in it would keep breaking, and no one would ever be asked to mend them.

She packed a bag. She took the blue notebook with the old-language vocabulary in it, the one her grandmother had filled by hand, the pages soft as skin. She took a pen. She took a small brass compass she had no use for, because it was the only thing in the house that still belonged to her grandmother.

She did not take her mother's photo. She would think about that later.

---

**II.**

The train from Bruges went south and then, without announcement, stopped.

Maren stepped off at a station that had a sign with a word on it she recognized. *Oosterdam*, though it might have been *Oosterdaem*, or *Oosterdame*. The letters were slightly different in different lights. The platform was wet. The sky above it was the color of a bruise healing, yellow at the edges, aching in the center. There was a single bench. There was a man sitting on it.

He was old in the way that certain trees are old, not decrepit but settled, as though he had been growing in one place so long that the place had grown around him. He was mending a pair of gloves. The thread he used was the color of morning light, not yellow exactly, but the precise yellow that comes through glass at four in the morning when the world is still asleep.

"You're early," he said.

"I don't know where I'm going yet," Maren said.

He looked at her for a moment. "The letter doesn't say. It says *arrive*. You arrived at the train. The town is the rest of the way. You'll know it by the smell."

"What smell?"

"Breakfast that never happens. Burnt bread that's still warm."

He handed her the gloves. She took them. They were soft and warm and they fit her hands exactly, which was strange, because she had never seen them before.

"Walk," he said. "Don't take the road. The road is where the days get lost. The footpath is where they wait to be found."

Maren walked. The footpath was a narrow thing, edged in grass that grew in no particular direction, leaning slightly toward her as she passed, curious. The trees on either side were beech, and they were in no season at all — some with leaves, some without, some with fruit that had not yet decided what it wanted to be.

After a mile, the smell reached her. Burnt bread. And underneath it, something like honey and iron. The smell of a kitchen where someone had been waiting for someone who was not coming, and the bread kept toasting because the oven did not know the difference between being used and being kept warm.

The town appeared at the edge of a meadow. It was not a large town. It had maybe two hundred houses, all the same height, all with shutters that were slightly open, as though they had been propped by hands that had let go slowly. There was a church in the center with a steeple that leaned, and a well with a rope that went down into the dark. The market square was empty. The shops were open. Nothing moved.

And then she saw the first broken morning.

A woman was standing in the street, holding a child's hand. The child was asleep. The woman's hand was shaking. Around them, the morning was not happening. It was happening in pieces. The light came in from the left, but the shadows fell to the right. The sound of a bicycle bell rang from somewhere behind the woman and also from somewhere in front of her. A dog barked three times in the past, once in the present, and once in a future that the dog would never reach.

Maren stopped. The woman looked up. Her eyes were full of a kind of terror that was not fear of danger but fear of *incoherence*. The kind of fear you feel when a sentence in a foreign language suddenly makes no sense and you have forgotten why it ever meant what it meant.

"I can't hold the morning together," the woman said. Her voice came from her mouth and from the street and from somewhere above, all at once.

Maren stepped forward. She reached into her bag and took out the blue notebook. She opened it to a page her grandmother had written in her own hand. The word was *samen*, and it meant *together*, but in the old language it also meant *the act of holding the pieces without forcing them*, and the grandmother had written under it: *You do not fix. You witness. You say the shape of the thing is still here. That is enough.*

Maren said to the woman, "The shape is here. The morning is still a morning. You don't have to make it make sense. You just have to keep standing in it."

The woman blinked. The shadows stopped falling to the right. The bicycle bell rang once, correctly, from behind her. The dog was silent. The light came from the left and stayed there.

The morning mended. Not perfectly. The shadows were a fraction too long, and the bread in the shop window across the street was still slightly burnt. But the morning was a morning again.

The woman wept, and then she smiled, and then she went inside to feed her child, and the morning held.

---

**III.**

That was the first.

There were thirty-seven more, over the following weeks. Maren learned the shape of the town's brokenness. It was not that time had stopped. It was that time had become *plural*. Each morning in Oosterdam was a small stack of moments, and the stack kept tilting, and someone had to stand beside it and say *you are still a morning* until the pieces settled into an order that was not the original order but a *possible* order, a good order.

The town gave her a room above the bakery. The baker, a thick-armed woman named Hilde, who had not baked a loaf in three days because the morning she woke up had the oven working backward, left a plate of bread on her door every night. It was always slightly burnt. Maren ate it. It was the best bread she had ever had.

She met the other menders. There was a boy, perhaps twelve, who mended afternoons and kept a jar of clock hands in his pocket. There was a man named Karel who mended evenings and spoke only in questions. There was an old woman with white hair who mended the nights and who had been doing it for so long that her hands had become translucent, as though the years were leaking through her skin into the dark.

The old woman's name was Aaltje, and she had been the town's mender before Maren. She told Maren that the town had always been like this, that the mornings had been breaking since the first person had ever lived here and forgotten what day it was, and that the mending was not a fix but a *practice*, a daily act of attention, like sweeping a floor or turning a page.

"Why me?" Maren asked.

Aaltje smiled. "Because you carry the words. Because you know what it is to lose a language and spend eleven years trying to find it. Because you are afraid, and the broken mornings only respond to people who are afraid enough to stay."

Maren thought of her grandmother. She thought of the house, which her aunt had sold, which would soon be gone, which was the only place that had ever spoken to her in a voice she trusted completely. She thought of the stone in her chest.

"Can I go home?" she asked.

Aaltje was quiet for a long time. "You can," she said. "But you would have to leave the mornings broken. And they would break more. And someone else would have to come, and they might not be as good as you. And some of them would not be brave enough to stay."

Maren thought about that.

She thought about Bruges. About the library. About the corkboard and the lost cat and the coffee machine attendant. She thought about how she had come here because the letter had been in her grandmother's hand, and that she had not chosen to come, and that the coming had been less a decision and more a *recognition*, the way you recognize a face in a crowd and the whole world rearranges itself around that recognition.

She thought about the woman in the street, the one with the shaking hands. She thought about the bread. She thought about the footpath, the way the grass leaned toward her.

She thought: *I did not come here to fix the mornings. I came here to learn that the shape is still here. That is enough. That is enough.*

---

**IV.**

It was raining. It was the kind of rain that does not stop so much as *continues*, a long, patient pouring that seems to have no beginning and no end, the rain of a morning that has been going on for so long it has forgotten it was ever a morning.

Maren was in the bakery, mending an afternoon, when the bell above the door rang. Hilde stood in the doorway with a tray of bread and a look on her face that Maren had not seen before. A look of decision.

"I'm leaving," Hilde said.

The rain kept falling.

"Where to?"

"Somewhere where the mornings work. Somewhere where the oven just makes bread."

Maren set down her notebook. She looked at the tray. She looked at Hilde's hands, which were flour-dusted and strong and tired.

"You'll miss it," Maren said.

Hilde looked at the tray of bread. "I miss it every day," she said. "But I'm tired of being the one who makes it. I'm tired of holding the morning together and having no one say *thank you*. I'm tired of the bread being burnt because that's what the morning is."

Maren nodded. She did not try to stop her. She did not try to convince her that the work was noble or that the town needed her. She did not do any of the things people do to make others stay. She just said, "The bread will still be burnt, for a while. Someone will have to take your place. I'll help them learn."

Hilde looked at her for a long time. Then she handed her the tray.

"Take one," Hilde said. "It's not burnt. I made that one in the old way. I'm not proud of it, but it's not burnt."

Maren took the bread. She bit into it. It was soft and warm and it tasted like nothing at all, which was the best taste.

Hilde left. The bell rang. The rain fell.

Maren went back to the bakery window. The afternoon outside was tilting. The light was coming from below. A child was running in a circle and the sound of her shoes was ahead of her. The shape was loose.

She opened her notebook. She wrote the word *samen*. She said it aloud, in the old language, in her grandmother's voice, in the voice that lived in her mouth and had not been used for eleven years.

The afternoon settled. Not into the order it had been in this morning. Into a new order. A possible order. A good one.

The light came from below a little less. The child's shoes caught up. The afternoon held.

Maren ate her bread. It was not burnt. It was still warm.

She wrote in her notebook: *I am here. The shape is here. That is enough.*

The rain continued.

Outside, the town of Oosterdam, with its leaning steeple and its open shutters and its half-burnt bread, went on. The mornings would keep breaking. The afternoons would keep tilting. And Maren Voss would stand in the street and the bakery and the footpath, and she would say the words, and the shape would hold, and the shape would not be the same every day, and that was fine.

That was enough.

That was, she decided, more than enough.

That was everything.

---

*The letter on the corkboard in Bruges, no one knew how or when, was replaced by a new one. It was in a different hand, smaller, more urgent. It said:*

*We seek a mender of broken mornings. The town is still breaking. A woman is needed. The pay is fair. The work is quiet. Do not ask what the town is called. You will know when you arrive.*

*Underneath, in a different ink, someone had written: She's doing fine. You don't have to worry.*

*The cat from the other notice was found, and it was fine, and it was sitting on the letter, and it was not looking at you, and the coffee machine attendant said that the machine was fine, that it had just been in the wrong mode, and the morning in Bruges went on, and the morning in Oosterdam went on, and the bread was still warm.*

*It was enough.*
