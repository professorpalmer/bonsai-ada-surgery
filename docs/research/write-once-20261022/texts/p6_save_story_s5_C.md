# Salt for Tomorrow

The first thing Mara learned in the galley on the Ark was that it had no windows, and the second thing was that she would never learn a third. For three hundred and twelve years the ship had been falling through a sky with no stars in it, and the kitchen had no reason to know the difference from the day before.

She was the cook of the third galley, the one that fed the forward decks, and her hands knew the work the way water knows a riverbed: smooth, patient, unbothered by the question of where it had come from. At thirty-two she was already older than most of the people she cooked for, older than most people in her own deck, and the food she made was older still. Every cut, every seasoning, every low simmer was a thing passed down from the cook before her, who had learned it from the cook before her, in a chain so long that no one could point to a first hand.

Her name, in the official registry of the Ark, was Mara-114, but the crew had been using just Mara for as long as anyone remembered, which was to say since the naming of the ship and the naming of everything after it. People liked the small freedoms the Ark allowed. A nickname. A song hummed off-key in the canteen. A plate of stew that came out a shade richer than the standard. The Ark ran on strict arithmetic of calories and nutrients, but the galleys were where the arithmetic bent a little, and where it bent, a person lived.

On a Tuesday, Mara was making the broth she called grandmother's, though no grandmother had ever lived on this ship and no one in the forward decks had a grandmother they could name. The recipe was a set of proportions rather than a method: two parts water, one part root-stock, a fistful of leaf from the hydroponic shelf, a pinch of salt so fine it had been distilled for six hundred years. She had tasted it every day for nine years. It would taste the same to the last cook on the ship, unless the ship broke or ran out of salt.

It did not run out of salt. That was the first rule, older than any rule the Council had ever written. Salt for the kitchens, salt for the seed vaults, salt in the medicine stores. Salt had been carried out of Earth's oceans in sealed canisters before the Ark had left the launch bay, and it was treated with a kind of reverence the rest of the world had forgotten. To waste salt in a spill was a minor offense. To waste it in a joke was a serious one. Mara never wasted it. She measured it in pinches no wider than a fingernail and felt, in the small salt of her tongue, that she was eating a piece of the old world.

What the old world tasted like, she could not say for certain. The histories called it a time of weather, of soil, of fields that grew things without being asked. She had read the histories the way everyone read them, carefully and without hunger, and she knew the words for wheat and rain and the smell of cut grass, but the words had no flavor. Flavor, she had decided at some point, had to be earned. A taste you could only earn with work, with care, with years of doing the same thing until it became part of you.

That was the quiet argument the galleys ran every day, never out loud. The Council had built the Ark to last, to carry, to keep. It had been designed by engineers who thought in terms of cycles and reserves, and everything on the ship had a cycle: the hydroponics, the water reclamation, the air, the lights, the food. Mara's food had a cycle too. She made the same broths and stews and baked goods every day, in the same order, in the same order the cook before her had made them, and she did not change them because no one had asked her to, and no one would know if she did. She had changed one thing once, a small one. She had added a pinch more salt to the winter broth because the cold was bad that year, and the cold had not been bad the next. She had added it again the following year, and the next, and after a while the pinch of salt had become the recipe, and the recipe had become the tradition, and the tradition had become the reason the broth was the way it was.

That was what terrified her. Not the cold, not the work, not the ship, though the ship was a great and beautiful thing and she loved it in the way you love a house that will outlive you. What terrified her was the pinch of salt, the small private choice that had become a rule without her meaning it to, the way every small choice of every cook over three hundred years had become the food of the Ark, the way the Ark itself had become a kind of recipe, a set of proportions that had been passed down and refined and mistaken for the truth.

She thought about it one night while the galley was empty and the lights were low and the only sound was the hum of the refrigeration and the soft hiss of the steam lines. She had written a letter once, years ago, to a cook in the rear decks who had died when the ship was still young enough to be young, and in that letter she had asked a question. She had asked whether the food on the rear decks was the same as it had been when the Ark left Earth, whether the broth had ever changed, whether the salt had ever been lost. The letter had gone in the mail. The mail on the Ark was a set of sealed tubes that ran from deck to deck on a slow schedule, and letters were not common, but they were allowed, and the one she had written was answered three cycles later by a cook who said, in plain words, that no one knew anymore. The back galleys had their own traditions, their own pinches and omissions, and no two galleys had tasted the same food in a hundred years. The food of the Ark was not a single thing. It was a hundred small things, each one a recipe that had been made by a cook who had never set foot on Earth.

Mara read that answer again the next day, and the next, until the words wore a groove in her mind. She thought about the salt. She thought about the way the ship was built, the way every system had been built to be maintained, not understood. The engineers had built it to outlast them, and in doing so they had made it a kind of recipe too: a set of parts, a sequence, a maintenance schedule, a chain of hands that would keep it going until it was done. And she thought about the way a recipe works, the way it is not the thing itself but a memory of the thing, a way of saying, do this and you will have what was there. The broth she made was not the broth from the old world. It was a memory of a memory, a shadow of a shadow, but it was honest about that. It was a story told in salt and water, and the story was true enough.

She made the broth that night, as she had made it every night for nine years. She measured the salt by pinch, and she tasted it on her tongue, and it was the same as it had been before, and it would be the same as it would be after, until it would not be, and then a cook would come after her, and that cook would make a pinch her own, and the pinch would become the recipe, and the recipe would become the tradition, and the tradition would become the reason the broth was the way it was.

She was not afraid. She had decided that. The Ark would go on. The food would go on. The pinch of salt would go on. It was not a small thing. It was a whole way of being, a way of saying, I was here, I made this, and someone will make it after me, and after them after them. She ladled the broth into the trays and sent them down the corridor to the forward decks, where three thousand people would eat it and never know her name, and she would make another, and another, and another, and the story would go on, and the ship would keep falling through the sky with no stars in it, and the kitchen would have no windows, and the food would taste like what it was, which was enough.
