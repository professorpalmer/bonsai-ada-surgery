## final text

# The Unwritten Child

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, when the November wind had been driving rain against the lantern room for three days and the light had been turning its slow gold arc over the Atlantic like a man turning over in his sleep. Eamon Cuthbert, seventy-one, lighthouse keeper at Mullachar Point, found it wedged under the door of the keeper's cottage, tucked beneath a slab of stone the size of a dinner plate so that the wind would not carry it off into the sea.

The envelope was cream. The ink was a thin blue-black, and the handwriting was small and slanting, as if written by a hand that had not yet fully learned to hold a pen. The address line read: *For Eamon Cuthbert, the Keeper, Mullachar Point.*

The return address was blank.

He opened it in his kitchen, at the table where his wife, Aisling, had once placed a cup of tea with the saucer turned twice clockwise before setting it down. He had done it a hundred times since she was gone, without thinking. He did it again now.

The letter read:

*I can feel the lamp. It turns. I can feel the salt on your hands where you wiped the brass. I think about the sound the glass makes when the wind presses against it, because it is like someone tapping a fingernail on a table and then stopping. I don't know your name yet, but I think it sounds like a door closing slowly. I will write again when I can.*

Eamon read it three times. He sat very still. The lamp turned outside, throwing its beam across the black water, and the cottage swayed in the way that old buildings sway when they are thinking.

He did not write back. There was no one to write to, and no one to write to.

The second letter came the following Thursday. The third came the next week, and the fourth the week after. Each arrived under the door, beneath the stone, the same cream envelope, the same small slanting hand. The voice inside them was changing, not in vocabulary so much as in confidence. The second letter said, *The woman is carrying me and I am very still and it is warm and dark and I have not yet met the light and I want to.* The third said, *The man in the window below me is reading a book. He is old. I know he is old because his hands make the paper rustle like leaves in a bad October.*

Eamon was not in the window below the cottage. He was on the lighthouse stair, halfway up, when he thought of it. The woman was in the lighthouse below, in the lower room where the keeper's kitchen had been before Aisling died and before the house had been made only the lighthouse.

He understood, without the clarity of a name, that the woman was his daughter, Niamh.

She had come to Mullachar Point two weeks before the first letter. She had not announced it. She had walked up the headland path with a coat too thin for November and stood at the base of the lighthouse and raised her hand, and he had seen her from the gallery and had not gone down. She had stayed three nights in the cottage, in the room that had been his, and she had spoken to no one but the sea, and she had gone back to the village and left the door unlocked.

The fourth letter said, *The woman is sleeping. She says the lighthouse is my first country. I am not yet here but I am in the lighthouse.*

Eamon read this at his table and set the paper down and pressed his palm flat against the wood and sat with his hand there until the lamp made three full turns.

The letters continued. By the eighth letter the child had a name, or rather the child was being given one. *They say my name will be Iona,* the letter read. *It is a small name. It fits.* By the twelfth letter Iona knew things that were not quite possible. *I know what the foghorn sounds like. I know that the keeper puts a cloth over the glass at night because the rain makes the light wobble and the wobble is embarrassing and I think it is like a man coughing in public.*

Eamon had done that. He had wrapped a clean cloth around the lower pane and held it while the beam shuddered. He had been ashamed of it. He had never told anyone.

The letters stopped on a Saturday. Eamon stood in the doorway of the lighthouse for four hours, looking at the water, waiting for the next letter to appear beneath the stone. The wind was light. The sea was grey and flat and the light turned without hurry. At dusk the cottage door opened and Niamh came out of the headland path, her face pale and her eyes the same brown as his mother's.

She said, "Eamon, I'm going to have the baby."

He said, "When?"

She said, "Tonight."

He said nothing for a moment. Then he said, "Iona."

She looked at him. She said, "Yes. I've been calling her that. I just—" She stopped. She looked at the lighthouse, at the lamp turning, and something in her face went still. "She's not here yet," she said. "Not yet."

"I know," he said. "I know she isn't."

He took her coat from her. He went down to the kitchen and put the kettle on. She sat at the table and turned the cup twice clockwise, and he watched her do it, and he felt the small, sharp grief that had been in his chest since Aisling died, and it did not break open, it simply let the cup move.

The baby was born at eleven, in the cottage, with no midwife and no one but Niamh and the wind against the glass. She was a girl. She weighed nothing, or a great deal, it was hard to say. She cried.

Eamon held her. She was small and red and furious and she was not yet a person in the way he understood persons. She was a sound, a weight, a mouth moving at the air. He held her against his chest and she breathed against his skin and the lighthouse light turned outside and he thought about a letter that would never be written, because the sender had no name yet, no address, no history, and he thought that the letters had been the only true thing in the cottage for six weeks, the only thing that had not been a memory.

Niamh, when she was able to lift her head, said, "She'll never know you wrote those letters."

He looked down at the baby in his arms. He looked at Niamh. He looked at the table where the last letter had been, folded, with the small slanting hand.

He said, "I know."

He said it very quietly, and it was the only thing he ever said that made the room feel smaller.

---

Three months later, Iona came to Mullachar Point with her mother. She was not a person yet, not really. She was a child in the way that a river is a river, in the way that a door is a door before it opens. She grabbed Eamon's coat and pulled. She said, "Grandpa. Grandpa." It was a small word and it fit.

He knelt. He held her. She smelled of milk and salt and the particular warm smell of a baby's skin. She looked up at him with her mother's eyes and he thought about a woman in a window, about a hand raised on a headland path, about a door closing slowly, and he thought: *I know you. I know you. I know you.*

He did not write a letter. There was no need. There was no need.
