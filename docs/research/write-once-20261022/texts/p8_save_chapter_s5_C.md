# Chapter One: The Weight of the Sky

No one remembers the day the turtle began to walk.

The oldest ledgers in the Hall of Currents, written in the reed-ink of the Third Founding, begin with the same first line: *He came up out of the sea, and the city did not flee.* The scribes of Thalun were not fools. They knew how to build a home that would not run. What they did not know was whether the turtle was a god, a beast, or a mistake that the sea had made and refused to correct.

Mara did not care about any of those questions on the morning she woke with blood in her mouth and the sky at the wrong height.

She lived in the Upper Shell, in the city's northern quarter, where the streets were paved with polished shell-plate and the houses were built from the ribs that grew up like columns of bone-white coral. Below her, if she had climbed down to the rim, she would have seen the world: grey-green, moving. The turtle's back was an ocean, and the city was the reef that had decided, three hundred years ago, to stay on.

Her name was Mara Vess, and she was a scale-reader.

It was a small trade, and a necessary one. Every morning, the tides of the turtle's shell—those slow, almost imperceptible swells that rolled over the city as the beast moved across the deep—left their marks on the stone. The stone sweated. The streets groaned. And where the groaning was wrong, a reader could find the tell: a hairline fracture in the old paving, a seam of salt that had wept through a wall, a door that would not close because the floor had shifted a breath to the left.

Most days, the tells were trivial. Mara would chalk a circle, mark the seam, and walk on. But this morning, the fracture ran through the door of the chapel of the First Scale, and it ran deep enough that a child could push a hand through it.

She knelt and pressed her fingers into the crack.

The stone was warm.

Not warm the way stone is warm after the sun has touched it. Warm the way skin is.

Mara stood, and behind her, the street of her quarter was already waking. A woman with a laundry basket paused at a corner and stared at the crack in the chapel wall. An old fisher, hauling his trawls of barnacle and kelp from the rim, cursed something in the old tongue. Two boys on a cart of salted eels pointed at Mara and called her name at her, not unkindly, as if she were a street sign they could not quite read.

"Scale-reader." The word was always the first thing they said. And the second thing was: *What is it telling us?*

Mara wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. The blood was thin, and she thought, very calmly, that this was not a morning for answers.

She went to the chapel door.

The First Scale was the oldest thing in the city, older even than the ledgers. It was a plate of black shell, broad as a table, set into the wall of the chapel like a window. For three hundred years it had shown the turtle's mood in the only way it knew how: when the beast was calm, the scale was smooth and unmarked, and when it was troubled, the shell rippled, and the ripples were read by the elders as prophecy.

This morning the scale was rippled.

Not in the slow, wide waves of the last trouble—a storm, the scribes had said, or a great fish, or the long sickness that had passed through the deep. This was a fine, tight quiver, like the surface of a drum someone had just struck and had not yet stopped striking. The black shell trembled, and with every tremor a thin line of gold bled through the stone, like light through water.

The elder who kept the scale was not in the chapel. She had been in her cells below the rim for a week, since the dream that had not ended. Mara did not ask what the dream had been. Some doors, even in a city built on a god's back, were not opened.

But the scale was speaking, and no one was there to hear it, and so Mara stood with her fingers on the black shell and listened to the city.

The sound came up through the stone, and it was not a sound at all, not truly. It was more like the memory of a sound, the ghost of a voice she had heard in her mother's kitchen, years ago, when the kettle was singing and the steam had been thick enough to hold. Her mother had hummed while she stirred the soup, and the note had gone on and on, and Mara had been small enough then to believe that the world was just one long note, and that if she could hum along, the world would keep turning.

The turtle was humming.

It was low, and it was very, very old, and it went up through the bone of the city and out of the black scale and into Mara's hands, and for a moment she was not Mara Vess, scale-reader of the Upper Shell, but a small girl in a kitchen, and the note was the world, and the world was humming because it was alive.

Then the note changed.

It did not stop. It did not end. But it turned, like a wave turning on itself, and Mara felt the turn in her teeth, in the fillings of her mouth, in the small bones behind her eyes. The gold light in the scale went out, and the black shell was still, and the street outside the chapel was very quiet.

Mara pulled her hands off the wall and looked at her fingers.

They were covered in salt.

Not the salt of the rim, not the dry grey salt that crusted the eels and the kelp and the old stones of the city. It was wet, and it was warm, and it smelled of the deep, of the black water far below the turtle's belly, where nothing lived that had ever been named.

She wiped her hands on her tunic, and the wet salt came off in a line, and where the line had run, a dark mark had been left on the black scale.

It was a shape.

It was not a ripple, not a tremor, not any of the marks that the elders had ever taught her to read. It was a line that bent, and bent, and bent, and it went down, and it went down, and it went down, and it did not stop.

Mara stood in the chapel, and she listened, and the turtle hummed beneath her, and the hum was very old, and the hum was not quite the same as it had been this morning.

She understood, with a calm that surprised her, that the city was about to learn a word it had never needed to say.

She went to her small room in the Upper Shell, where the windows looked out over the bone-white columns of the quarter and the grey-green sea moved, and moved, and moved. She sat on the edge of her bed and opened the ledger she had kept for ten years, the ledger that held every tell she had ever read, every mark the shell had ever left on the city.

She turned to the last page, where the last entry read: *Seventh of the Month of Ribs. The scale was smooth. The city slept. Nothing was wrong.*

She looked at the salt on her tunic. She looked at the mark on the scale, still faint in the morning light.

She picked up her reed-ink pen.

She began to write.

*Seventh of the Month of Ribs. The scale was not smooth. The city did not sleep. The turtle spoke.*

*It said: we are coming up.*

She set the pen down, and she sat very still, and outside the window the turtle's back moved on, and the city moved with it, and the sky was at the right height, and the sky was not, and it would not stay that way for long.

Mara Vess, scale-reader of the Upper Shell, closed the ledger, and went out into the street to find the old fisher, and the old fisher had a trawl of barnacles, and he had been watching the chapel wall for an hour, and he looked at her, and he said:

"Well?"

And Mara Vess, who had been a small girl in a kitchen, and a scale-reader for ten years, and a woman who had just heard the word the city would not say, looked at the mark on the black scale, and the salt on her hands, and the low hum in the bones of the world, and she said:

"It's time to pack."
