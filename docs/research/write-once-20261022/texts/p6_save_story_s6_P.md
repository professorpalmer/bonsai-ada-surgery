## final text

# The Last Kitchen

The tomatoes had ripened overnight, which meant Elara Voss would have to lie awake tonight watching them.

On the *Perseus*, a generation ship two hundred and forty years into its slow crawl toward Kepler-442b, nothing ripened quickly. The ship had never been fast. Nothing aboard it ever had to be. Her grandmother had called that the first truth of ship-life: *you arrive when the fruit is ready, not when the fruit is ready for you.* The ship carried its own weather. It grew its own seasons, one slow green turn at a time.

She sat at the end of the hydroponic bay on deck nine, the one they called the Garden, and held a single tomato up to the pale artificial sun. It was the size of a plum. It was the color of the inside of a heart, if a heart had ever been held up to light. It smelled like summer. Elara breathed it in and let her eyes go wet.

"Your grandmother would have called that a miracle," said a voice behind her. "Or a disease."

Elara didn't turn. Jax was leaning in the doorway, arms crossed, still wearing his maintenance suit from the water reclamation plant. He was twenty-three. He had never been on Earth. He had never seen a sky that wasn't a ceiling, never felt rain, never known a tree that hadn't been planted in a metal box with a name tag. To him, the Garden was a museum that used too much power.

"It's not a disease," Elara said.

"From the efficiency standpoint, it's a disaster. You're growing fruit for the sake of growing fruit. You could turn that bay into protein cultures and feed twice as many people for half the energy."

"You could feed twice as many people with protein cultures."

"And you could be proud of it." Jax pushed off the doorframe. "I'm not going to stand here and argue with a woman who's been growing vegetables in a tin can for forty years. My shift doesn't end until zero-four." He looked at the tomato again, and something flickered behind his eyes. He almost reached for it. He didn't.

Jax walked away, and Elara stood alone with her plum-sized miracle.

The cook's life on a generation ship was mostly a negotiation with the void.

Elara had been born three years into the voyage. The *Perseus* was not a ship in any sense that mattered to people on the ground anymore. It was a city in transit, a floating country with no soil. Every meal was a small act of faith. You took the reclamation water, the algae paste, the cultured protein, the hydroponic greens that tasted faintly of metal and chlorine, and you mixed them into something that reminded the crew they had once been human. You made it into food. You made it into *dinner*, with plates and cups and a chair at the table and a person across from you saying, "This is good. Eat more."

The kitchen on deck nine was small. It was a rectangle of steel and steam. The ovens were industrial things, more like the mouths of furnaces than the hearths of the old stories. Elara had been the cook for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned to make a meal out of almost nothing. She learned it from the stories her mother had told her, and her mother from hers, and hers from the cook who had carried the recipes in her head, back through the dark years before the ship's records were fully digitized.

There were no cookbooks on the *Perseus*. Not really. The digital archives had been corrupted more than once, and the original recipes had been lost in the noise. What survived was fragmentary. A note from a woman named Amara: *"Sour cream, too much acid, don't fear it."* A diagram of a pie crust that looked like a spiderweb. A single line from a child: *"Grandma made bread that was soft and warm and tasted like the world."*

Elara's job was to make bread. To make it soft and warm. To make it taste, as much as she could, like the world.

The Garden was her secret weapon. Not because it fed the ship. It didn't. The tomatoes, the peppers, the herbs, the small green beans — they were a few hundred kilograms a year, a rounding error. The real value was in the smell. The green, sweet, living smell of something that had not been manufactured in a lab or reconstituted from powder. The smell told the crew that the ship still had a pulse. That somewhere, in a bay on deck nine, a woman was growing a tomato.

And Elara fed the tomato.

Every week, she made a meal. A small meal, for a handful of people she chose. A soup with the new beans. A salad with the basil, which she grew in a pot no bigger than a child's shoe. A simple bread, with a little salt and a little flour from the grain store, baked until the crust crackled like it was trying to become a different, smaller thing. She brought it to the mess hall at seven o'clock. She put the pot in the center of the table. She told people to come, but only if they wanted to.

No one complained. No one measured it. Most of them came. Some of them cried, and Elara pretended not to notice, because the tears were for her and they were for the ship and they were for the world that had left them and the world that would receive them, and she could not sort them.

Jax came back the next evening.

Elara had set the table. Two plates. A small bowl of tomato salad, the tomatoes sliced so thin they were almost translucent. A piece of bread, torn by hand. A glass of reclamation water, clear as anything you'd ever want to drink.

She set the table and walked away.

When she looked back, Jax was at the table. He was holding the tomato slice between two fingers. He hadn't eaten it. He was looking at it.

"It's just a fruit," he said.

"It is," Elara said.

"I don't understand the point. My mother made me a bowl of protein paste every night for twenty-three years. It fed me. It kept me alive. I don't need a tomato."

"Did you like the protein paste?"

Jax opened his mouth. Closed it. "It was... it was fine."

"It was fine," Elara said. "That's what we said about everything for three hundred years. We said it was fine. We said we were fine. We said the ship was fine. And the whole time, we were forgetting what it was like to taste something. To taste it and know, without thinking, that it was good. That it was *something*."

Jax set the slice on the plate. He picked it up again. He ate it.

He chewed. He stopped chewing. He looked at Elara with an expression she had seen only once before, on her own face, in a mirror, the night her mother died and the last of the Garden went bad and she sat on the floor of the bay and couldn't remember if she had ever seen the sky.

"It tastes like," Jax said. "I don't know. Like a memory I don't have."

"Yeah," Elara said. "That's what it is."

He ate the rest of the tomato. He ate the bread. He finished the water. He washed the bowl and dried it and set it on the drying rack, the way Elara had taught him, because she had taught him the way and it was the only thing that had ever felt like teaching something that wasn't broken.

"You'll come again," Elara said.

Jax stood there, towel over his shoulder, and he didn't answer. But he didn't leave right away. He stood there and he breathed, and the smell of the tomato clung to him, and Elara thought: this is how it travels. Not in the archive. Not in the data. Not in the records that get corrupted and the stories that get thinned out. It travels in the mouth, and the memory of the mouth, and the mouth of the next person who is handed a bowl and told, *eat, this is good.*

The *Perseus* would arrive in three hundred and forty years. Two hundred and thirty-nine to go. Three hundred and thirty-eight. The tomato would go bad. The Garden would be replanted. The next cook would be born, and would be taught the way, and would set a table in a steel room, and would make something soft and warm and almost, almost like the world.

Elara went to wash the pot. The tomato juice was red in the sink. She watched it swirl down the drain and thought about all the kitchens that had ever existed, on a ship or a shore, and all the people who had stood in front of a fire or a stove or a hydroponic rack and done the only thing that mattered:

They had made something.

They had fed someone.

They had kept a small, stubborn piece of the world alive.

Outside the window of the mess hall, the stars turned. Slowly. Steadily. On time.

The *Perseus* carried on.
