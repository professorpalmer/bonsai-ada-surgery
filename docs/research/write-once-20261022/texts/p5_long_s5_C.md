# The Salt Bellows of Merrow's Point

## I

The first thing people noticed about the girl was that she never blinked at the same time as everyone else. When the ferry horn blew across the grey water, the passengers on the deck would blink in unison, a small synchronized flinch of the whole boat, and the girl on the gangplank would be two seconds late, or two seconds early. Her mother, who was dead for eleven years but still smelled of kelp and paraffin on certain cold mornings, had said, *That's just how you are, Cae. The world goes on in its own time, and you go on in yours. You don't have to be a metronome.*

Cae was thirty-one. She kept the light at Merrow's Point. The light had been automated since 1994—no one had actually lived there in that capacity for three decades—but the Coast Guard still sent someone, a nominal keeper, a human presence for the paperwork, the monthly walk of the gallery, the logbook entry. Cae took the job because the rent on the old cottage was nothing and because she was not good at cities. She was good at standing in a narrow stone room at the top of a tall tower and watching the sea throw itself against the rocks and writing *Wind NNE, 32 knots. Visibility poor. Nothing to report.*

The town below was called Hallowmere. Six hundred people, most of them connected by fishing, by the old cannery that ran maybe two days a week, by the fact that everyone knew everyone's business and no one said what they meant. Cae went down on Thursdays for the co-op and the post office. She ordered coffee and a tin of shortbread and nodded at the butcher who owed the postmaster money and let the postmaster nod back.

In autumn, something changed.

It began with the fog. Not the usual low fog that rolled in off the Channel and burned itself out by ten. This fog came on a Tuesday in October and did not leave. Not on Wednesday. Not on Thursday. By Saturday, the whole coast of England had reported it, the Met Office called it an "unseasonal nor'wester advection layer," and people put up flags in their gardens to mark their driveways.

But the thing that mattered was what the light was doing. The beam was sweeping the gallery, the great brass lens rotating on its motor with its slow, reliable tick. And the beam was hitting the fog and coming back. Not reflecting. *Coming back.* The light was reaching through the fog and finding something on the other side and turning, as if the fog were a wall and the light had walked around it and come back around the far side. Cae stood in the gallery at 2 a.m. with a tin of coffee and watched it happen. The beam crossed the fog. The beam reappeared from the east, where the land ended. She stood there for two hours.

The next morning she wrote in the log: *Unusual refraction observed. Beam returns from the east at approx. 137 degrees. No equipment fault. No explanation. Nothing else to report.*

The Coast Guard replied, *Nothing else to report. Acknowledged.*

## II

The fog lasted eleven days.

On the seventh day, Cae went down to Hallowmere and found the co-op empty. The doors were unlocked. The coffee machine was warm, as if someone had been making a cup five minutes ago. The tin of shortbread was there. The butcher, Dolan, was there, behind the counter, looking at a fish he was gutting with a knife that seemed too large for the hand.

"Cae," he said. "You're late. The others are still up top."

"The others?"

He looked at his knife. "The others are still up top, Cae. They've been up top since Wednesday."

She went up to the post office. The postmaster, Maren, was at the counter. Maren was also at the counter. There were two of her, one behind the glass and one behind the wood, and the one behind the glass was writing a letter in a hand so small it looked like a child's scrawl, and the one behind the wood was looking at Cae with a face that had the exact same lines as her own but arranged slightly differently, as if someone had taken a photograph and turned it a quarter degree.

"Cae," Maren-behind-the-wood said. "You should come inside. They're having tea."

"They?"

Maren-behind-the-wood pointed a finger at the window. Outside, in the fog, there were shapes. Not people, exactly. More like the *idea* of people, the way you see a figure at the edge of a room when you're tired and you can't decide if it's a person or a coat rack or the leg of a chair. The shapes were standing in a line along the harbor wall. They were not walking. They were not turning. They were standing, and they were all facing the sea, and they had been standing there for, Maren said, *seven days and four hours, and none of them has moved, and none of them is going to stop.*

Cae went back to the tower. She climbed the two hundred and forty-one steps to the lantern room. The beam was still coming back. She could see it now, through the fog, the way a streetlight glows in a window: a soft, wet yellow, and on the far side of the fog, just at the edge of the water, there was another tower. Same height. Same spiral of steps. Same brass. And from its gallery, a beam was sweeping, and where the two beams met in the middle of the fog, there was a point of bright, almost painful white.

She climbed down. She took the little tin of oil she kept in the tower for the lamp in the cottage. She took the flashlight. She took the old wool coat her mother had worn.

## III

She walked the harbor road. The fog was so thick she could feel it in her teeth, a salt-and-iron taste, like a mouth full of pennies. The shapes were still there on the harbor wall. She got closer. One of them, the nearest, was a man in a yellow waterproof. Cae knew him. He was the man who sold ice cream on summer evenings from a little cart he kept by the cannery. He had a beard like a wet mop and a laugh that was too loud.

The shapes were not frozen. Cae had been wrong. They were moving. So slowly it was almost not movement, a millimeter per second, but they were *drifting*, and they were all facing the sea, and they were all slowly turning, the way a sunflower turns toward the sun, toward the water, toward the dark open water on the other side of the fog.

One of the shapes turned its head.

It was Dolan. The butcher. The one with the too-large knife. His face was the same as Dolan's face, but the expression was not Dolan's expression. The eyes were wide and wet and *knowing*, and there was a kind of terrible patience in them, the patience of a man who has been waiting so long that waiting has become the shape of his life.

"Cae," Dolan-shape said. "Come. The others are having tea. The tea is going cold."

"Cae," the other Dolan-shape said, from behind the butcher counter, in a voice that was not quite the same pitch, "you should come inside. The tea is going cold."

And Cae understood, or thought she understood, what was happening. The fog was not weather. The fog was a *border*. Not a border between two places, but a border between two *times*. The fog was the dead hour, the space between one second and the next, the crack in the floorboards of the world where the world is not yet fully committed to what it is about to be. For eleven days, Hallowmere had slipped into that space. The people had not died. They had not been taken. They had *lagged*. They were the afterimage of a hand that has been pulled away from a light switch, the flicker that remains after the bulb has gone out.

And the tower on the other side of the fog—the other Merrow's Point, the one with the beam sweeping the empty water—that was where the time was going. Not a different place. The same place, eleven days ahead. The people were standing in the fog because the fog was where *not-yet-happening* goes, and the people of Hallowmere had stopped happening.

## IV

She climbed the tower on the other side. She climbed the two hundred and forty-one steps and came out into a lantern room that was her lantern room, with the same brass ring under her knees, the same scratch on the glass from a wrench in 1987, the same smell of hot oil and salt. The beam was sweeping. The fog was thick.

And on the gallery railing, standing, with a mug of tea in her hands and looking at her with an expression that was both relief and accusation, was Cae. The other Cae. The Cae of eleven days ago, or eleven days from now—she could not tell. The fog made the tenses soft.

"You went up," the other Cae said.

"Yes."

"You should have come down. The tea's going cold."

Cae looked out into the fog. On the harbor wall below, the shapes were still turning. Slowly. Toward the water. Toward the open, dark, indifferent sea. They would get there. She could feel it in her bones, the way the tide turns. They would get to the water's edge and the fog would not be enough to hold them and they would go on, and they would be gone, and Hallowmere would be empty, and no one would know why, because the only person who could explain it—the only person who had stood in the fog and seen the afterimage and written *Nothing else to report*—was here, in the lantern room, talking to herself.

"Can you go back?" Cae asked the other Cae.

The other Cae set the mug down on the railing. Her hands were steady. Her face had the same lines. Her eyes were not quite the same eyes. "You can go back," she said. "But the hour is going. It's already going. I can feel it in my teeth. The salt-and-iron taste. It's thinning."

"And if I go back?"

"Then you go back, and the fog lifts, and the people on the wall come back to themselves, and Dolan puts his knife down and goes home, and Maren writes the letter in her normal hand, and the co-op has coffee that tastes like coffee, and the town is a town, and no one ever has to see what it means to stand in the crack of the world and watch the people you know become *almost*, and then become *not yet*, and then—"

The other Cae's voice went quiet. Not stopped. Quiet. The way a voice goes quiet when it has reached the part of the sentence it cannot say without breaking.

"And if I stay?" Cae asked.

The other Cae smiled. It was not a kind smile. It was not an unkind smile. It was the smile of someone who has been standing in a lantern room for eleven days, watching a beam sweep the fog, and has learned what it means to be the thing that keeps turning when there is no one to see it turning.

"You stay," the other Cae said, "and you are the lighthouse. And the lighthouse does not go home. The lighthouse is the home. The lighthouse is what's left when the people have gone into the sea and the sea has not decided what to do with them yet. You stay, and you keep the light on, and you keep the beam sweeping, and the fog lifts, and the town comes back, and it never knows what almost happened, and it never knows that it *almost didn't*, and you know, and you know, and you know, and you write it in the log, *Wind NNE, 32 knots. Visibility poor. Nothing to report.* And you write it every night for the rest of your life. And the only person who knows what the log really says is the other Cae, standing in the fog, who will come and go with the tide, who will stand on the harbor wall and watch you climb the tower and turn the light and drink the coffee that tastes like coffee and write in the log, and you will see each other, and you will nod, and you will not blink at the same time, and it will be enough."

The fog was thinning. Cae could feel it in her teeth, the salt-and-iron taste retreating like a tide. On the harbor wall below, the shapes were still turning. Dolan. Maren. The ice-cream man with the wet-mop beard. The postmaster. The butcher. All of them, slowly, the way the sun rises, the way the dead hour gives way to the living minute.

Cae picked up the mug. The tea was cold. She drank it anyway.

She climbed down the two hundred and forty-one steps. She walked down the harbor road. The fog was a thread of grey at her waist. The shapes were at the water's edge. One by one, they stepped forward into the fog, and the fog took them, and they were gone, and the harbor wall was empty, and the sea was grey and wide and ordinary, and the light was on.

She went to the co-op. Dolan was behind the counter. He looked up. He looked at her. He looked at her for a long time, and there was something in his face that was not quite a recognition and not quite a forgetting, a gap, a flicker, the afterimage of a hand pulled from a light switch.

"Cae," he said. "You're late. The others are still up top."

"I'm coming down, Dolan," she said. "I'm coming down."

The co-op had coffee. The coffee tasted like coffee.

That evening, Cae climbed the tower. She stood in the lantern room. The beam was sweeping. The fog was gone. The sea was black and empty and the stars were coming out, one by one, in their old, indifferent way.

She wrote in the log: *Wind NNE, 32 knots. Visibility poor. Nothing to report.*

And then, in a hand so small it looked like a child's scrawl, she added: *Nothing else to report. Nothing else to report. Nothing else to report.*

The beam swept. The sea threw itself against the rocks.

And somewhere, in the dead hour, in the crack of the world where the people go between what they were and what they are, the other Cae was standing on the harbor wall, watching the light turn, and nodding, and not blinking.
