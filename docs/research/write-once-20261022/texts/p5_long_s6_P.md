## final text

In the village of Brinemoor, at the very edge of the gray, unforgiving sea, there stood a lighthouse that had never once been turned off. Its name, carved in stone that had been chewed by a thousand tides, was The Warden.

Elara had been the keeper for eleven years. She was a woman of sharp angles and quiet habits, her hair the color of wet slate, her hands perpetually stained with the oil of the wick. She loved the routine of it: the cleaning of the glass, the polishing of the brass, the counting of the seconds between the light's pulse. But she had a secret that no one in Brinemoor knew. She hated the sea. Not with fear, but with a profound, aching resentment, because she believed it had taken everything from her. It had taken her husband, a sailor who had sailed out to the horizon and never returned, and it had taken her first child, a boy who had fallen into the tide pool while chasing a crab. Elara told herself she was simply a keeper of light. But deep down, she was a keeper of a grudge.

One autumn evening, a storm rolled in from the west, turning the sky into a bruised and churning mess. As Elara climbed the spiral stairs to the lantern room, she passed a small, shivering figure huddled in the doorway of the lighthouse base. It was a boy, no older than ten, with wild hair that moved strangely, even in the stillness of the room, as if stirred by a current only he could feel.

"Go on, then," Elara said, her voice like cracked ice. "The door is not for you."

"I'm not cold," the boy said. His eyes were the exact, impossible color of the deep ocean. "I'm hungry. But not for food."

Before Elara could answer, a sound echoed through the stone—a low, mournful hum, like a cello played in the next room. The boy pointed a thin, pale finger at the base of the lighthouse. "It remembers," he whispered. "It remembers the ship. The one that broke. The one that you didn't see."

Elara felt her heart lurch. "There was no ship. Not that night."

The boy smiled, a sad, knowing expression. "You closed your eyes, Keeper. You closed them so the sea wouldn't take anything else from you. But it took you the truth instead."

The storm intensified outside, the wind howling through the narrow gaps in the stonework like a thousand voices. Elara looked at the boy, and for the first time in years, she saw the reflection of the lighthouse's beam in his eyes—a beam that was not from the lamp, but from the sea itself, a cold, pale glow rising from the water below.

She realized then that the lighthouse had never been a lighthouse at all. It was a lens. A great, stone lens that focused the light of the deep, and that light was not meant to warn the ships away, but to draw them in. The sea had been luring the ships, and Elara’s grudge, her refusal to see the truth, had been the anchor that kept the lighthouse dark within, allowing the sea to continue its cruel work.

"What is your name?" she asked, her voice trembling.

"I am the tide," he said. "And I am here to ask a question. Will you open your eyes, Elara? Or will you let the sea take you too?"

The wind screamed. The glass of the lantern room groaned. The boy reached out, and where his fingers touched her cheek, she felt the cold of the deep, the pressure of the miles, the memory of the crab in the pool and the ship on the rocks and her husband, her child, all of it rushing back with a force that broke her ribs and mended them at the same time.

She looked down through the floor, into the water below, and for the first time, she saw it: a ship, broken and splintered, sitting in the shallows, and beside it, a boy with wild hair, holding a crab in his hand, looking up at her with a face full of sorrow and hope.

Elara closed her eyes again, not to hide, but to let the light in. When she opened them, the boy was gone. The storm had passed. The sea was calm, a mirror of silver and black.

Elara walked down the stairs and out into the dawn. She picked up a small, smooth stone from the beach—a piece of the ship's wood, worn by the water into a shape like a hand—and she placed it on her windowsill. She would not turn off the light. She would not hide from the sea. She would be the keeper, yes, but she would also be the witness. The sea had taken from her, but as she walked back up the spiral stairs, she realized it had given her something it had never intended: the truth. And in the light of the Warden, she was no longer alone.
