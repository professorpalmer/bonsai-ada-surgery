# Chapter One — The Swell

Kessa had been born with the swell in her ears. Each morning the city of Vell woke to a single deep breath, and the whole of the shell — and all of Vell built upon it — rose three feet out of the black water.

The breaths had names. The Rising, the Holding, the Fall, and the long black Wait when Moru, the city's old word for the turtle beneath their feet, held still beneath them like a held note.

Kessa stood on the rim of the Upper Wall with the other tide-watchers, a row of hooded figures leaning into the wind, and counted the rising breaths before the bells rang out. The first bell. The second. And the third — three bells for the tide — told the city that Moru had drawn up into the light again.

She was seventeen, third daughter of a shell-smith, and the tide-watching was a job she had taken not for the glory but for the wages, which paid for her mother's medicine and, on quiet days, a cup of real honey from the orchards of the Shell-Meadows.

Below her, the city spread across the back of the turtle in concentric rings. The Inner Ring held the Council Hall, the great bell tower, and the Salt Market, where the pearl-divers sold their morning catch. The Middle Ring was the tangle of the smiths' quarter, where the shell-smiths hammered out new plating and where Kessa had grown up. And the Outer Ring, the rim where the tide-watchers stood, was where the salt ate the stone, and where the cliffs fell straight into the endless dark.

Some said the turtle was only a mile wide. Others said a thousand. The truth, like the rest of the world beyond the shell, was something no living person had ever seen. There was no need to know, they said. Vell had always been here. Moru had always been beneath. And the sea beyond had always been endless, black, and indifferent.

That was the lie Kessa wanted to prove. Not with a ship — no one sailed. Not with a sword either. But with a map. A true map, drawn from the only instrument in Vell that could see past the horizon: the old brass telescope in the Council's archive, which, on days when the sea was glass, showed the far edge of the shell and the water beyond.

She had the key to that archive. She had, in fact, stolen it three days ago from the pockets of Corvin, the archivist's apprentice, while he was drunk on market mead. The key was small, brass, and warm in her palm as she stood at the rim of the world and watched the sun climb over the edge of the shell.

The sun touched the seawall of the Outer Ring, and the water below turned from black to copper. In the copper light, the city looked like something half-asleep: the pearl-divers' ladders swinging, a cat climbing the side of the bell tower with no hurry, two merchants bickering over the price of a bundle of dried kelp. Normal. Alive. Every part of it breathing in time with the creature that carried them.

Then the tide turned. The great exhale. The whole of Vell settled back down into the sea with a sound like a cathedral creaking, and the seawall took a slow spray of black water up the face of the Outer Ring. Kessa's breath went out of her with it, and for a moment she was standing at the edge of something so big it was no longer a creature at all but a country, a continent, a god's shoulder.

It was then — in the middle of the Fall, with the water rising to her boots — that she heard the voice from the water. Not a shout. Not a wave. A word, shaped like a word, coming from somewhere below and beyond, from the dark side of the turtle's belly, from where no light reached and no map had ever been.

Kessa did not know what the word meant. But she knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a door you have never opened, the way the tide-watchers knew the shape of the Wait by the way the city held its breath.

It was not a single word. It was two. Kessa was still standing in the rising black water, listening, when the second came — lower, patient, the way the sea is patient with the rocks — and this time, impossibly, it was shaped like a name.

Not Moru's name. Moru's name belonged to the elders, a sound too old for a mouth. Kessa's own name, spoken from the deep water, soft as a hand on a windowpane.

The bells began to ring the Wait — the long, black, held note — and the city's lights began to flick on, one by one, from the Inner Ring out to the rim of the Outer Ring. Kessa turned her back on the dark water, put the brass key in her pocket, and began the long climb up the stairs of the shell, toward the archive, toward the telescope, toward the far edge of the world that no one had ever seen and something, at last, was calling to her by name.
